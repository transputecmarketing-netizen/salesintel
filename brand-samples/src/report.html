The Resilience Gap  ·  Foreword
Foreword
Foreword
Most IT leaders are not short of warnings. They are short of time, and of a clear view of what comes first.

This report is our attempt at that view. We went back to the public evidence (the government's annual survey, the National Cyber Security Centre, IBM's research on breach costs and the independent analysis of last year's biggest UK incidents) and set it side by side.

Two things stand out. The first is that the number of businesses being hit has stopped climbing. It has held at 43% for two years running. The second is harder to read: preparedness has barely moved. Three in four businesses still have no formal plan for the day something goes wrong.

We work with organisations that run on very little spare attention. What they ask of us is rarely another tool. They want a calmer operation, a cost they can predict, and a straight answer when something breaks.

We hope this report makes the case for that kind of resilience on the evidence rather than on fear. Read it for the figures. Use the playbook that accompanies it for the next ninety days.

The Transputec team

What the evidence says

Attack volume is flat, but the incidents that land are larger. The NCSC handled more than twice as many nationally significant incidents as the year before, and one manufacturing outage cost the UK economy an estimated £1.9 billion.3,4

Cost follows speed. UK organisations that used security automation widely found and contained breaches 42 days sooner and saved about £670,000 per breach.2

What it means

The gap is no longer awareness. It is practice: rehearsed recovery, a view of supplier risk, and rules for the AI tools staff already use. Each is cheaper to build before an incident than during one.

Our view: resilience is an operating discipline with a budget line, not a project with an end date.

A plan on paper

Having a document is the start. The organisations that recover quickly have run the plan: someone has been woken at 2am, the phone tree has been tried, and the backups have been restored somewhere other than the lab.

Suppliers are the blind spot

Supplier compromise was the most common cause of UK breaches in IBM's data, yet only 15% of businesses review supplier risk.1,2

AI arrived first, rules second

Staff are already using AI tools. Fewer than a third of UK organisations have rules for it, and breaches involving heavy unsanctioned use cost £498,000 more.2

Our view
The gap is cheap to close and expensive to discover. It is closed with rehearsal and ownership, not with another purchase.

Moving up

Most of the distance between A and C is rehearsal and ownership, which cost time rather than licences. The step from C to D is where a managed partner earns its fee: evidence, supplier reviews and a board pack someone else prepares.

Our view: do not skip a stage. An assured organisation that has never restored a backup is not assured.

Method notes

Figures are reproduced as published. We have rounded nothing except where a source itself rounds. The DSIT survey reports what organisations identified and chose to disclose, so true prevalence is likely higher. IBM's UK figures come from a subset of its global sample and are indicative. The JLR estimate depends on assumptions about recovery timing that the CMC states in its assessment.

Cross-source comparisons are avoided by design. We have not modelled or extrapolated any figure.
