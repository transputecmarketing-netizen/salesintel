The best data is a year old.
We say which year.

UK government measured AI adoption in spring 2025. Tools and habits have moved since. We use the numbers, date them, and avoid stretching them.

Source 01

DSIT AI Adoption Research

Government, published January 2026. 3,500 businesses with 5 or more staff. Fieldwork February to May 2025.

Source 02

Cyber Security Breaches Survey 2025/26

DSIT and Home Office, 30 April 2026. Fieldwork August to December 2025. New AI questions.

Source 03

IBM Cost of a Data Breach 2025, UK

Vendor-sponsored study of organisations that had breaches. UK sample size not published.

Source 04

ICO and the AI Cyber Code

The regulator's AI guidance (2023, under review) and the Government's voluntary code of practice.

Source 05

Microsoft Work Trend Index

2024 edition, global, vendor-run. Used once, labelled, for the unofficial-tools figure.

Do not compare

Government's two adoption figures use different size bands, bases and years. They are not a trend, and we never put them side by side as if they were.

Control is lagging.

What the data says

Government's survey found one in six businesses using AI, rising to nearly one in four mid-sized ones, mostly off-the-shelf text tools. Skills, not cost, is the main brake. Its own authors note that it cannot see unofficial use, and a later government survey finds only about a quarter of businesses that use, are adopting or are considering AI have security practices for its risk.1,2

What it means

The gap that matters is between the AI a business has chosen and the AI its people actually open. The first is a procurement question. The second is a data question, and it is probably the bigger one.

Our view: the cheapest first step is not a ban. It is a short approved list, a clear red line on data, and a named owner.

Dated data
The government adoption study was fielded in spring 2025, about 18 months ago. Use the direction, not the decimals.1

Government also found that of those who cited each barrier, 80% rated ethical concerns as significant, 76% cost and 72% unclear regulation. Those are ratings among people who raised the barrier, not shares of all firms.1

Our view

Skills include knowing what not to paste. A short, practical briefing on data does more for safety than a long policy few have read, and it is the cheapest part of an AI programme.

03 · Rules that already apply

Two sources of guidance.
Neither is new.

ICO · data protection

Where AI processes personal data, you need a lawful basis chosen before you start. The ICO says that in the vast majority of cases AI use is likely to be high risk and so triggers a data protection impact assessment. Where you decide it is not, document how you decided.5

Core guidance last updated March 2023 and under review because of the Data (Use and Access) Act. This does not mean every AI tool needs an assessment: it applies where personal data is processed.

Government · AI cyber code

A voluntary code of 13 principles for the cyber security of AI, covering organisations that deploy AI as well as those that build it. Six that matter most to a business buying tools:6

  • Raise awareness of AI security threats and risks
  • Evaluate the threats and manage the risks to your AI system
  • Enable human responsibility for AI systems
  • Identify, track and protect your assets
  • Secure your supply chain
  • Monitor your system's behaviour
Not law

The code is voluntary. We did not verify the status of any related international standard or of any UK AI bill, and we make no claim about either.

Sources: 5 ICO guidance on AI and data protection (accountability and lawfulness). 6 Code of Practice for the Cyber Security of AI, GOV.UK (January 2025).

04 · A one-page AI policy
04 · A one-page AI policy

Five lines.
One page.

A starting point, not a legal document. It maps to play 04 of The Resilience Playbook.

An approved list
Name the AI tools staff may use for work, and how to request another.
A red line
Say, in one sentence, what must never be pasted in: customer data, contracts, credentials, source code.
An owner
One person with authority to approve, review and say no.
A check on personal data
Where a tool touches personal data, decide the lawful basis and whether an impact assessment is needed.
A briefing
Ten minutes, once a quarter, with real examples. Not an annual course.
Illustrative

Our suggestion, built from the sources in this report. Take legal advice before relying on it for personal data or contracts.
