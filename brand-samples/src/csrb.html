Executive summary
Executive summary

Managed IT providers are
about to join the rulebook.

What the Bill does

It amends the Network and Information Systems Regulations 2018 to cover managed service providers: firms that remotely manage a customer's IT. They must manage security risks, register with the regulator, report significant incidents on a 24 and 72 hour clock, and tell affected customers.1,2

What it means for buyers

Customers should hear about a serious incident at their provider sooner and more reliably. But the duty to tell customers has no fixed deadline, the thresholds are still to be set, and the Bill gives customers no right to compensation.1

Our view: a provider that already works to ISO 27001 will find the duties familiar. The 24-hour clock is the real change.

Not yet law
Lords Third Reading is listed for 2 November 2026. The Commons then considers the Lords' amendments. No Royal Assent date or commencement date has been published.

02 · Who is in scope
02 · Who is in scope

If you remotely manage
someone else's IT.

The Bill defines a relevant managed service provider by what it does and how big it is.

In scope
  • Remote support and help desk
  • Managed security: SOC, SIEM, firewall management
  • Managed cloud
  • Ongoing monitoring and administration of customer systems

Examples from the Government factsheet.3

Out of scope
  • Software sold as a product
  • Install-only projects
  • Pure operational-technology vendors
  • Consultancies without ongoing access
  • Micro and small enterprises

Small and micro providers can still be designated as critical suppliers.1

About 6 in 100 UK MSPs

of an estimated 12,867 UK managed service providers fall in scope, central estimate 788. The assessment assumes in-scope firms employ at least 50 people in the UK and exceed €10 million turnover. The Bill itself sets no headcount or turnover figure and refers to the EU Commission's definition of small enterprises.2

Also covered

Data centres, at 1 MW (third-party) or 10 MW (enterprise), regulated by Ofcom. And any supplier of any size that the regulator designates as critical because an incident could significantly disrupt the economy or daily life.1

Sources: 1 HL Bill 62. 2 Impact assessment; Frontier Economics research (March 2025). 3 GOV.UK factsheet on relevant managed service providers.

Resilience Bill Report  ·  03 · The 24-hour clock
03 · The 24-hour clock
03 · The 24-hour clock

Two deadlines for the regulator.
None for you.

Today the NIS rules allow a single 72-hour notification. The Bill replaces it with a faster, two-step model for providers.

Hour 0

The provider becomes aware

Of a significant incident affecting the systems it relies on to deliver the managed service.

Within 24 hours

Initial notification

To the Information Commission, the regulator formerly known as the ICO: name, service and brief details. A copy goes to the NCSC at the same time.

Within 72 hours

Full notification

Timing and duration, nature, impact including cross-border, and any upstream incident.

Then, as soon as reasonably practicable

Customers are told

Those likely to be adversely affected, with the nature of the incident and why they are affected. No fixed deadline, and updates only 'as the provider considers necessary'.

Also required

Register with the regulator within three months of the rules starting to apply, nominate a UK representative if based overseas, and report changes within seven days.1 What counts as a significant incident will be set later in secondary legislation.3

Sources: 1 HL Bill 62, new regulations 14C to 14G. 3 GOV.UK incident reporting factsheet.

What it costs to get
it wrong.

In each band the maximum is the greater of the two figures. Penalties consider impact, remedial steps and previous compliance.

Higher maximum
£17m or 4% of turnover
  • Failing the security risk-management duty
  • Failing to notify an incident
  • Missing the 24 or 72 hour deadlines
  • Failing to notify affected customers
Standard maximum
£10m or 2% of turnover
  • Registration failures
  • Failing to nominate a UK representative
  • Failing to copy the NCSC
  • Failing to give customers updates

The existing NIS maximum penalty bands today.3

Turnover

The Bill counts turnover inside and outside the UK. The precise definition is left to secondary legislation.1,3

Resilience Bill Report  ·  04 · What it means for you
04 · What it means for you
04 · What it means for you

Ask now, before it is
a contract dispute.

What changes
  • Your provider must manage risks to the systems it uses to serve you, proportionately and with regard to the state of the art.
  • It must tell the regulator within 24 hours of a significant incident.
  • It must tell you if you are likely to be adversely affected.
What does not
  • You gain no statutory right to compensation.
  • Nothing in the Bill obliges you to act.
  • Your contract and your SLA still decide what you can recover.
Four questions for your provider
“Are you a relevant managed service provider under the Bill, and will you register?”
“How quickly would you tell us of an incident at your end, in writing?”
“Who would you notify besides us: the regulator, the NCSC?”
“Which of our systems can your staff reach, and how is that access protected?”

Procurement checks are our inference from the Bill's duties, not a legal effect of the Bill.

Sources: HL Bill 62, new regulations 14B and 14G; Government factsheet on relevant managed service providers.

Our view

A statutory duty on providers is welcome, but it is a floor, not an assurance. The Bill tells you what your provider must do after something goes wrong. Checking what it does before is still your job, and a certification such as ISO 27001 or Cyber Essentials Plus is a sensible place to start.

06 · What we do not know yet
06 · What we do not know yet

Six open questions.

Royal Assent

No date is published. The Commons must still consider the Lords' amendments after 2 November.1

Commencement

The managed-service-provider provisions start on a day the Secretary of State appoints by regulations. None is stated.1

Significant incident

The thresholds that trigger reporting will be set in secondary legislation.1

Turnover

The precise definition for penalty purposes is left to secondary legislation.1

Small and micro providers

Ministers proposed a power to bring individual high-risk small providers into scope. It is a statement, not law.1

The text itself

The Lords has added substantial new material, including national-security directions and a provision on loss of control of an AI agent. The Bill can still change.1

Our view
Do not wait for the dates. A provider that can already show a tested incident process will have no scramble when they arrive.
Source: 1 HL Bill 62 as amended on report, section 69 (commencement); Hansard, Lords, 26 October 2026; GOV.UK summary of the Bill.
