Foreword

Seeing and hearing is no longer believing.

In early November 2026, federal prosecutors in New York announced charges against three men accused of using AI to mimic the voices, and sometimes the faces, of chief executives and lawyers. Prosecutors allege the scheme took more than $129 million from companies including hospital systems, banks and a UK fintech firm. The charges are allegations and have not been proved.

In the same week, courts in England and India were dealing with something quieter but related: legal documents that cited cases that do not exist. One was filed by a prosecutor, another by a government authority. Neither involved criminals. Both involved people trusting material that looked right and was not.

The two problems share a root. Generative AI makes convincing content cheap, whether that content is a cloned voice or a plausible paragraph of law. The answer in both cases is the same discipline: decide in advance where you will check, and make the check part of the process rather than a matter of instinct. This playbook shows how.

Introduction

Three habits stop most of the damage.

None of these needs new technology. They need clear rules, practised in advance, that hold even when the person on the screen looks and sounds exactly like your chief executive.

Verify money on a second channel

Any new payment, urgent transfer or change of bank details is confirmed by calling back on a number you already hold, and approved by two people.

Treat voice and video as claims, not proof

A call can be faked. Use code words, call-backs and a pause before acting on any unusual request from a senior person.

Check AI work before it leaves

Every citation, figure, quote and name in AI-assisted work is checked against a source by a named person before it goes to a client, court or regulator.

The news

Fake bosses, fake lawyers, fake law

In both stories, nobody broke a system. People trusted content that looked right.

On 5 November 2026, the US Attorney's Office for the Southern District of New York announced an indictment against three men. Prosecutors allege that between July 2024 and February 2026 they used AI to impersonate the voices and images of executives and lawyers at major firms, and took more than $129 million from companies worldwide.1 Two were arrested in Spain and one in the UK; extradition requests are pending.2 All are presumed innocent.

The alleged method was simple. An email, supposedly from the chief executive, asked someone in accounts to help with a confidential transaction. A phone or video call followed in which AI mimicked the executive's voice and sometimes face. The employee was then passed to a supposed lawyer, also fake, who gave the payment instructions.2 Named victims include hospital systems, banks and a UK fintech firm.1

This is not new in shape. In 2024 the engineering firm Arup confirmed that an employee in Hong Kong made 15 transfers totalling HK$200 million, about $25 million, after a video call with what appeared to be senior colleagues. Arup said fake voices and images were used and none of its internal systems were compromised.3

Fabricated content is reaching courts too. In early November a High Court judgment in England recorded that a prosecutor had cited a Court of Appeal case that does not exist; the court found the explanation unsatisfactory but said it did not change the outcome.4 In 2025 the High Court warned that freely available AI tools are not capable of reliable legal research.5

A public database of court decisions involving AI-fabricated material listed 2,266 cases worldwide by 5 November 2026, about 90 of them in the UK.6 The Solicitors Regulation Authority issued a formal warning notice in August 2026 after 42 reports of potential AI misuse.7

The thread joining these stories is trust in what looks and sounds right. The fix is to stop relying on that feeling and build checks into the process instead.

The control

The payment and bank details check

Use this every time a payment is new, urgent, unusual or follows a change of bank details. No exceptions for seniority.

Pause

Any request that is urgent, confidential or outside normal process triggers the check, whoever appears to be asking.

Call back on a known number

Ring the requester or supplier on a number already held in your records, never one given in the request.

Second approver

A second person, who was not on the original call or email thread, approves the payment in the banking system.

Confirm the payee

Use your bank's Confirmation of Payee result and treat a mismatch as a stop, not a warning.

Record it

Log who verified, how and when. The record protects staff who said no and helps if money must be recovered.
The NCSC advises verifying important email requests using a second type of communication.8 The same logic applies to calls and video.

Voice and video

Five rules for verifying a call

Assume a voice or face can be copied. These rules work regardless of how good the fake is.
01

Agree code words for executives

Senior leaders and their finance and executive assistants share a phrase that is never written in email or chat. The FBI recommends a secret word for the same reason.9
02

Hang up and call back

For any unusual request, end the call and ring a number you already hold. The FBI gives the same advice for suspected voice cloning.9
03

Ask something only they would know

A recent internal meeting, not a fact found online. Public profiles feed the fakes.
04

Never move money on one channel alone

A request made on a call is confirmed in writing through a known system, and the other way round.
05

Make it safe to say no

Leaders state publicly that they will never be offended by a call-back, and that staff who pause will be backed.
Remember
If the request is real, a two-minute check costs nothing. If it is fake, it saves everything.

In practice

Three scenarios to rehearse

Finance team

The chief executive on video

The scenario
A video call from the chief executive asks for an urgent, confidential transfer to complete an acquisition.
The right move
Pause, end the call, ring the executive's known mobile and ask for the code word.
What good looks like
The payment waits for a second approver, whatever the deadline.
Pitfall
Accepting a callback number or meeting link supplied in the request itself.
Accounts payable

The supplier's new bank account

The scenario
A trusted supplier emails new bank details a day before a large invoice is due.
The right move
Call the supplier on the number held before the change and confirm the details verbally.
What good looks like
The change is held until verified, and the check is logged.
Pitfall
Replying to the email to confirm. The attacker may control that thread.
Executive assistants

The lawyer who needs it today

The scenario
A caller says they are a partner at a well-known firm acting on a confidential deal.
The right move
Ring the firm's public switchboard and ask for the person by name before sharing anything.
What good looks like
No information or payment moves until the firm confirms the instruction.
Pitfall
Being reassured by a convincing name, accent or email signature.

Training

Training that changes behaviour

Annual click-through modules do not prepare people for a convincing call from their boss.

Use realistic scenarios

Rehearse the three scenarios in this playbook with the people who handle money, using your own executives' names.

Practise the pause

Teach one reflex: urgency plus secrecy plus money means stop and verify. Make the pause a habit, not a judgement call.

Include leaders

Executives should know they are the ones being copied, limit what they post, and model the call-back themselves.

Repeat and refresh

Short sessions every quarter, with real examples from the news and from your own near misses.
Reward the person who stopped a payment that turned out to be real. That is how the habit survives.

Fabricated content

Why confident errors slip past good people

AI output is a first draft from a fast, fluent colleague who sometimes invents things.

AI assistants write fluent, well-structured text. That fluency is the problem. A made-up case name in the right format, with a plausible year and court, reads exactly like a real one. A figure with one decimal place looks more precise than a rounded guess, even when it was invented.

Courts have seen this repeatedly. In 2025 the High Court in London dealt with two cases in which lawyers had put fake authorities before the court, one involving 45 fake citations or quotations, and said freely available tools are not capable of reliable legal research.5 In August 2026 the Solicitors Regulation Authority warned that individuals remain responsible for the work they produce, whether or not AI is used.7

The judiciary applies the same rule to itself. Guidance for judges in England and Wales says they are personally responsible for material produced in their name and warns that AI tools can invent cases and quotations.10

The risk is not limited to lawyers. Consultants, accountants, surveyors, HR teams and anyone producing reports for clients or regulators face the same failure: a draft that looks finished, a deadline, and no clear rule about who checks what.

Most errors get through for organisational reasons, not technical ones. Nobody is named as the checker. Juniors assume seniors will review; seniors assume juniors checked. Approved tools are not distinguished from public chatbots. And when an error is found, a second AI prompt is used to explain it, which can make it worse.

The fix is a routine that treats every factual element in AI-assisted work as unverified until a named person has traced it to a source.

A checking routine

Four things to check before anything goes out

Apply this to any AI-assisted document going to a client, court, regulator, board or the public.

Citations and references

Open every case, statute, standard or paper in an authoritative source. If you cannot find it, it does not go out.

Figures and calculations

Trace each number to your own systems or a published source. Recalculate anything that drives a decision.

Quotes and attributions

Check that quoted words appear in the source and that the person or body actually said them.

Names, dates and facts

Confirm people, organisations, dates and events. AI often blends similar names or shifts dates.
Record who checked and how. A short sign-off line on the file is enough.

Policy

Five rules for AI in professional advice

A short policy, applied consistently, does more than a long one nobody reads.
01
Use approved tools only
Business-grade tools with suitable data protections. No client data in public chatbots.
02
A named person owns every output
The author signs off the content as if they wrote every word, because professionally they did.
03
Verify before you rely
No citation, figure or quotation leaves the building until it has been traced to a source.
04
Supervisors check the checking
Reviewers ask how facts were verified, not just whether the draft reads well.
05
Be open about use
Tell clients and courts when AI was used where rules or contracts require it, and keep a record.

When it goes wrong

Incident steps for fakes and fabrications

Speed matters for payments; candour for fabricated content.
01

Payment sent to a fraudster

Call your bank at once to stop or recall the funds, then report to Action Fraud or Police Scotland.
02

Suspected deepfake call or message

Do not act. Keep the call log, messages and any recording; alert security and finance.
03

Fabricated content found after release

Tell the recipient promptly, correct the record and identify every other document from the same source.
04

Court or regulator involved

Inform the court or regulator quickly and honestly. Check whether you have a duty to self-report.
05

Learn and fix

Review what failed: the control, the training or the tool. Update the process and share lessons.
Remember
Do not use an AI tool to explain an AI error. Go back to the source.

Glossary

Words worth knowing

Deepfake
Audio, image or video generated or altered by AI to make someone appear to say or do something they did not.
Voice cloning
Using AI to copy a person's voice from recordings so new speech sounds like them.
Hallucination
A confident AI output that is wrong or made up, such as a case that does not exist.
CEO fraud
A scam in which criminals pose as a senior executive to get staff to make payments or share data.
Mandate fraud
Tricking an organisation into changing a supplier's bank details to an account the criminal controls.
Call-back
Verifying a request by contacting the person on a number already held, not one supplied in the request.
Code word
A pre-agreed phrase used to confirm identity on calls, never shared in writing.
Confirmation of Payee
A UK banking check that compares the account name you enter with the name on the account.
Two-person approval
A control requiring a second, independent person to approve a payment.
Authorised push payment fraud
When a victim is tricked into authorising a payment to a fraudster.
