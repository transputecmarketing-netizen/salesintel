Decision or delivery · Week 1 · AWS04 / 64
Introduction

Two purchases that sound the same.

Most AWS conversations start with a vague request for help. The first job is to decide which kind of help you are buying, because the contract, the team and the measure of success are all different.

Consultancy buys a decision

Advice, design and a defined piece of work that ends with a clear recommendation or a finished change.

A managed service buys delivery

A team that runs the estate every day: monitoring, patching, backups, support and cost reporting against agreed service levels.

Mixing them up is expensive

Buy advice when you needed operations and nothing gets run. Buy operations when you needed a decision and you pay to run the wrong thing well.

Common mistakes

Five ways the purchase goes wrong

Each of these is common, and each is avoidable with one clear conversation before the contract is signed.
01

Expecting a consultancy to run things

The report lands, the team leaves and nobody is responsible for the changes it recommended.
02

Expecting a managed service to set strategy

The estate runs smoothly in the wrong direction because nobody owns the design decisions.
03

Scoping by hours, not outcomes

Time gets used, but the decision or the service level you needed is never defined.
04

No handover or exit plan

Knowledge leaves with the supplier, or switching later becomes painful and slow.
05

Nobody owns the cost

Advice and operations both assume the other side is watching the bill.
Remember
Many organisations need both, in sequence: a consultancy to decide what good looks like, then a managed service to keep it that way.

Introduction

A climbing bill is rarely one big mistake.

It is usually many small ones, running quietly where nobody is looking. The good news: they tend to hide in the same five places, and each one can be found with tools already in your AWS account.

Visibility comes first

Before anything can be cut, someone has to see it, name it and know who owns it.

Every cost needs an owner

Spend without an owner is spend nobody will question. Ownership turns a number into a decision.

Finding it once is not enough

Waste returns. The habit of looking every month is what keeps the bill honest.

01

Idle and oversized compute

Instances sized for a peak that never comes, or left running overnight and at weekends.
What it looks like
Low utilisation week after week. Development servers running around the clock. Instance types chosen years ago and never revisited.
Why it happens
Teams size for safety, and nobody is asked to size down once the system settles.
Where to look
AWS Compute Optimizer recommendations, and CPU and memory utilisation in CloudWatch.
What to do first
Schedule non-production to stop out of hours, then rightsize the clearest cases.
Owner
Engineering lead for each workload
Bring this to the monthly review, with a date for each action.

02

Storage nobody deletes

Volumes, snapshots and buckets that outlive the work they were created for.
What it looks like
Unattached EBS volumes. Snapshots from servers long gone. Buckets with everything in the most expensive tier.
Why it happens
Deleting feels risky, keeping feels free, and nobody owns the clean-up.
Where to look
EBS volumes in the available state, the snapshot list sorted by age, and S3 Storage Lens.
What to do first
Agree a retention rule, delete what is clearly orphaned and add lifecycle policies to buckets.
Owner
Platform or infrastructure team
Bring this to the monthly review, with a date for each action.

Run it yourself

A one-hour cost hunt

Block an hour with one engineer and one person from finance. Work through the five suspects in this order and leave with named owners.
01

Open Cost Explorer and group by service

Note the top services and anything that grew since last month.
02

Check compute utilisation

List instances with consistently low use, and anything non-production running all week.
03

List orphaned storage

Unattached volumes, old snapshots and buckets without lifecycle rules.
04

Look at data transfer lines

Find the biggest flows and ask whether they were designed or accidental.
05

Review untagged resources and commitments

Find what has no owner, then check commitment coverage and utilisation.
06

Assign an owner to every finding

One name per item, with a date to come back to it.
Remember
The goal of the first hour is not savings. It is a list of findings with an owner against each one.

Introduction

Spending with intent, not just spending less.

FinOps is how engineering, finance and the business work together so that every pound of cloud spend is seen, owned and deliberate. It is less about a product you install and more about how your teams behave.

It starts with seeing

Clear, shared data on what is being spent, where and why.

It works through ownership

Teams who build and run services also own what those services cost.

It lasts through habit

A regular rhythm of review keeps decisions fresh as the estate changes.

Who does what

Shared work, clear roles

FinOps works when each group knows its part and meets the others regularly.

Engineering

Designs and runs services with cost in mind. Owns rightsizing, scheduling and clean-up.

Finance

Owns budgets, forecasts and commitments. Turns cost data into planning.

Business leaders

Set priorities and decide what is worth spending on.

FinOps lead

Runs the rhythm: data, reviews, actions and follow-up.
If you only do one thing, name the FinOps lead. Everything else follows from someone owning the rhythm.

FinOps, explained · Week 3 · AWS · FinOps37 / 64
Getting started

Your first month of FinOps

A simple sequence that gets the cycle turning without a big programme.

Week one: see it

Turn on cost allocation tags, agree a tagging standard and build one shared view of spend.

Week two: own it

Name an owner for each account or product, and share their spend with them.

Week three: act on it

Take the clearest optimisation actions: schedule non-production, clear orphaned storage.

Week four: review it

Hold the first monthly review. Agree actions, owners and the next date.
Next month, repeat. That is the discipline.

The real cost of a device fleet · Week 4 · IT Procurement41 / 64
Introduction

The invoice is only day one.

A device fleet is judged at purchase on price. It is paid for over its whole life: every set-up, support call, security update, repair and, finally, a safe and certified retirement.

Price is visible

The purchase cost is on one invoice, so it gets the scrutiny.

Running cost is scattered

Support, repairs and lost hours are spread across teams and budgets, so they rarely get added up.

Risk is the hidden cost

An unpatched, unencrypted or badly retired device can cost more than the fleet itself.
