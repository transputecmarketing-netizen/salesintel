Executive summary

The IT bill was marginal. The trading bill was up to €250 million.

On Sunday 11 October 2026, Ahold Delhaize, owner of Albert Heijn, Delhaize, Food Lion and Stop & Shop, began taking systems offline after a cybersecurity incident.1,2 Online ordering stopped in the Netherlands and the United States, several Dutch distribution centres halted and some Albert Heijn shelves emptied.3

On 4 November the company said the incident is expected to cut fourth-quarter net sales by €200 million to €250 million and operating income by €75 million to €100 million. In the same statement it said incremental response and remediation costs would have only a marginal impact on its 2026 results.4

That contrast is the story, and it is not new. Marks and Spencer first expected a £300 million hit to operating profit, against direct costs it later put at about £136 million.5,6 Co-op estimated £285 million of lost revenue.7 Jaguar Land Rover booked £196 million of cyber costs, while an independent estimate put the cost to the UK economy at £1.9 billion.8,9

Most cyber risk models still start with the IT bill: forensics, rebuilding, legal fees, perhaps a fine. Those costs are real, but in each case above they were the smaller part. The larger part is trade that never happens while systems are down, and the margin that goes with it.

The bottom line
Price cyber risk in lost trading days, not IT invoices. A board that knows what a week without its core systems would cost, and has rehearsed working through it, will spend its security budget better.

Key findings

Seven things the evidence shows

01
The headline figure is a range, and an estimate.
Ahold Delhaize gave €200 million to €250 million of lost fourth-quarter sales "based on current estimations". Up to €250 million is the top of that range.4
02
Lost trade dwarfed the response bill.
The company expects €75 million to €100 million less operating income, while response and remediation costs are described as marginal.4
03
Containment caused much of the damage.
The company took systems offline to protect its environment, which stopped online orders and, in the Benelux, parts of the supply chain.4,10
04
Two weeks was enough to move a full-year forecast.
Most disruption was contained within two weeks, yet guidance for underlying earnings per share growth fell from mid-to-high single digits to low single digits.4
05
UK retail saw the same shape in 2025.
M&S lost 46 days of online orders, and Co-op estimated £285 million of lost revenue after restricting its own systems.11,7
06
The wider economy pays too.
The Cyber Monitoring Centre estimated the JLR incident cost the UK £1.9 billion and affected over 5,000 organisations, mostly through lost manufacturing output.9,12
07
Most UK boards are not set up to see this.
Only 31% of UK businesses have board-level responsibility for cyber security, and 25% have a formal incident response plan.13

What happened

Two weeks offline, one quarter dented

The bills most people picture when they think of a cyber attack were the marginal part.

Ahold Delhaize is one of the largest food retailers in the world, with more than 9,000 supermarkets in nine countries.14 Its brands include Albert Heijn in the Netherlands, Delhaize in Belgium, and Food Lion, Stop & Shop, Giant Food and Hannaford in the United States. It has been here before: a November 2024 incident disrupted some US brands, and the company later confirmed files had likely been taken.15

The trouble began on the afternoon of Sunday 11 October.1 The company described a cybersecurity incident, brought in external experts and took certain systems offline.3 Within a day Dutch and US websites and apps were down.

The effects reached the shop floor. Several Dutch distribution centres stopped, fresh products ran short, and for nearly two weeks electronic shelf labels could not always show correct prices.3,16 Stores stayed open. Distribution centres were running again by 15 October, but home deliveries did not restart until 23 October.2

On the nature of the attack the company has said little. It called it a malicious action by third parties and filed a police report on 18 October.10 It has not said whether ransomware was involved, and no group has been confirmed as responsible. In mid-October it had found no evidence of data theft.3 We do not speculate beyond that.

The numbers came on 4 November: €200 million to €250 million less fourth-quarter net sales, €75 million to €100 million less operating income, and a lower full-year outlook.4 The chief executive said most of the disruption was contained within two weeks, but that work to fully restore normal operations continues.

One line in the release deserves more attention than it got. Incremental response and remediation costs, the bills most people picture when they think of a cyber attack, are expected to have a marginal impact on 2026 operating income.4 The money was lost at the till and in the supply chain.

How it works

How downtime becomes lost sales

A ransom or a rebuild is not where most of the money goes. This is the chain that turns days offline into a hole in the quarter.
01
Containment switches things off
Taking systems offline limits the attacker but also stops ordering, picking and pricing. It is usually the right call, and it has a price.
02
Dependent processes stall
Distribution, replenishment and online fulfilment run on shared systems. When they stop, stock stops moving and shelves thin.
03
Customers go elsewhere
Cancelled deliveries and gaps on the shelf send shoppers to competitors. Some of that trade does not come back.
04
Fixed costs keep running
Rent, wages and energy continue while sales fall, so much of the lost revenue drops straight through to profit.

Analysis

Where the money really goes

Each euro of lost sales cost Ahold Delhaize roughly 37 to 40 cents of operating income.

Put the four incidents side by side and a pattern appears. The costs that sit naturally with IT, such as forensics, rebuilding systems and specialist advice, are significant but bounded. The costs that sit with the business, lost sales, wasted stock, lost margin and the knock-on to partners, are larger and harder to cap.

M&S is the clearest example because it reported both. In May 2025 it expected the attack to reduce operating profit by about £300 million before mitigation.5 Six months later it put total direct costs at about £136 million, of which £83 million was systems response and recovery, and claimed the £100 million maximum on its cyber insurance. UK online sales fell 42.9% in the half.6

Co-op shows the cost of containment. Its food business was hit hardest, with availability reduced as systems were proactively taken offline.17 By March 2026 it put the direct revenue impact at £285 million and the impact on profitability at £107 million, figures it described as unaudited.7

JLR shows how far the damage travels. The company booked £196 million of cyber costs and arranged a £500 million supplier financing scheme and a £1.5 billion loan guaranteed by UK Export Finance.8 The Cyber Monitoring Centre put the UK impact at £1.9 billion, mostly lost output at JLR and its suppliers.9,12

Ahold Delhaize fits the same shape. On our calculation from its own ranges, each euro of lost sales cost roughly 37 to 40 cents of operating income.4 That is roughly nine to ten times the group's operating margin of around 4%, because rent, wages and energy carry on when the tills go quiet.

The lesson is not that IT costs are trivial. It is that a risk model built on IT costs alone will understate the exposure by a wide margin, and will steer investment towards tools rather than towards the ability to keep trading.

Cost lines

The four lines of cost

Every major incident produces costs in four places. Most risk registers count only the third.

Lost trading

Sales that do not happen while ordering, picking or production is down. Usually the largest line, and the least modelled.

Supply chain and waste

Stock that cannot move, fresh food written off, and partners and suppliers who cannot operate.

Response and recovery

Forensics, rebuild, specialist advisers and overtime. Real money, but usually the smaller part.

Customers, legal and regulatory

Refunds and compensation for cancelled orders, legal advice, and regulatory exposure if personal data is involved.
Ask finance to estimate each line for a two-week outage. The first line is usually the biggest and the least understood.

The UK picture

Asked to plan, still not pricing

What is not measured tends not to be funded.

The UK has had its own warnings. The NCSC handled 204 nationally significant incidents in the year to August 2025, up from 89 the year before, and 18 were highly significant.18 Its chief executive said cyber security is now a matter of business survival.

In October 2025 ministers, the NCSC and the National Crime Agency wrote to the chief executives and chairs of leading UK companies, including the FTSE 350. They asked boards to use the Cyber Governance Code of Practice, join the NCSC's Early Warning service and require Cyber Essentials in their supply chains, and to plan and exercise how they would continue operations and rebuild after a destructive incident.19

That last request is the one the Ahold Delhaize figures speak to. Continuing operations is a business problem, not an IT one. It depends on whether stores, warehouses and service teams can keep working in some reduced form while core systems are rebuilt.

The evidence suggests many organisations are not there yet. The government's 2025/26 survey found board-level responsibility for cyber security in 31% of businesses and a formal incident response plan in 25%. Some 47% had cyber insurance of some kind, but only 10% held a specific cyber policy, and 15% reviewed risks from their immediate suppliers.13

Self-reported costs in the same survey are low, with a median of zero for the most disruptive breach.13 That is not proof that incidents are cheap. It suggests most organisations do not measure the cost of disruption, and what is not measured tends not to be funded.

The Cyber Governance Code, published in April 2025, gives boards a structure built on five principles: risk management, strategy, people, incident planning, response and recovery, and assurance and oversight.20 What most boardrooms still lack is a number: what a week without core systems would cost.

Recommendations

Six things boards should do now

Each one turns cyber risk from a technical worry into a business number the board can act on.
01
Put a price on a day of downtime
Work with finance to estimate lost sales and margin per day for each critical service, and use it in every cyber investment decision.
02
Run a business impact analysis, not just an IT one
Map which processes stop when which systems stop, and how long each can survive on manual workarounds.
03
Exercise with finance and operations in the room
Rehearse a two-week outage. Record what keeps trading, what stops and what it costs.
04
Read the insurance policy against the scenario
Compare your modelled loss with the limits, waiting periods and sub-limits. Know the gap you are carrying.
05
Rank resilience spending by value
Prioritise what shortens an outage or keeps trading going, measured in days avoided times cost per day.
06
Report cyber risk in money
Replace traffic lights with ranges: the loss from a plausible severe scenario, and what reduces it.

Glossary

Words worth knowing

Business impact analysis
A structured look at which processes matter most, what they depend on and what an outage of each would cost.
Business interruption cover
Insurance that pays for lost profit and extra costs while operations are disrupted.
Containment
Action to stop an attack spreading, often by taking systems or connections offline.
Cost of downtime
Lost sales, lost margin and extra costs for each day a service is unavailable.
Cyber Governance Code of Practice
UK government guidance, published in April 2025, setting out what boards should do about cyber risk.
Early Warning
A free NCSC service that alerts organisations to potential attacks on their networks.
Operating income
Profit from trading before interest and tax. Ahold Delhaize reports underlying and IFRS measures.
Ransomware
Malicious software that encrypts or steals data so criminals can demand payment.
Recovery time objective
The maximum time a service can be down before the damage becomes unacceptable.
Waiting period
The time an insurance policy requires before business interruption cover starts to pay.
