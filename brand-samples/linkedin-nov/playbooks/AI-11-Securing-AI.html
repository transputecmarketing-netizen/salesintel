Foreword

AI is a new way in. Treat it like one.

Every time a new technology joins the business, it brings new doors with it. Email brought phishing. The cloud brought misconfigured storage. AI assistants and agents bring something different again: software that reads untrusted text, makes decisions about it and, increasingly, takes action on your behalf.

Most of the security basics still apply. Identity, least privilege, logging, patching and training matter as much as ever. But some risks are genuinely new, such as prompt injection, and some familiar ones move faster, such as convincing phishing and fraudulent calls that sound exactly like your finance director.

This playbook is written for leaders who want to adopt AI with their eyes open. It explains the risks in plain English, sets out the controls that make the biggest difference, and gives you the questions to ask your security team and your suppliers.

Introduction

Secure AI is mostly good security, applied early.

You do not need a separate security programme for AI. You need your existing one to understand where AI changes the picture, and to be involved before tools are switched on rather than after.

Know what is in use

You cannot protect AI tools you do not know about. Start with an honest inventory of approved and unapproved use.

Limit what AI can reach and do

Most serious incidents come from assistants with too much access. Give them the least they need, and nothing more.

Watch, test and rehearse

Log AI activity, test it the way an attacker would, and know who does what when something goes wrong.

Securing AI · AI Playbook Series06 / 24
The new attack surface

Why AI changes the security conversation

Most AI risk is ordinary security risk with a new front door. Close the door before you furnish the room.

Traditional software does what its code says. An AI assistant does what its instructions say, and its instructions are written in ordinary language that can arrive from almost anywhere: the user, a document, a web page, an email or a tool it has been connected to. That is what makes assistants so useful, and it is also what makes them a new kind of target.

The first and most common risk is not exotic at all. It is people pasting customer lists, contracts, source code or board papers into whichever AI tool is nearest to hand, often a personal account with consumer terms. Data leakage through unapproved tools is the everyday AI risk, and it is driven by good intentions rather than malice.

The second shift is that AI now acts. Assistants connected to email, files, calendars, ticketing systems and code repositories can read and change things. Agents can take a series of steps on their own. Each connection extends the reach of anyone who can influence what the assistant does, which is why access and permissions matter more than ever.

The third shift is on the other side. Attackers use AI too. Phishing emails arrive in flawless English and reference real projects. Cloned voices and video can impersonate senior people convincingly. Vulnerabilities can be found and exploited more quickly. Defences built on spotting bad grammar or a slightly wrong voice are no longer enough.

None of this means AI is too risky to use. It means AI needs to be brought inside your security programme: inventoried, governed, permissioned, logged and tested, just like any other system that handles sensitive data and can take action.

The good news is that the controls that work are mostly familiar. Approved tools, strong identity, least privilege, human approval for consequential actions, good logging and regular testing will deal with the majority of the risk. The rest of this playbook shows how to apply them.

Five risks

The risks that matter most

Four you can control directly, plus a fifth that comes from outside.

Data leakage

Sensitive information pasted into unapproved tools, or surfaced by an assistant to someone who should not see it.

Prompt injection

Hidden or crafted instructions, in a message or a document, that steer the assistant away from what the user intended.

Insecure output handling

AI output passed straight into code, emails, databases or web pages without checks, so a bad answer becomes a bad action.

Excessive agency

Assistants and agents with broader access or autonomy than their job needs, which turns a small mistake into a large one.
The fifth risk is the supply chain: models, data, plugins and connectors from third parties that you are trusting without checking.

01

Prompt injection, up close

The risk most specific to AI, and one that no supplier has fully solved. Plan on the assumption that it will sometimes succeed.
Direct injection
A user types instructions designed to override the assistant's rules, for example to reveal its configuration or ignore its guardrails.
Indirect injection
Instructions hidden in content the assistant reads, such as an email, web page or shared file, written by someone outside your organisation.
Why it matters
If the assistant can send email, move files or call systems, a successful injection can leak data or take actions in the user's name.
What reduces it
Limit permissions, require human approval for sensitive actions, separate trusted and untrusted content, and log what the assistant does.
Owner
Security lead, with each AI product owner
Bring this to the monthly review, with a date for each action.

Principles

Five principles for securing AI

Simple rules your security team, suppliers and AI product owners can all work to.
01
Approve the tools, and make them easy to reach
If the approved route is slower than a personal account, people will use the personal account. Remove the friction.
02
Grant the least access that does the job
Assistants inherit permissions. Scope connectors, service accounts and agent tools to the task, and review them regularly.
03
Treat everything AI reads as untrusted
Emails, web pages and shared files may carry instructions. Design as if some of them are hostile.
04
Keep a person on consequential actions
Payments, deletions, external emails and changes to systems should need a human click, not just a model's decision.
05
Log it, test it and own it
Every AI system has a named owner, activity that can be reviewed, and a test before and after go-live.

When attackers use AI

Three attacks to prepare for

Finance

The cloned voice of a director

The attack
A call or video message, apparently from a senior leader, urges an urgent payment.
Why it works
Voice and video cloning can be convincing, and urgency discourages checks.
The control
Call back on a known number, and dual approval for payments and bank changes.
What good looks like
Staff are thanked, not criticised, for pausing to verify a senior request.
Everyone

Phishing that reads perfectly

The attack
Well-written emails that reference real suppliers, projects or colleagues.
Why it works
Spelling mistakes and awkward phrasing are no longer reliable warning signs.
The control
Phishing-resistant multi-factor authentication, email filtering and easy reporting.
What good looks like
Training focuses on unusual requests and process, not on spotting typos.
IT

Faster discovery of weak spots

The attack
Attackers use AI to scan for and exploit known vulnerabilities more quickly.
Why it works
The gap between a flaw being published and being exploited keeps shrinking.
The control
Prompt patching, an accurate asset inventory and continuous vulnerability scanning.
What good looks like
Critical patches applied quickly, with internet-facing systems prioritised.

Identity and supply chain

Trust nothing you have not checked

Every agent, plugin and API key is an identity. If nobody owns it, nobody is watching it.

AI tools are only as secure as the identities that use them. Put every approved AI service behind single sign-on with multi-factor authentication, so access follows your joiners, movers and leavers process. When someone leaves, their access to the assistant, its history and any agents they built should end on the same day as everything else.

Agents and integrations often run under service accounts or API keys rather than a person. These deserve particular care. Give each one its own identity, scope it tightly, store its secrets in a proper vault, rotate them, and make sure someone owns it. An unowned service account with broad access is an incident waiting to happen.

The AI supply chain is wider than it looks. It includes the model provider, any open models your teams download, the data used to tune or ground them, plugins, connectors, browser extensions and the libraries developers pull in. Each is a dependency you are trusting with data or behaviour.

Some model files can contain code that runs when the file is loaded, so models should come from reputable sources, be scanned, and use safer formats where they are available. Training and reference data can be deliberately poisoned to change how a model behaves. Plugins and extensions can request far more access than they need.

Treat AI components like any other third-party software. Keep an inventory, approve sources, review permissions before installation, and run supplier due diligence on data handling, retention, sub-processors and where data is stored. Read the contract terms on whether your data is used for training.

Finally, make sure AI activity is visible. Prompts, outputs, connector access and agent actions should be logged in a way your security team can search and alert on, with retention that balances investigation needs against privacy.

Red teaming

Test it the way an attacker would

Before go-live and after every significant change, try to make your AI misbehave on purpose.
01

Map what it can reach and do

List the data, connectors and actions each assistant or agent can use. That is your scope.
02

Try to override its instructions

Ask it to ignore its rules, reveal its configuration or act outside its role.
03

Plant hostile content

Hide instructions in documents, emails or pages it reads, and see whether it obeys.
04

Test the output path

Check what happens when output flows into code, email, databases or web pages.
05

Check for oversharing

Sign in as ordinary users in different roles and ask for things they should not see.
06

Fix, retest and record

Close the gaps, repeat the tests and keep the results as evidence for your risk register.
Remember
Models, prompts, connectors and permissions all change. Each change can reopen a door.

Incident response

When an AI incident happens

Extend your existing incident plan rather than writing a new one. These are the AI-specific steps to add.

Detect

Recognise AI incidents: leaked data, unexpected agent actions, injected content or fraud using cloned voices.

Contain

Disable the affected connector, agent or API key, and revoke tokens. Know who has authority to switch AI off.

Investigate

Use prompt, output and action logs to establish what the AI saw, said and did, and what data was involved.

Recover

Reverse unwanted actions, correct permissions and notify affected people or regulators where required.

Learn

Update tests, guardrails and training, and add the scenario to your next red team exercise.
If personal data is involved, your UK GDPR breach assessment and reporting duties apply as they would for any other incident.

Glossary

Words worth knowing

Prompt injection
Crafted instructions that try to make an AI system ignore its rules or act against the user's intent.
Indirect prompt injection
Injection hidden in content the AI reads, such as an email, web page or file.
Excessive agency
An AI system with more access, permissions or autonomy than its task needs.
Insecure output handling
Using AI output in code, systems or web pages without validation.
Data poisoning
Deliberately corrupting training or reference data to change how a model behaves.
Shadow AI
AI tools used without the organisation's knowledge or approval.
Red teaming
Testing a system by deliberately trying to make it fail or misbehave, as an attacker would.
Deepfake
AI-generated audio, image or video that imitates a real person.
Least privilege
Giving a person or system only the access it needs to do its job.
Service account
A non-human identity used by an application, integration or agent.
Guardrails
Controls that limit what an AI system will accept, say or do.
Single sign-on
One managed login for many services, so access can be granted and removed centrally.
