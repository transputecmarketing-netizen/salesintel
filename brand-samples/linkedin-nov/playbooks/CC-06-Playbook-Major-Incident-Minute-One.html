Foreword

In healthcare, an IT incident becomes a patient safety incident within the hour.

In June 2024, in the UK, a ransomware attack on Synnovis, the pathology provider for major hospitals in south east London, stopped normal blood testing. NHS England later reported that 10,152 acute outpatient appointments and 1,710 elective procedures had been postponed at the two most affected hospital trusts.1 A year later one of the hospitals confirmed that a long wait for a blood test result, caused by the attack, was one of the factors in a patient's death.2

It is not only a UK story, and not only a cyber story. In May 2024, in the US, a cyber attack on Ascension, which operates 140 hospitals in 19 states, forced several hospitals to divert ambulances and moved staff onto paper records.3 In June 2026 an English hospital declared a critical incident when its cooling systems failed in a heatwave.4 Almost two years after the Synnovis attack, one UK provider was still working in business continuity for pathology.5

This playbook is for the people who carry the bleep when that happens, in hospitals and health systems in the UK, the US, Europe and the Middle East: emergency managers and business continuity leads, administrators and executives on call, clinical, nursing and digital leaders, and communications teams. It covers the first hour, the downtime that follows and the plan to be ready before the next one. The companion intelligence report sets out the evidence in full.

Executive summary

Declare early, reach the right people, keep care going without the systems.

01 · The operational challenge
Clinical care runs on systems, suppliers and utilities that the hospital does not fully control.
Plans exist, but the first hour often depends on who happens to be on call and who answers.
Clinical teams, management, digital teams and external partners work from different information when it matters most.
02 · Incidents and risks to prepare for
A cyber attack or IT failure that takes pathology, the patient record or prescribing offline, at the hospital or a supplier.
A mass casualty incident or sustained surge that needs staff called in out of hours.
Loss of power, water, cooling or medical gases to clinical areas.
A long downtime: weeks on paper, results to back-enter and staff to rest.
03 · Key recommendations
Write declaration triggers and give named roles the authority to declare without waiting for certainty.
Call staff in by role on several channels, and track who has acknowledged.
Print, practise and time downtime procedures in every department, including transfusion.
Put suppliers, the ambulance service and your regional health authority on your incident lists.
Rehearse all three scenarios each year, and close every action from the review.
04 · What effective coordination looks like
A declaration in agreed words within minutes, reaching wards and partners at the same time.
One incident log and one actions board for clinical, management, digital teams and partners.
Downtime running as owned tasks, with the time to the first paper result measured.
Updates to staff and patients at fixed times until stand down, then a debrief within days.
05 · How Crises Control supports the response
Most organisations can send alerts. Few can manage incidents end-to-end.
Crises Control is an Operational Incident Coordination Platform. Ping calls staff in by role by push, SMS, voice and email, and no reply escalates to a deputy under the rules you configure.
Incident Manager and Task Manager give one log and owned downtime tasks; Audit & Reporting keeps the timestamped record for your review and your regulator.

Introduction

Three things decide how the first hour goes.

Almost every hospital can send an alert. Far fewer can run an incident from first report to final review: declare it, mobilise the right people, keep care safe on paper and show what was done. The ones that cope best have rehearsed three simple things until they are habits.

Declare early, in agreed words

Define incident levels and triggers in advance, name who can declare, and use the same words with staff and partners. Where your framework sets the wording, use it.

Know who is coming in

Reach on-call staff, ward leaders and off-duty teams in minutes, and know who has acknowledged, who is on the way and who has not answered.

Make downtime safe

Paper requests, manual result reporting, safe blood and clear handovers, practised before the systems fail, so patient care does not depend on the network or the plant room.

Names vary by country and framework. What matters is that everyone, from the ward to the board, means the same thing. Illustrative definitions: use your own framework's wording where one exists.

Declare and decide

Why the first decision is the hardest, and the most important

Declaring is how you get the right people moving. Waiting for certainty puts you two hours behind the wards.

Nobody wants to declare too soon. A critical incident is a public signal, it pulls in partners and it can feel like an admission. So the first hour often goes on fixing the problem quietly, while wards improvise and the executive on call hears about it second hand.

Declaring is how help arrives. United Kingdom: NHS England's framework describes a critical incident as principally an internal escalation response to disruption that will affect the ability to deliver safe patient care.6 United States: many hospitals organise their response with the Hospital Incident Command System (HICS), an incident command framework with job action sheets for each role.7 The labels differ; the purpose is the same.

Declaring is also how the right people start moving. An administrator on call who declares a business continuity incident at 08:15 can start the downtime procedures, call in the laboratory and digital leads and warn the emergency department. One who waits for certainty at 10:00 is two hours behind the wards.

So set the triggers in advance. Loss of a clinical system that supports results, prescribing or patient flow for more than a set time. Any suspected cyber attack on a clinical system. Loss of power, water, cooling or medical gases to a clinical area. A surge that takes the hospital to its highest escalation level. Write them down, give named roles the authority to declare and tell them they will never be criticised for declaring early and standing down.

Use the agreed words, and share facts in a structured form such as METHANE or SBAR, which the NHS framework sets out with an example for a cyber incident.6 Then tell your own staff, in the same words, at the same time. A declaration that reaches the regional health authority before it reaches the wards has gone the wrong way round.

Regional notes

Emergency preparedness: what applies where

Hospitals everywhere are expected to plan, train and exercise for emergencies. The detail, and who checks it, differs by country.
Regional note · United Kingdom
England: NHS-funded organisations work to NHS England's EPRR Framework and assess themselves each year against the EPRR Core Standards; the NHS Standard Contract requires providers to follow EPRR guidance.6 Under the Civil Contingencies Act 2004, NHS England, integrated care boards and foundation trusts providing A&E or ambulance services are Category 1 responders.8
IN PRACTICE Map your triggers to the EPRR definitions and keep the evidence for the annual assurance return. Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland have their own arrangements.
Regional note · United States
Hospitals in Medicare and Medicaid must meet the CMS emergency preparedness rule, 42 CFR 482.15: an all-hazards risk assessment and plan, policies, a communication plan, and training and testing, reviewed at least every 2 years, with exercises at least twice a year.9 The Joint Commission's revised Emergency Management standards apply to accredited hospitals from July 2022.10
IN PRACTICE The rule expects drills, exercises and real events to be analysed and documented, and the plan revised. Many hospitals use HICS to run the response.7,9
Regional note · European Union
Hospital preparedness is set nationally, and systems vary. The CER Directive (EU) 2022/2557 covers health among its sectors: Member States had to transpose it by 17 October 2024 and identify critical entities by 17 July 2026. Critical entities must take resilience measures and notify incidents.11
IN PRACTICE Ask your national authority whether your hospital is, or serves, a designated critical entity, and align your plan with national emergency arrangements.
Regional note · Middle East (UAE and Saudi Arabia)
Dubai: the DHA framework for healthcare emergencies, effective 14 January 2025, requires licensed facilities to run an annual hazard vulnerability analysis, keep a 24/7 contact for DHA, give hospitals an emergency operations centre and hold at least one exercise a year.12 Abu Dhabi launched a healthcare business continuity standard in May 2025.13
IN PRACTICE Saudi Arabia: CBAHI accreditation is linked to hospital licence renewal.14 JCI surveys use its 8th edition hospital standards from 1 January 2025.15
A summary for orientation, not legal advice. Requirements depend on sector, size, licence and jurisdiction, and change over time. Confirm the current text with your legal and compliance teams.

Regional notes

Reporting a cyber or data incident: the clocks differ

A cyber attack on a clinical system is an operational incident and, often, a reportable one. Know which clock starts, who files and to whom, before the day.
Regional note · United Kingdom
England: NHS trusts, foundation trusts, integrated care boards and certain independent providers are operators of essential services under the NIS Regulations 2018. They report network and information system incidents through the Data Security and Protection Toolkit within 72 hours of becoming aware.16
IN PRACTICE Name who files the report and when the 72-hour clock starts. Personal data breaches can also need a separate report to the data protection regulator.
Regional note · United States
Under the HIPAA Breach Notification Rule, covered entities notify affected individuals without unreasonable delay and no later than 60 calendar days after discovering a breach of unsecured health information; breaches affecting 500 or more people are reported to HHS at the same time.17
IN PRACTICE A ransomware attack can be both a downtime and a breach. Bring privacy, legal and digital leads into the incident team in the first hour.
Regional note · European Union
Under NIS2, health is a sector of high criticality. Entities in scope send an early warning within 24 hours of becoming aware of a significant incident, an incident notification within 72 hours and a final report no later than one month later. Member States had to transpose NIS2 by 17 October 2024.18
IN PRACTICE The detail is in national law. Check whether your hospital is an essential or important entity, and who your CSIRT or competent authority is.
Regional note · Middle East (UAE)
Requirements are set nationally and, in the UAE, by each health regulator. Dubai: the DHA framework requires facilities to name a focal point, preferably from incident command, and keep a phone number and email that reach them 24/7 during emergencies.12
IN PRACTICE Confirm with your health regulator and national cyber security authority which incidents you must report, to whom and how fast. Do not assume another region's clocks apply.
A summary for orientation, not legal advice. Requirements depend on sector, size, licence and jurisdiction, and change over time. Confirm the current text with your legal and compliance teams.

Three scenarios, three first hours

Scenario 1 · Acute hospital, three sites

Cyber attack at a laboratory partner

Where it fails
The laboratory is run by a partner; nobody at the hospital was on its call list, so wards heard first.
Who decides
The executive on call declares; the transfusion lead authorises O type use, agreed with the blood bank.
What happens
A joint call-in list and shared incident log with the partner; downtime forms printed on every ward.
How it is monitored
Time from first report to first paper result, measured in a joint exercise and again on the day.
Scenario 2 · Regional trauma centre

Mass casualty incident at night

Where it fails
The ambulance alert reaches the emergency department, but calling in theatres, critical care and the blood bank relies on a phone tree.
Who decides
The emergency department lead and executive on call declare; the incident commander opens the command centre.
What happens
Role-based call-in: coming, later, not available. Planned lists stood down; beds and theatres updated at set times.
How it is monitored
Acknowledgements by role against the casualty estimate, staff arrival times and beds released.
Scenario 3 · Hospital with a shared plant room

Cooling and power failure in a heatwave

Where it fails
Theatres, scanners and the server room shared one cooling plant, as one English hospital found in June 2026.4
Who decides
The estates lead advises; the executive on call declares and decides whether to move lists and patients.
What happens
Lists moved to a partner site; generator, fuel and medical gas checks run as tasks.
How it is monitored
Temperatures, generator and gas supply checked at set times against triggers.

Call-in

Five rules for calling staff in

Most hospitals have a cascade. Few know, in real time, who has answered. It matters most in a mass casualty incident at 03:00.
01

Alert by role, not by name

Lists are built around roles and rotas: the on-call transfusion scientist, the theatre coordinator, the nurse in charge. People change; roles do not.
02

Ask for an answer, not a read receipt

Every call-in asks a question: available now, available later, not available. Silence after the set time escalates to a deputy, under rules agreed in advance.
03

Use more than one channel

Push, SMS and voice. If the incident is a cyber attack, assume hospital email and chat may be down or untrusted.
04

Protect people off duty

Ask for what you need and no more. Track hours so the people who come in at 08:30 are not still there at midnight. Plan the second shift in the first hour.
05

Stand down in the same way

Release staff with a message as clear as the one that called them in, and tell them when the hot debrief will be.
Remember
If you cannot see who has acknowledged within ten minutes, you do not yet know whether you have a response team.

Keep patients safe

Downtime procedures: the plan you hope never to use

Can a ward request an urgent blood test, get the result and act on it safely with no computers at all?

Most clinical areas have a downtime folder. The question is whether anyone has opened it this year. The test is simple: can a ward request an urgent blood test, receive the result and act on it safely with no computers at all? If the answer depends on one person who knows how, it is not a procedure.

Downtime can last far longer than plans assume. United Kingdom: after the Synnovis attack, one provider estimated in early 2026 that entering 161,560 pathology reports into patient records had been delayed, and it had recorded 122 patient safety incidents involving incorrect, unavailable or delayed results.5 United States: Ascension told patients it expected to rely on downtime procedures for some time.3 Recovery has its own risks: results back-entered weeks later, duplicate records and patients lost to follow-up.

Blood is the sharpest edge. When blood cannot be matched in the usual way, transfusion falls back on O type blood, and stocks come under pressure. Every transfusion laboratory needs a downtime plan agreed with clinicians, including who authorises O type use.

Good downtime procedures are short, printed and practised. They name the forms, the phone numbers, the runners and the order of priority: emergency department, critical care, maternity, theatres, then the rest. They say how results will be checked and entered when systems return. And they are tested with the people who will use them, not just the people who wrote them.

Treat a cyber attack differently from a power cut in one way: you may not be able to trust the systems even when they come back. Keep digital and clinical leads in the same incident meeting, and decide your out-of-band channel now, before staff choose one for you.

System partners

Who needs to hear from you, and when

A declared incident is rarely yours alone. Agree these routes in advance and test them in exercises.

Regional health authority

Tell your health authority or commissioner when you declare, in the agreed format. United Kingdom: the integrated care board, which reports to NHS England through regional on-call.6

Ambulance service

If you need diverts or redirection, say so early, and tell them again when you can accept patients normally.

Suppliers and partners

Laboratory, imaging, patient record and facilities partners belong on your call-in lists, and you on theirs.

Primary care and the public

Family doctors, care homes, pharmacies and patients need to know what still works and where to go instead.

Toolkit 04 · Escalation workflow

How an incident climbs, and who decides at each step

From the first report on a ward to a system-wide response. Times are illustrative: set your own and write them into the plan. Never wait at a level because the next one feels too big.
LEVEL 1
Department response
Trigger
A system is slow or failing in one service, with a working fallback. Patient care is not yet affected.
Who is alerted
Department lead, digital service desk, site manager.
Who decides
Department or site manager. Logs the fault as a possible incident, not a ticket.
↓ESCALATE IF NO FIX IN 30 MINUTES, CARE IS AFFECTED OR A CYBER CAUSE IS POSSIBLE
LEVEL 2
Business continuity incident
Trigger
A clinical system or utility is down beyond the agreed time, or special arrangements are needed. SEV 3 on the ladder.
Who is alerted
Manager on call, service leads, digital, estates, emergency management lead. Call-in by role, with acknowledgement.
Who decides
Manager on call declares and starts downtime procedures.
↓ESCALATE IF CRITICAL SERVICES ARE LOST, PEOPLE ARE AT RISK OR RANSOMWARE IS SUSPECTED
LEVEL 3
Critical incident
Trigger
Loss of critical services or risk of harm; suspected cyber attack on a clinical system. SEV 2 or SEV 1.
Who is alerted
Executive on call, incident commander, clinical leads, comms; health authority told at once.
Who decides
Executive on call declares, in the agreed words, and opens the command centre.
↓ESCALATE IF OTHER HOSPITALS ARE AFFECTED, CASUALTIES ARE ARRIVING OR HELP IS NEEDED FROM OUTSIDE
LEVEL 4
System or major incident
Trigger
Several providers affected, mutual aid needed, ambulance diverts, or a major incident declared.
Who is alerted
Regional health authority, ambulance service, suppliers, emergency services as needed.
Who decides
Your executive still decides for your hospital. The health authority coordinates the system and mutual aid.
Notify your regional health authority when you declare. United Kingdom: notify the integrated care board, which reports to NHS England through regional on-call.6 Step down as deliberately as you stepped up, with a message at each level.

Toolkit 05 · Communication templates

Six messages ready to send

Short, specific and honest. Fill the brackets, do not speculate on cause, and always give the time of the next update.
SMS · On-call staff137 chars
[Hospital] critical incident: lab systems down. Incident team to [room] now. Reply 1 coming, 2 later, 3 not available. Next update 09:00.
Send by role. Escalate no reply after 10 minutes.
Push · All clinical staff
Laboratory systems unavailable. Use paper request forms and phone urgent results via [extension]. Transfusion is on downtime procedures. Do not use [system] until told. Update at 09:30.
Pin it in the app; repeat at each handover.
SMS · Patients booked today131 chars
[Hospital]: an IT problem is affecting blood tests. Please attend as planned unless we contact you. Info: [web page]. Do not reply.
Approved by clinical leads first.
Email · Health authority and regulator
Critical incident declared by [hospital] at [time]: [system] unavailable, cause under investigation. Patient impact: [summary]. Reports filed: [ref]. Requests: [mutual aid]. Next update [time].
From the incident log, not from memory.

[Hospital] critical incident since [time]. Please send your incident lead to our call at [time] and route urgent samples via [route]. Contact: [name, number].
Agree the joint log and update times on the first call.

Glossary

Words worth knowing

Incident command
A standard structure of roles for running an incident, led by an incident commander.
HICS
Hospital Incident Command System: an incident command framework for hospitals, used widely in the US.
Emergency operations centre
The room or virtual space from which the incident is run; also called a command centre.
EPRR
Emergency preparedness, resilience and response: the NHS England framework for incidents (UK).
Business continuity incident
Disruption below acceptable levels that needs special arrangements until services recover.
Critical incident
Loss of ability to deliver critical services, or risk of harm to patients and staff.
Major incident
An event with serious consequences needing special arrangements by one or more responder agencies.
Mass casualty incident
More casualties than normal resources can manage, needing a declared, coordinated response.
METHANE
A format for sharing incident information: major incident, exact location, type, hazards, access, number of casualties, emergency services.
SBAR
Situation, background, assessment, recommendation: a structured way to escalate.
Downtime procedure
How a service works safely when a clinical system is unavailable.
Mutual aid
Help from other organisations, such as testing capacity or staff, during an incident.
Out-of-band channel
A way to communicate that does not depend on the systems that may be compromised.
Back-entry
Entering results and notes recorded on paper during downtime into the system once it is safe.
NIS2
The EU directive on cyber security risk management and incident reporting, which covers health.
Hot debrief
A short review straight after an incident, usually within 48 hours of stand down.
