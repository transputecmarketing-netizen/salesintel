Foreword

In healthcare, an IT incident becomes a patient safety incident within the hour.

On 3 June 2024 a ransomware attack on Synnovis, the pathology provider for major hospitals in south east London, stopped normal blood testing. Guy's and St Thomas' and King's College Hospital declared a critical incident the next morning.1 NHS England later reported that 10,152 acute outpatient appointments and 1,710 elective procedures had been postponed at the two trusts.2 Because blood could not be matched in the usual way, NHS Blood and Transplant asked O type donors to book urgently.3

In June 2025 King's College Hospital confirmed that a long wait for a blood test result, caused by the attack, was one of the factors that contributed to a patient's death.4 In April 2026, almost two years on, one mental health trust in the area was still working in business continuity for pathology.5 And in the summer of 2026 trusts declared critical incidents when chillers failed in a heatwave and when IT systems went down.6,7

This playbook is for the people who carry the bleep when that happens: accountable emergency officers, EPRR and business continuity leads, on-call managers and directors, digital, nursing and communications teams, in NHS trusts and in independent providers that deliver NHS-funded care. It covers the first hour, the downtime that follows and the plan to be ready before the next one. The companion intelligence report sets out the evidence in full.

Introduction

Three things decide how the first hour goes.

Every trust has an incident response plan and a business continuity plan. The ones that cope best on the day have rehearsed three simple things until they are habits.

Declare early, in the right words

Use the NHS England definitions for business continuity, critical and major incidents, and the standard message formats, so partners know exactly what you mean.8

Know who is coming in

Reach on-call staff, ward leaders and off-duty teams in minutes, and know who has acknowledged, who is on the way and who has not answered.

Make downtime safe

Paper requests, manual result reporting, safe blood and clear handovers, practised before the systems fail, so patient care does not depend on the network.

Definitions

Three declarations, in NHS England's words

From the NHS EPRR Framework, version 3, July 2022. Use them exactly, and always say who declared it.8
Declaration
Definition
What it means in practice
Business continuity incident
“an event or occurrence that disrupts, or might disrupt, an organisation’s normal service delivery, to below acceptable pre-defined levels. This would require special arrangements to be put in place until services can return to an acceptable level.”
Your business continuity plan is running. Examples in the framework include a surge in demand, a utilities breakdown and significant equipment failure.
Critical incident
“any localised incident where the level of disruption results in an organisation temporarily or permanently losing its ability to deliver critical services; or where patients and staff may be at risk of harm.”
Principally an internal escalation. You need support from partners across the system to restore normal operating functions.
Major incident
Defined by the Cabinet Office and JESIP as “an event or situation with a range of serious consequences that require special arrangements to be implemented by one or more emergency responder agency.”
Usually multi-agency. There are no universal triggers; the decision is made in a local, operational context.
Format
“Critical Incident declared by (organisation)”. For major incidents: standby, declared, cancelled or stand down.
Tell your ICB, which reports to NHS England through regional on-call.8

Declare and decide

Why the first decision is the hardest, and the most important

Declaring is how you get the right people moving. Waiting for certainty puts you two hours behind the wards.

Nobody wants to declare too soon. A critical incident is a public signal, it pulls in partners and it can feel like an admission. So the first hour often goes on fixing the problem quietly, while wards improvise and the on-call director hears about it second hand.

The NHS framework is clear that a critical incident is principally an internal escalation response to increased system pressures or disruption to services that will affect the ability to deliver safe patient care.8 It exists to bring in help. When East Kent Hospitals declared one over IT problems in July 2026, it said the declaration let it keep patients safe by drawing on support from colleagues across the health system.7

Declaring is also how you get the right people moving. An on-call manager who declares a business continuity incident at 08:15 can start the downtime procedures, call in the pathology and digital leads and warn the emergency department. One who waits for certainty at 10:00 is two hours behind the wards.

So set the triggers in advance. Loss of a clinical system that supports results, prescribing or patient flow for more than a set time. Any suspected cyber attack on a clinical system. Loss of power, water, cooling or medical gases to a clinical area. A surge that takes the organisation to its highest operational pressure level under the OPEL framework.9 Write them down, give named roles the authority to declare and tell them they will never be criticised for declaring early and standing down.

Use the agreed words. NHS England asks for the format “Critical Incident declared by (organisation)”, and the four standard major incident messages: standby, declared, cancelled and stand down.8 Share facts with partners in METHANE or SBAR form, which the framework sets out, including an example for a cyber incident.8

Then tell your own staff, in the same words, at the same time. A declaration that reaches the ICB before it reaches the wards has gone the wrong way round.

Severity

A healthcare severity ladder for the first hour

An internal scale for alerting and staffing. It sits alongside the formal EPRR declarations, not instead of them. If in doubt, grade up and step down later.
SEV 1
Major or critical, trust wide
Triggers
Loss of pathology, EPR or prescribing across sites; suspected ransomware; mass casualty major incident declared by your trust or the ambulance service; loss of power or water to clinical areas.
Who is alerted, how
Executive on call, AEO, incident team, all clinical leads, digital, comms; ICB on call informed at once.
Push, SMS and voice · ack in 5 min
SEV 2
Critical in one service or site
Triggers
One site's emergency department, theatres or diagnostics unable to work safely; critical system down beyond the agreed time; OPEL 4 with patient safety risk.
Who is alerted, how
Director and manager on call, service and site leads, digital, comms.
Push and SMS · ack in 10 min
SEV 3
Business continuity incident
Triggers
Planned downtime overrunning; single system or equipment failure with a working fallback; staffing gap in one area; supplier outage.
Who is alerted, how
Manager on call, service leads, EPRR lead.
Push and email · ack in 30 min
SEV 4
Watch and prepare
Triggers
Supplier warning, heat or cold alert, cyber advisory affecting your systems, rising pressure.
Who is alerted, how
EPRR lead, digital, site team.
Email and app · no ack needed

Call-in

Five rules for calling staff in

Most trusts have a cascade. Few know, in real time, who has answered.
01

Alert by role, not by name

Lists are built around roles and rotas: the on-call transfusion scientist, the theatre coordinator, the matron on duty. People change; roles do not.
02

Ask for an answer, not a read receipt

Every call-in asks a question: available now, available later, not available. Silence after the set time escalates to a deputy automatically.
03

Use more than one channel

Push, SMS and voice. If the incident is a cyber attack, assume trust email and Teams may be down or untrusted.
04

Protect people off duty

Ask for what you need and no more. Track hours so the people who come in at 08:30 are not still there at midnight. Plan the second shift in the first hour.
05

Stand down in the same way

Release staff with a message as clear as the one that called them in, and tell them when the hot debrief will be.
Remember
If you cannot see who has acknowledged within ten minutes, you do not yet know whether you have a response team.

Messages

Six messages ready to send

Short, specific and honest. Fill the brackets, do not speculate on cause, and always give the time of the next update.
SMS · On-call staff134 chars
[Trust] critical incident: lab systems down. Incident team to [room] now. Reply 1 coming, 2 later, 3 not available. Next update 09:00.
Send by role. Escalate no reply after 10 minutes.
Push · All clinical staff
Pathology systems unavailable. Use paper request forms and phone urgent results via [extension]. Transfusion is on downtime procedures. Do not use [system] until told. Update at 09:30.
Pin it in the app; repeat at each handover.
SMS · Patients booked today133 chars
[Trust]: we have an IT problem affecting blood tests. Please attend as planned unless we contact you. Info: [web page]. Do not reply.
Approved by clinical leads first.
Web and phone · Public holding line
We have declared a critical incident because of an IT problem affecting pathology. Emergency care is open. Please use NHS 111, your GP or a pharmacy for non-urgent needs. We will update this page at [time].
Same words on the switchboard message.
Email · ICB and partners
Critical Incident declared by [trust] at [time]. METHANE summary attached. Requests: urgent testing mutual aid, ambulance awareness. Next sitrep [time].
From the incident log, not from memory.
SMS · Community and lone staff118 chars
[Trust]: IT incident. Visits go ahead. Use paper notes, call [number] for results. Check in at 12:00 and on finishing.
Ask for a reply to confirm they are safe.

In practice

Three providers, three first hours

Acute trust, three sites

Ransomware at a pathology partner

The gap
The lab is run by a partner; nobody at the trust was on its incident call list.
The fix
Joint call-in list and shared incident log with the partner; downtime forms printed on every ward.
The check
A joint exercise with the partner, timed from first report to first paper result.
Watch out
Transfusion needs its own downtime plan; agree O type use with the blood bank.
Community trust, 1,200 field staff

EPR outage during a cold snap

The gap
Visit lists live only in the electronic record; staff were already on the road.
The fix
Daily offline visit lists, a check-in rule for lone workers and SMS updates that need no app login.
The check
Every lone worker confirmed safe by the end of the shift.
Watch out
About one in seven NHS staff reported physical violence from patients or the public in the 2025 staff survey.12
Independent provider, NHS contract

Cooling failure in a heatwave

The gap
Theatres and the server room relied on the same chiller plant.
The fix
A business continuity incident declared and the ICB told; lists moved to a partner site.
The check
Temperature triggers written into the severity ladder.
Watch out
The NHS Standard Contract requires providers of NHS-funded services to comply with EPRR guidance.8

Keep patients safe

Downtime procedures: the plan you hope never to use

Can a ward request an urgent blood test, get the result and act on it safely with no computers at all?

Most clinical areas have a downtime folder. The question is whether anyone has opened it this year. The test is simple: can a ward request an urgent blood test, receive the result and act on it safely with no computers at all? If the answer depends on one person who knows how, it is not a procedure.

Synnovis showed how long downtime can last. NHS England reported that services in south east London were largely restored by the end of 2024, but in early 2026 one trust estimated that entering 161,560 pathology reports into patient records had been delayed, and it had recorded 122 patient safety incidents involving incorrect, unavailable or delayed results.5 Recovery has its own risks: results back-entered weeks later, duplicate records and patients lost to follow-up.

Blood is the sharpest edge. When hospitals cannot match blood in the usual way they rely on O type blood, which is safe for all patients. That is why NHS Blood and Transplant appealed for O negative and O positive donors within a week of the attack.3 Every transfusion laboratory needs a downtime plan agreed with clinicians, including who authorises O type use.

Good downtime procedures are short, printed and practised. They name the forms, the phone numbers, the runners and the order of priority: emergency department, critical care, maternity, theatres, then the rest. They say how results will be checked and entered when systems return. And they are tested in exercises with the people who will use them, not just the people who wrote them.

Treat the cyber attack as different from the power cut in one way: you may not be able to trust the systems even when they come back. Keep digital and clinical leads in the same incident meeting. The 2017 WannaCry review found that local staff shared information by personal mobile phones and WhatsApp when email was lost.13 Decide your out-of-band channel now, before staff choose one for you.

System partners

Who needs to hear from you, and when

A declared incident is rarely yours alone. Agree these routes in advance and test them in exercises.

ICB and NHS England

Tell your ICB on call when you declare. The ICB reports to NHS England through regional on-call, and coordinates mutual aid.8

Ambulance service

If you need diverts or redirection, say so early. The framework lists ambulance redirection as a reason to notify.8

Suppliers and partners

Pathology, imaging, EPR and facilities partners belong on your call-in lists, and you on theirs.

GPs, social care, the public

Primary care, care homes and patients need to know what still works and where to go instead.
Report cyber incidents to the NHS England cyber security operations centre straight away.10 Operators of essential services report network and information system incidents through the DSPT within 72 hours.11

Long incidents

Winter, heat and the incident that does not end

Many healthcare incidents are rising tides, not explosions. They need a different rhythm.
Pressure
What often happens
What good looks like
Winter surge
Escalation drifts from OPEL 3 to 4 without a declared incident or extra staff.
Triggers that link OPEL levels to your severity ladder and call-in lists.9
Heat
Chillers and server rooms fail together; theatres and scanners stop.
Temperature triggers, cooling checks and a plan for diagnostics, as trusts learned in June 2026.6
Long cyber recovery
Staff run on paper for weeks with no rota change or welfare plan.
A recovery cell, shift patterns, welfare checks and a plan for back-entering results.
Lone and community staff
Visits continue with no way to raise an alarm if systems are down.
Check-in times, one-touch alarms and visit lists that work offline.
Communication fatigue
Updates stop once the crisis feels normal.
Fixed update times for staff and patients until stand down.

Glossary

Words worth knowing

EPRR
Emergency preparedness, resilience and response: the NHS England programme and framework for incidents and emergencies.
Accountable emergency officer
The board-level executive responsible for EPRR in an NHS-funded organisation.
Business continuity incident
Disruption below acceptable levels that needs special arrangements until services recover.
Critical incident
Localised loss of ability to deliver critical services, or risk of harm to patients and staff.
Major incident
An event with serious consequences needing special arrangements by one or more responder agencies.
OPEL
Operational pressures escalation levels, from 1 to 4, used to describe pressure on services.
METHANE
A JESIP format for sharing incident information: major incident, exact location, type, hazards, access, number of casualties, emergency services.
SBAR
Situation, background, assessment, recommendation: a structured way to escalate.
Downtime procedure
How a service works safely when a clinical system is unavailable.
Mutual aid
Help from other organisations, such as testing capacity or staff, during an incident.
Hot debrief
A short review straight after an incident, within 48 hours of stand down.
DSPT
The Data Security and Protection Toolkit, used by health bodies to report security incidents.
ICB
Integrated care board: plans and coordinates NHS services across a local area, with 24/7 on-call.
Out-of-band channel
A way to communicate that does not depend on the systems that may be compromised.
