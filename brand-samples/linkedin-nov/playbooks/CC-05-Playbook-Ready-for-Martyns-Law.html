Foreword

In a busy bar or hotel, the first minutes belong to the people on shift.

On the night of the London Bridge and Borough Market attack in June 2017, the attackers moved through a restaurant courtyard, bars and a restaurant on Stoney Street. The Chief Coroner found that all eight people who died did so within 15 minutes of the attack beginning.1 In the City Room at Manchester Arena a few weeks earlier, a concern raised by a member of the public did not reach anyone who could act on it, partly because a radio channel was busy.2

Martyn's Law, the Terrorism (Protection of Premises) Act 2025, is named after Martyn Hett, one of the 22 people killed at Manchester Arena.2,3 It received Royal Assent on 3 April 2025, and the Security Industry Authority expects it to come into force in spring 2027.3,4 Pubs, bars, restaurants, cafés, hotels and nightclubs where 200 or more people can be expected at the same time will need procedures to evacuate, invacuate, lock down and communicate with everyone on site.4,5,6

This playbook is for the people who will make that work on a Saturday night in December: owners, general managers, duty managers, security leads and operations teams. It covers the law as enacted, the roles each shift needs, how to reach guests and staff in minutes, the other emergencies that are far more likely, and a dated plan. The companion intelligence report sets out the evidence in full.

Introduction

Three things protect people on the worst night.

Hospitality venues are busy, noisy, open late and staffed by people on different shifts, many of them new. The venues that cope best are not the ones with the thickest plan. They are the ones where every shift knows who decides, what to do and how to tell everyone fast.

One person decides

Every shift has a named duty manager who can declare an evacuation, invacuation or lockdown without waiting for permission, and everyone knows who that is tonight.

Everyone hears it at once

A code phrase, a radio call and an alert to every phone, so the kitchen, the bedrooms and the smoking area hear the same instruction within a minute.

Plans people actually know

Short procedures, briefed at induction and walked through on the late shift, not a binder in the office that nobody has opened.

Know the duty

What Martyn's Law asks of hospitality

Count the busiest realistic night, not a quiet Tuesday.

The Terrorism (Protection of Premises) Act 2025 received Royal Assent on 3 April 2025. The Home Office expects an implementation period of at least 24 months, and there is no legal requirement to comply until the duties come into force.4 The SIA, which will regulate the Act, says it expects the Act to come into force in spring 2027 and will confirm the exact date ahead of time.3

The Act covers premises wholly or mainly used for purposes listed in Schedule 1. Statutory guidance says the "food and drink" use "includes bars, pubs, restaurants, cafés and food halls"; "hotels etc" also covers hostels and holiday parks; and nightclubs fall under entertainment and leisure.6

Premises are in the standard tier where 200 to 799 people, including staff, can reasonably be expected to be present at the same time from time to time, and in the enhanced tier at 800 or more.4 Count the busiest realistic night, not a quiet Tuesday: a Christmas party, a New Year's Eve ticketed event or a wedding in the function suite.

Standard tier premises must notify the SIA and have, so far as is reasonably practicable, appropriate public protection procedures for evacuation, invacuation, lockdown and communication.4,5 The Home Office says these are simple, low-cost steps that do not require physical alterations or new equipment.4

Enhanced tier premises must also have public protection measures covering monitoring, movement, physical safety and security, and the security of information; document compliance and give it to the SIA; and, where the responsible person is an organisation, designate a senior individual.8

Penalties reach £10,000 for standard duty premises, and £18m or 5% of worldwide revenue for enhanced duty premises.9 Existing premises will have three months from commencement to notify the SIA, and changes must be notified within 28 days.10

Four procedures

Evacuate, invacuate, lock down, communicate

The Act's four public protection procedures, as the Home Office defines them, and what each means in a hotel, bar or restaurant.5

Evacuation

"Getting people away from danger by moving them out of the premises." Know every exit, including through the kitchen, and where guests go once they are out.5

Invacuation

"Moving people away from danger to a place within the premises." A function room, back corridor or cellar away from glass and the main doors.5

Lockdown

"Securing the premises to prevent individuals entering or leaving." Who locks which door, who drops the shutters, how fast, and on every shift.5

Communication

"Ensuring information is provided to individuals at the premises." Staff, guests, bedrooms, terraces, toilets, contractors and the next shift.5
The Home Office's own example is a 400-seat restaurant: two evacuation routes, nominated staff who lock doors and close shutters on the shift manager's instruction, and a short briefing for every new starter at induction.5

Roles

Who does what, on every shift

Name the people at the start of each shift. There is no statutory training requirement, but staff with a role in the procedures must know them and what they have to do.5
01

Duty manager: decides and declares

Has authority to call evacuation, invacuation or lockdown at once, and to call 999. Does not wait for head office.
02

Door and security team: first eyes

Spot and report early, lock doors, stop people entering, and give police clear information on arrival.
03

Bar and floor staff: move the room

Each person owns a section: move guests from glass and doors, sweep toilets and terraces, and report when clear.
04

Reception or night manager: the hotel

Calls 999, reaches guests in bedrooms and holds the guest list and today's function bookings.
05

Kitchen and back of house: the rear

Secure the back doors and delivery yard, cut gas if instructed, and know which rooms can be used as safe areas.
06

Communicator: who has heard

Sends the alert, tracks who has replied and chases the rest, and logs who was told what and when.

Communication

Reaching guests and staff in minutes

Noise, music, bedrooms, outdoor areas and staff on breaks make hospitality hard to reach. Use more than one route, and confirm who has heard.

A code phrase everyone knows

A neutral phrase on the PA or radio that tells staff what is happening without alarming guests. Change it if it leaks.

Stop the music, light the room

Music off and house lights up so announcements can be heard, unless lockdown means lights off on police advice.

An alert to every phone

One message to staff on duty by SMS, push and voice, with a reply to confirm. Chase anyone who does not answer.

Guests you cannot see

Bedrooms, toilets, terraces, smoking areas and private dining rooms each need a named person or route.

People off site

The next shift, off-duty staff and families. Tell them not to come in, and keep updates on a schedule. Counter Terrorism Policing's public advice is RUN, HIDE, TELL.11
At Manchester Arena, a steward without a radio passed on a concern; a colleague with one tried to report it but someone else was talking, and it went no further.2 Never rely on a single channel.

Guest communication

Talking to guests: what to consider

Guests do not know your code phrases, your layout or your staff. Plan how each group is reached, and agree what nobody says.
Guests
How to reach them
What to say, and what to avoid
In bars, restaurants and function rooms
Music off, lights up. Staff in each section give the same short instruction face to face.
Say what to do, not why: "Please come with me to the back room now." Do not shout words that cause panic.
In bedrooms
Room phone, a text to the mobile given at booking, and door knocks only where it is safe.
"Stay in your room, lock the door, keep away from windows. Next update by [time]."
Who do not speak English
Short translated lines in the languages your guests most often speak, plus showing the way.
Short sentences and plain words. No idioms, and never the staff code phrase.
Who need help to move or to hear an alarm
Ask at booking and check-in; name a member of staff for each guest on the shift.
Tell them who is coming, where to wait and how they will be told it is safe.
Outside: terraces, smoking areas, queues
Bring them in or move them away, as the duty manager decides. In lockdown the door team controls entry.
"Come inside now" or "Move away from the building", with a clear direction.
Everyone, at every stage
One approved message, repeated, with a time for the next update.
Never guess the cause or casualties, name anyone involved or post on social media. Media get the holding line.

Other emergencies

The emergencies that are far more likely

Terrorism is rare. Fire, medical emergencies, spiking, power and system failures are not. The same roles and alerts handle them all.
Emergency
First action on site
First action for managers
Fire alarm in a full venue
Evacuate by the plan. Sweep sections and bedrooms. Do not let guests collect coats or go back in.
Account for staff and guests from the booking and room lists. Meet the fire service with the plan.
Medical emergency
Call 999, send for the defibrillator and a first aider, clear space and send someone to guide the ambulance.
Look after witnesses and staff, record what happened and check first aid cover for the rest of the night.
Suspected spiking
Stay with the person, get help and call police or an ambulance if needed. Keep the drink if you can.
Report it, keep CCTV and brief door staff. Police received 6,732 reports in a year to April 2023.12
Power failure
Emergency lighting check, stop cooking, keep exits clear, and decide quickly whether to evacuate.
Call the supplier or facilities, plan for lifts, fridges, tills and door locks, and tell arriving guests.
Booking or payment systems down
Switch to the paper booking list and offline or cash procedure. Do not keep restarting terminals.
Find out whether it is an outage or an attack, and tell sites what still works. A 2022 cyber incident disrupted IHG's booking channels.13
Violence or disorder
Make safe, call 999 if anyone is in danger, and do not detain. Staff safety first.
Support the staff involved, keep CCTV and review staffing for the next nights.

Messages

Six messages ready to send

Short, specific and calm. Fill the brackets, never guess the cause, and always say when the next update will come.
SMS · Staff on duty139 chars
LOCKDOWN at [venue]. Move guests away from doors and glass to [area]. Doors stay locked. Do not go outside. Reply 1 when your area is safe.
Under 160 characters, so it arrives as one message.
SMS · Hotel guests144 chars
[Hotel]: police are dealing with an incident nearby. Please stay in your room, lock the door and keep away from windows. Next update by [22:45].
Only to guests who have given a mobile number.
SMS · Off-duty and next shift131 chars
[Venue] is closed tonight due to an incident nearby. Do not travel in. Everyone on site is being looked after. Next update [08:00].
Email · Guests with bookings
Because of an incident near [venue], we are closed this evening. Please do not travel to us. Your booking will be [moved or refunded] and we will contact you by [time].
Send from the booking system so it reaches tonight's and tomorrow's bookings.
Email · Suppliers and booking platforms
Our booking and payment systems are offline while we investigate. Deliveries continue as booked. Do not act on any request to change bank details; call [known number].
Attackers often follow an outage with fake payment requests.
Holding line · Media
We are aware of an incident near [venue] this evening. Our priority is the safety of our guests and team, and we are following police advice. We will share more when we can.

Glossary

Words worth knowing

Martyn's Law
The Terrorism (Protection of Premises) Act 2025, named after Martyn Hett, who was killed at Manchester Arena.
Responsible person
The person or organisation in control of qualifying premises for the use that brings them into scope.
Standard tier
Premises where 200 to 799 people, including staff, can reasonably be expected at the same time.
Enhanced tier
Premises where 800 or more people can reasonably be expected at the same time.
Public protection procedures
Actions staff can take if they suspect an attack: evacuation, invacuation, lockdown and communication.
Public protection measures
Wider enhanced tier measures on monitoring, movement, physical security and information security.
Invacuation
Moving people away from danger to a safer place inside the premises.
Lockdown
Securing the premises to stop people entering or leaving.
Reasonably practicable
Proportionate: weighing what a step achieves against its cost, time and difficulty.
Immediate vicinity
The area close to the premises. There is no fixed distance.
SIA
The Security Industry Authority, the regulator for Martyn's Law.
Senior individual
The person an organisation must designate for enhanced tier premises.
Acknowledgement
A recipient's confirmation that an alert has been read and acted on.
Holding line
A short, accurate statement used while facts are still being established.

Buyer's guide

Eight questions to ask any incident platform supplier

Many tools can send an alert. These questions separate a notification tool from a platform that coordinates the whole response.
01
Does it stop at the message?
Ask what the platform does after the alert is sent: tasks, log, escalation, recovery.
02
Can it prove who acknowledged?
Delivery is not acknowledgement. Ask how silence is detected and escalated.
03
Does it run your procedures?
Ask whether your plans become owned tasks with deadlines, or stay as documents.
04
Is there one shared picture?
Ask what leaders see live, and whether it needs someone to update a spreadsheet.
05
Does it work when your systems do not?
Ask how teams coordinate if email, chat or your network is down.
06
Can frontline staff raise the alarm?
Ask how a colleague on site escalates, with their location, in one action.
07
What evidence does it leave behind?
Ask for a sample timeline of the kind a regulator, insurer or inquiry would want.
08
Can you rehearse in it?
Ask whether you can run exercises on the same platform you would use for real.
