Executive summary

The weak points in UK transport were known. What failed was the link between knowing and acting.

Late on 20 March 2025 a fire at the North Hyde substation cut power to part of Heathrow. The airport closed for most of 21 March and more than 270,000 passenger journeys were disrupted.1,2 NESO's review found that losing one of Heathrow's three supply points cut power to some operationally critical systems, and that a high moisture reading taken at the substation in 2018 had not been acted on.1

Heathrow's own review, led by Ruth Kelly, found the decision to close was correct, but that nearly all emergency power on the affected supply would have run out before main power returned, and that a key alert went unheard overnight.2,3 The CAA's review of the August 2023 air traffic failure had already found partners hearing late and an on-call engineer who could not log in remotely.4,5 On 8 September 2026 a different software defect at NATS delayed, cancelled or diverted more than 2,000 flights.6,7

Cyber attacks on suppliers added a second pattern. A ransomware attack on a check-in system supplier hit Heathrow, Brussels and Berlin in September 2025, and an attack on Jaguar Land Rover stopped production for five weeks, with a modelled UK cost of £1.9bn across more than 5,000 organisations.8,9,10

This report sets out what the reviews found, separates findings from commentary, and ends with practical steps for UK transport and logistics leaders.

The bottom line
Treat power, systems, suppliers and people as one resilience problem: know your single points of failure and how long each fallback lasts, reach every key person and partner by more than one route, and rehearse the first hour with the organisations you depend on.

The Heathrow lesson

One night in March

An alert is only as good as the device that receives it.

Late on 20 March 2025, North Hyde, a 275kV substation in west London, lost all supplies after a fire. NESO found the fire was caused by a catastrophic failure of a high voltage bushing on a transformer, most likely because moisture had got in. An elevated moisture reading in oil samples taken in July 2018 had not been followed by actions appropriate to its severity.1

The government's response to NESO adds that the protocols for logging and scheduling routine maintenance had not flagged that essential work was outstanding, and that the fire risk assessment did not include a site-level view of nearby oil-filled equipment.16 Ofgem opened an enforcement investigation into the transmission owner on 2 July 2025, stressing that this did not mean it had found any breach.12

The fire did not only hit Heathrow. NESO says 66,919 customers supplied directly from the local distribution network were affected, alongside road and rail services, Hillingdon Hospital and homes near the site, some of which were evacuated.1 Three data centres lost mains power but kept running on generators.1

At Heathrow, the loss of one of three supply points cut power to some operationally critical systems.1 Reporting on the Kelly Review says the first automated alert to senior leaders went out at 00:21, that the decision to suspend operations was taken at 01:15, and that limited arrivals resumed at 16:00, with full operations on 22 March.2

The review found the decision was correct and that alternative choices would not have materially changed the outcome.11 It also found that overnight alerts had not reached one senior leader because the phone had switched to silent without their knowledge, and recommended a second means of contact for significant incidents.2 The lesson applies to every organisation, not one person: an alert is only as good as the device that receives it.

Most transport and logistics businesses do not run an airport. But almost all of them depend on a small number of power connections, control rooms and systems, and assume that backup will cover the gap. North Hyde tested that assumption in public.

The pattern

When the failure is in someone else's system

The operator facing passengers was rarely the one whose system failed.

On the August 2023 bank holiday, the UK's automatic flight plan processing failed at 08:32 after receiving a plan with duplicate waypoint names. Restrictions ran until 18:03, with knock-on disruption for days.5

The independent review found that the on-call engineer's login could not readily be verified remotely, so the engineer travelled in and arrived 3 hours 15 minutes after the failure began. The manufacturer was not contacted for more than four hours, then found a fix within 30 minutes.5 More than 700,000 passengers were affected, and the panel made 34 recommendations.4

On 8 September 2026 a different defect stopped the same national system. NATS says a paused request to allocate an aircraft code did not resume correctly and corrupted later flight data, within a millisecond. Restrictions had to be applied across the UK so the system could be restarted.6 More than 2,000 flights were delayed, cancelled or diverted, and the CAA has opened a wider review.7

Airports met a similar problem a year earlier from a supplier. From the evening of 19 September 2025, a cyber attack on the passenger processing software provided by Collins Aerospace hit check-in and bag drop at Heathrow, Brussels and Berlin. Staff used handwritten boarding passes, and Brussels asked airlines to cancel about half of one day's departures.8 ENISA confirmed it was ransomware, and the NCA arrested a man in West Sussex on 23 September.9,17

Rail has seen the same pattern with power. On 6 August 2026 a power loss of about 90 seconds at the Manchester Rail Operating Centre took down signalling when backup did not start in time, halting trains across the North West into a second day.15 On 30 December 2025 a power problem in the Channel Tunnel suspended Eurostar services between London, Paris, Brussels and Amsterdam.18

In each case the operator facing passengers was not the one whose system failed. That is the pattern: transport depends on shared systems, and the first hour depends on how fast the people who rely on them are told.

Supply chains

One attack, a whole supply chain

A customer's shutdown stops your work and your cash flow without warning.

Jaguar Land Rover shut down production in early September 2025 after a cyber incident. It began a phased restart on 8 October, starting with engine and battery assembly in the West Midlands, then stamping and vehicle lines in Solihull and Nitra.13 Production returned to normal levels only by mid-November, and vehicles then took extra time to reach markets.19

The effects ran well beyond JLR. On 28 September the government announced a loan guarantee backing up to £1.5bn of lending, provided through a commercial bank and backed by UK Export Finance, to help JLR support its suppliers.14 JLR introduced financing to pay qualifying suppliers early, speeding payment by up to 120 days, starting with those critical to the restart.13

The Cyber Monitoring Centre estimated the UK cost at £1.9bn, within a modelled range of £1.6bn to £2.1bn, and said more than 5,000 UK organisations were affected. It described how disruption spread from suppliers to transport and retail, with a little over half of the modelled cost falling on JLR and the rest on its supply chain.10

JLR's own accounts show the direct cost. It recorded £196m of cyber incident costs in the quarter to September 2025 and a further £64m in the quarter to December.19,20

For logistics firms, the lesson is two-sided. A customer's shutdown stops your work and your cash flow without warning. Your own outage can do the same to every haulier, warehouse and supplier that depends on you. Both need a plan: who you call, what you can keep doing, and how long your partners can survive a stop.

UK relevance

What it means for UK organisations in Transport and Logistics

Plan on the basis that a long power loss is yours to manage.

Operators of essential transport services already have a duty under the NIS Regulations 2018 to notify their regulator of any incident with a significant impact on the continuity of the service, without undue delay and no later than 72 hours after becoming aware of it.21 The Cyber Security and Resilience Bill, still before Parliament at the time of writing, would add a light-touch initial notification within 24 hours, followed by a full report.22

On power, there is no general safety net. NESO found that critical national infrastructure has no priority within the electricity legal or regulatory framework, and the government accepted there is currently no cross-sector requirement on CNI operators to ensure continuity during power disruption.1,16 Its action plan includes business continuity guidelines for CNI and a review of how CNI customers are treated in restoration, but operators should plan on the basis that a long power loss is theirs to manage.16

Weather adds to the load every winter. When Storm Éowyn brought a red wind warning to central Scotland on 24 January 2025, officials warned of lengthy delays, bridge closures and cancelled rail, ferry and air services, and police advised people to avoid travel in the red warning area.23

People remain the first priority. HSE says employers must monitor lone workers and keep in touch with them, test emergency procedures regularly and use devices for raising the alarm where needed.24 Transport and storage recorded 15 worker deaths in 2025/26 in HSE's provisional figures, which do not include deaths in road traffic accidents.25

The practical test is simple. Could you name every single point of failure in your operation, say how long each backup lasts, reach every key person and partner within ten minutes at night, and show a regulator a timed record of what you did? If not, that is the first job.

Recommendations

Seven steps for UK transport and logistics leaders

In priority order. The first four can start this week.
01

Map single points of failure

Power, control rooms, systems and suppliers, and what each one feeds.1
02

Test backup for duration

Run fallbacks for as long as a real outage might last.3
03

Give every key person two routes

App, call and SMS, with escalation on no response.2
04

Tell partners first

Agree routes and timings with suppliers and partners.4,5

Run manual procedures for a shift; agree incident terms.8,13

Glossary

Terms used in this report

NESO
The National Energy System Operator, which reviewed the North Hyde outage for government.
NATS
The UK's main air navigation service provider, which runs en route air traffic control.
CAA
The Civil Aviation Authority, the UK aviation regulator.
Supply point
A connection to the electricity network. Heathrow has three.
Bushing
An insulated part that carries a high voltage conductor through a transformer casing.
CNI
Critical national infrastructure: assets whose loss would have a major impact on essential services.
Flight plan processing
The system that checks flight plans and passes them to air traffic controllers.
Ransomware
Malware that locks or steals data and demands payment.
Category 3 event
The Cyber Monitoring Centre's rating for a systemic event, on its five-point scale.
NIS Regulations
The Network and Information Systems Regulations 2018, covering essential services including transport.
Lone worker
Anyone who works without close or direct supervision.
Second means of contact
A separate route to reach a key person if the first alert is missed.
