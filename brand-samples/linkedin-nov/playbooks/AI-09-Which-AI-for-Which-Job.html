Foreword

The hard part is no longer finding an AI tool. It is choosing well.

Every software product now claims AI features, and new tools appear every week. General assistants, assistants built into office suites, specialist tools for every function, platforms for building your own. The choice is wide and the sales pitches sound alike.

Choosing badly is expensive in quiet ways. Overlapping licences, data spread across tools nobody approved, staff confused about which tool to use for what, and contracts that renew before anyone has checked whether they are delivering.

This playbook gives you a simple, repeatable way to decide. Start from the job, test tools on your own work, check the terms that protect your data and keep the estate small enough to manage. The specific products will keep changing. The method will not.

The market

Five kinds of AI tool, and why the distinction matters

Start with one well-governed general assistant. Add specialists only where they clearly beat it on a specific job.

The AI market looks crowded, but most products fall into one of five categories. Knowing which category you are buying from tells you a lot about what to expect, and what to ask.

General assistants such as Claude, ChatGPT and Gemini are broad, capable tools for writing, analysis, research and coding. Business editions add admin controls, data protections and connections to everyday tools. They suit a wide range of roles and are often the best first purchase.

Suite assistants such as Microsoft 365 Copilot and Gemini for Google Workspace live inside the email, documents and meetings your people already use. Their strength is context: they can work with your files and messages under your existing permissions. That makes good permissions hygiene essential before you switch them on.

Specialist tools focus on one job, such as coding, meeting notes, writing, service desk or recruitment. They can be excellent at that job and fit neatly into a team's workflow, but each one adds a contract, a data flow and something else to manage.

APIs and platforms let you build AI into your own products and processes, from a customer-facing assistant to an internal agent. Open models, whose weights are published for anyone to run, give you more control over where data goes, at the cost of more engineering and operational responsibility.

The lines between categories are blurring. General assistants are adding connectors to email and documents, suites are adding agent features, and many specialist tools run on models from the same few providers. Focus on the job and the controls rather than the label: what will this tool do for us, with which data, under which terms, and who will manage it?

Most organisations end up with a small mix: one general assistant, perhaps a suite assistant, a handful of specialist tools where they clearly earn their place, and a platform for anything you build. The skill is keeping that mix deliberate, and keeping your options open. Prefer tools that let you export your data and configurations, so switching later stays practical.

Data protection and training terms

The terms behind the tool decide where your data goes. They differ between consumer and business editions, and they change, so always check the current version.
Training use
Confirm in the contract whether your prompts, files and outputs can be used to train or improve models, and what the default is.
Location and processors
Know where data is processed and stored, which sub-processors are involved, and how international transfers are covered.
Retention and deletion
Check how long prompts, outputs and files are kept, whether you can shorten it and how deletion is confirmed.
Certifications and DPIA
Ask for independent security certifications and the information you need for your own data protection impact assessment.
Owner
Your data protection lead, with IT and procurement
Bring this to the monthly review, with a date for each action.

Tool sprawl

Fewer tools, used well, beat many tools used a little

Make the approved route the easy route. It is the most effective control you have.

Tool sprawl creeps in quietly. A team buys a meeting note-taker on a credit card, another signs up for a writing tool, a third starts using a free coding assistant. Each choice makes sense locally. Together they create overlapping costs, scattered data and no clear answer to the question of which tool to use for what.

The fix is not to ban everything. It is to make the approved route the easy route. Publish a short list of approved tools, say what each one is for and make access quick. When people can get a good tool in a day, far fewer go looking elsewhere.

Give every new request a light but real review. Can an existing tool already do this job well enough? If not, does the new tool pass the data protection gate? Who owns it, and when will it be reviewed? A simple form and a regular decision meeting are usually enough. Keep a register of every approved tool, its owner, the data it may hold and its renewal date.

Watch for AI features arriving inside software you already own. Vendors regularly add AI capabilities to existing products, sometimes switched on by default, sometimes at extra cost. Each deserves the same quick check as a new tool: what data does it use, where is it processed, and is it worth paying for when you already have an approved assistant?

Contracts deserve the same discipline. AI products change fast, so prefer terms that let you adjust as you learn: shorter initial commitments, the ability to change licence numbers, clear usage caps for anything billed by consumption, and exit terms that let you take your data with you.

Finally, review the estate regularly. Look at usage, cost and value for every tool. Retire what nobody uses, consolidate where two tools do the same job and renegotiate where usage has grown. A small, well-used estate is cheaper, safer and easier to support. Share what you decide and why, so teams understand the choices.

Glossary

Words worth knowing

General assistant
A broad AI tool such as Claude, ChatGPT or Gemini, used for many kinds of task.
Suite assistant
An AI assistant built into an office suite, working with your email, files and meetings.
Specialist tool
An AI product focused on one job, such as coding, meeting notes or service desk.
API
A way for software to call an AI model directly, usually billed by usage.
Open model
A model whose weights are published, so you can run it on infrastructure you control.
Token
A small unit of text that AI providers often use to measure and price usage.
SSO
Single sign-on: staff use their work identity to log in, under central control.
Retention
How long a provider keeps your prompts, files and outputs.
Sub-processor
A third party a vendor uses to process your data on its behalf.
DPIA
Data protection impact assessment, used to assess risks from processing personal data.
Tool sprawl
Many overlapping tools adopted piecemeal, with scattered costs and data.
