Executive summary

Attackers no longer need to break in. Increasingly, they log in through the firewall.

On 6 October 2026 the FBI and the US Secret Service warned that FortiBleed, a credential-compromise campaign against internet-facing Fortinet FortiGate firewalls and SSL VPN gateways, was still active months after it was first exposed.1 The advisory cites SOCRadar's count of more than 86,644 compromised devices across 194 countries, and warns that some victims have been locked out of their own firewalls.1,2

FortiBleed is not a new software flaw. Fortinet's own analysis is that attackers reused credentials from earlier incidents and brute-forced devices with weak passwords and no multi-factor authentication.3 The same month, Volexity found a previously unknown group chaining three zero-days to gain root on fully patched Ivanti Connect Secure VPN appliances, and US authorities flagged exploited flaws in Citrix NetScaler and Palo Alto Networks PAN-OS.4,5,6

Two routes, one destination. Whether through stolen passwords or unpatched code, the device at the edge of the network is now where serious intrusions start. Verizon's 2026 breach report finds vulnerability exploitation is now the most common initial access vector, at 31% of breaches.7 The NCSC has been urging UK organisations to inventory, patch and replace their edge devices throughout 2026.8,9

This report sets out the evidence, explains the mechanics in plain terms, separates firm figures from preliminary ones and ends with practical recommendations for UK boards and IT leaders.

The bottom line
Treat every firewall, VPN and gateway as a high-value system in its own right: inventoried, patched within days, protected by phishing-resistant MFA, managed off the internet, logged centrally and replaced before it goes out of support.

The campaign

A credential operation, not an exploit

A patch cannot fix a password that has already leaked.

FortiBleed is the name researchers gave to an access-broker operation targeting Fortinet FortiGate firewalls and SSL VPN gateways. Its inner workings became visible only because the operators left their own back-end server open, exposing tooling, datasets and target lists.1,11

The FBI describes a mature, multi-stage campaign. Attackers scanned the internet for exposed FortiGate portals, then tried credentials from earlier Fortinet leak dumps and infostealer logs. Password hashes taken from compromised devices were cracked on a rented GPU cluster, turning stored data into working passwords.1

Fortinet has been clear that this is not a new vulnerability. Its PSIRT says the actors are reusing credentials from previous incidents and brute-forcing devices with weak password hygiene and no MFA.3 The FBI adds that legacy SHA-256 password storage made cracking easier, which is why it recommends the stronger PBKDF2 method on supported FortiOS versions.1

That distinction matters. A patch cannot fix a password that has already leaked. Huntress researchers noted that attackers often logged in on the first attempt, so no failed-login alerts fired.15 To the device, the intruder looked like an administrator.

Once inside, the operators created new administrator accounts, enumerated Active Directory and sprayed passwords to find privileged users.1 Some victims found original accounts deleted or changed, locking them out of their own firewalls, which means recovery can take more than patching and password resets.1,16 The end product was access, packaged and sold: the FBI says brokers using the chain supplied INC/Lynx and Payload ransomware affiliates.1

Attribution remains unsettled. Reporting has linked the operators to Russian-speaking actors, but CloudSEK found mixed-language artefacts and called the origin unresolved.11,17 We do not attribute the campaign to any state.

How it works

Four stages from scan to sale

The FortiBleed chain, as described by the FBI and researchers who examined the operators' own server.
01
Find the door
Automated scans locate internet-facing FortiGate SSL VPN portals and exposed management interfaces.1
02
Try the keys
Credential stuffing and password spraying use leaked passwords and infostealer logs. Hashes from configuration exports are cracked offline on rented GPUs.1,11
03
Move in quietly
New admin accounts give persistence. On some networks, sniffers captured further authentication traffic for cracking.1,11
04
Sell the access
Validated targets are ranked by revenue and sold on, including to ransomware affiliates.1

The pattern

October: the month the edge gave way

Different vendors, different flaws, different actors. The common factor is location.

FortiBleed did not happen in isolation. In the first week of October, Volexity was investigating a customer incident and found a previously unknown actor, which it tracks as UTA0576, compromising an Ivanti Connect Secure VPN appliance that was fully patched at the time.4

The attacker chained three flaws to gain root-level code execution without logging in. It deployed no malware and no web shell. Instead it re-ran the exploit for every command and deleted log entries, and Ivanti's Integrity Checker Tool did not detect the changes.4 The goal appeared to be credentials: service account passwords, local administrator passwords and BitLocker recovery keys.4

Ivanti patched the first flaw, CVE-2026-104935, on 13 October and said a limited number of customers were affected.14 On 29 October it added CVE-2026-111357 and CVE-2026-118527 to the advisory, with fixed releases 25.1.1.3 and 22.8R2.6. Customers who had already patched once had to upgrade again, and CISA set US federal agencies deadlines of 2 and 13 November.13,18

Other vendors were in the same position. CISA listed an exploited Citrix NetScaler flaw on 4 October, and on 14 October added a Palo Alto Networks PAN-OS management interface flaw and a Cisco Secure Email Gateway flaw, giving US federal agencies three days to fix them.5,6 Each vendor issued fixes or guidance; Palo Alto described exploitation as very limited.6

The UK has seen the same pattern. In July the NCSC and 17 partner agencies warned that a unit of Russia's FSB was targeting routers with weak SNMP settings and known Cisco flaws.19 In August the NCSC warned of a wider pattern of disruptive activity against internet-exposed systems and edge devices.9

Different vendors, different flaws, different actors. The common factor is location: every one of these devices sits on the boundary, reachable from the internet and trusted by the network behind it.

Why the edge

Why attackers start at the edge

The boundary is exposed by design, and attackers have industrialised finding the weak ones.

Edge devices are the most valuable and the least watched systems most organisations own. A firewall or VPN gateway is, by design, reachable from the internet. It holds credentials, sees authentication traffic and is trusted by everything behind it. Compromise one, and the attacker is already past the front door.1,4

They are also hard to see into. Many appliances cannot run the endpoint detection tools that protect laptops and servers. The Ivanti intrusion was found by network monitoring, not on the device, and it evaded the vendor's integrity checker.4 In 2025 the NCSC and Five Eyes partners published guidance asking manufacturers to build in better logging and forensic visibility for exactly this reason.21

Patching is slower than the threat. Verizon's 2026 report found only 26% of known exploited vulnerabilities were fully remediated, and the median time to fix rose to 43 days.7 A year earlier, Verizon had reported that edge devices and VPNs made up 22% of vulnerability exploitation actions, up from 3%.22 Network assets such as VPNs, firewalls and routers rose to 5% of breaches where the asset was known, partly because of a change in how Verizon codes remote access cases.7

Smaller organisations feel it most. Among SMB ransomware cases, Verizon found compromised credentials in 38% and unpatched edge devices in 29%.7 These attacks are opportunistic: scanners find whatever is exposed.

Finally, edge devices age quietly. A firewall that works is rarely replaced until it fails, and end-of-life kit gets no fixes at all. The NCSC's advice to retire end-of-life equipment is aimed squarely at this.9

None of this is a criticism of any one vendor. Every major edge vendor has issued urgent fixes in the past year. It is a structural problem: the boundary is exposed by design, and attackers have industrialised the work of finding the weak ones.

UK relevance

What it means for UK organisations

Could you list every device you expose to the internet today? If not, that is the first job.

The NCSC issued its own FortiBleed advice on 18 June 2026, noting some indications of potential impact in the UK.8 UK press reporting on the leaked dataset said it appeared to include logins linked to government departments, councils and NHS organisations, though that reporting was based on the alleged dataset, not confirmed breaches.23

The NCSC's guidance goes further than a password reset. It tells organisations to confirm every Fortinet device is theirs, check for unauthorised accounts, isolate any compromised device, report the incident, collect logs and then factory reset, because changing credentials alone may not remove an attacker.8 It also asks organisations to check other edge devices that share the same credentials.8

The wider message from the NCSC is consistent. Its August notice asks every organisation to keep an accurate inventory of internet-facing systems, understand what each edge device does, apply updates promptly, retire end-of-life equipment and monitor for unexpected configuration changes.9

The NCSC's Annual Review, published on 3 November 2026, records 80 nationally significant incidents in the year to August, and says many state-linked incidents involved vulnerabilities for which patches were available but had not been deployed quickly enough.24

For UK organisations, free help exists. The NCSC Early Warning service alerts registered organisations to exposed vulnerabilities and signs of compromise.25 Cyber Essentials covers firewalls, secure configuration and patching as a baseline, and the NCSC's zero trust principles describe how to reduce reliance on the network boundary.9,26

The practical test is simple. Could you list every device you expose to the internet today, who manages it, what version it runs and when it goes out of support? If not, that is the first job.

Recommendations

Seven steps for UK organisations

In priority order. The first four can start this week.
01

Inventory the edge

List every firewall, VPN and gateway with owner, version and support date. Compare with an external view.9,25
02

Take management off the internet

Admin access from trusted hosts only. Remove internet administration where possible.1,3
03

Reset and harden credentials

End sessions, reset admin and VPN passwords, remove unknown accounts and API keys.1,12
04

Enforce phishing-resistant MFA

On every VPN and admin login, including service accounts.1,8
05

Patch exploited edge flaws in days

Track advisories and the KEV catalog, then verify and check for compromise.7,13
06

Log centrally and watch

Send logs off the device and alert on new accounts or changes.1,9
07

Plan for the bad day, and shrink the edge

Keep offline admin access and a tested rebuild. Retire end-of-life kit and move users to zero trust access.8,9,12,26

Glossary

Terms used in this report

Edge device
Hardware or software at the network boundary, such as a firewall, VPN gateway, router or email gateway.
SSL VPN
A remote access service that lets staff connect to the internal network through a web browser or client.
Credential stuffing
Trying usernames and passwords leaked from other breaches against a login page.
Password spraying
Trying a few common passwords against many accounts to avoid lockouts.
Infostealer
Malware that steals saved passwords and session cookies from infected computers.
PBKDF2
A password storage method designed to make cracking stolen hashes slow and costly.
Initial access broker
A criminal who breaks into networks and sells that access to others, often ransomware groups.
Zero-day
A flaw exploited before the vendor has a fix available.
KEV catalog
CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities list, a useful priority signal for patching.
Zero trust
An approach that authenticates every request rather than trusting anything inside the network.
