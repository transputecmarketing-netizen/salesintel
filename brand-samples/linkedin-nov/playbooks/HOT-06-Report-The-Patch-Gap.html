Executive summary

The fix and the attack now arrive in the same week. Sometimes the attack comes first.

On 13 October 2026 Microsoft shipped the largest monthly security release in its history: between 1,407 and 1,413 CVEs, depending on how they are counted, including a Windows Shell flaw already being exploited.1,2 CISA added it to its exploited list the same day and gave US federal agencies three days to fix it.3

Eight days later the PHP project released emergency fixes for two critical flaws, known as PHP2Shell, in the language behind a large share of the web.4 Amazon's honeypots had logged an exploitation attempt four days before disclosure.5 In the UK, the technology minister told the House of Lords that 32 government services were unavailable, largely as a precaution.6 Meanwhile Oracle's October update fixed an unauthenticated SQL injection in E-Business Suite, and decoy servers recorded exploit attempts within a week.7,8

These are not isolated events. Mandiant estimates the mean time to exploit is now negative: on average, exploitation starts before a patch exists.9 VulnCheck found that almost a quarter of known exploited vulnerabilities in the first half of 2026 showed exploitation on or before the day the CVE was published.10

AI is speeding up discovery on both sides. It is also why the volume of fixes is climbing. Monthly patching, tested at leisure, can no longer keep up with the threat.

The bottom line
Patching is no longer a monthly chore. For anything exploited or internet-facing, it is an incident response activity measured in hours and days, and boards should ask for the numbers that prove it.

The October wave

A record month, and one flaw already in use

Out of more than 1,400 fixes, one was already being used. Finding it fast was the whole job.

Microsoft's October 2026 security release was the largest it has published. Tenable counted 1,407 CVEs across 301 products and components, 69 of them rated Critical.1 SecurityWeek counted 1,413, with 1,102 affecting Windows and 134 affecting Office, and reported that October alone exceeded Microsoft's total for all of 2025.2 The differences come from what each tracker includes, not from any dispute about the scale.

It followed September's release of 974 CVEs, itself a record at the time.11 Microsoft has been clear about why. In July it said advances in AI were changing the pace of vulnerability discovery and that customers "will see a higher volume of security updates" in each release.12

Among the 1,400-plus fixes, one mattered more than the rest. CVE-2026-96951 is a Windows Shell security feature bypass, rated 8.8, that lets an attacker get around Mark of the Web, the warning Windows shows on downloaded files.19 It needs a user to click a link or open a file, which is exactly what phishing is designed to achieve.

CISA added it to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalogue on 13 October, the day of release, and set US federal agencies a deadline of 16 October.3 No details of the attacks have been published.2

Microsoft recognised that volume is now a problem in itself. Alongside the release it published an approach called risk cohorts, grouping CVEs that need a similar assessment of the customer's environment. It noted that this "may become more useful" as AI-assisted research contributes to discovery, and that cohorts "do not replace careful review of high-severity or actively exploited vulnerabilities".15

The practical point is simple. No organisation can test 1,400 fixes one by one in a fortnight. The ones that coped in October were those that already knew which handful of CVEs mattered to them, and had a fast path for those.

The October wave

Two emergencies in two days

PHP2Shell was attacked before it was announced. The Oracle flaw was probed within a week of its fix.

On 20 October the PHP security team gave advance notice of a critical release, and on 21 October it shipped PHP 8.5.12, 8.4.27, 8.3.36 and 8.2.35. The two flaws, CVE-2026-109618 and CVE-2026-109619, are rated 9.2 and can allow unauthenticated remote code execution. PHP's guidance was blunt: "There are no complete mitigations, updating is the only safe choice."4

The flaws sit in PHP itself, so a single unauthenticated request can reach them before any application code runs.20 Wiz, which reported them in August, says they affect releases back to PHP 5.4.14 PHP 8.1 and older receive no official update.4

The embargo did not hold. Amazon recorded an exploitation attempt on 17 October, and proof-of-concept exploits appeared in the early hours of release day. Its advice was to measure fixes for critical internet-facing flaws "in hours, not days or weeks".5 Cloudflare pushed firewall rules to all customers, including free plans, about nine hours before disclosure, and described them as a stopgap.21

CISA added both CVEs to its exploited list on 26 October with a 29 October deadline.3 Tenable reports that the release was brought forward a day after details leaked.22

On 20 October Oracle's quarterly update fixed CVE-2026-101767, a 9.8-rated unauthenticated SQL injection in E-Business Suite 12.2.3 to 12.2.15. Horizon3.ai, which reported it in July, says the vulnerable endpoint is allowed by the default configuration and the flaw can be chained to run commands on the server.7 On 27 October Defused's decoys recorded exploratory attacks. Data at stake includes financial, payroll, HR and procurement records.8

At the time of writing CVE-2026-101767 is not on CISA's exploited list, and the reported activity is probing rather than confirmed compromise.3,23 But Oracle E-Business Suite has been here before: CVE-2025-61882 was exploited as a zero-day in a 2025 extortion campaign.24

The shrinking window

What the evidence says about speed

The question is no longer whether attackers will use a new flaw. It is whether you fix it first.

Time to exploit is hard to measure, and different studies measure different things. Read together, they point the same way.

Mandiant's M-Trends 2026 estimates the mean time to exploit has fallen to about minus seven days, meaning exploitation is routinely starting before a patch is released. Exploits were the most common way into networks for the sixth year running, at 32% of intrusions.9 This is an average. It does not mean every flaw is attacked early.

VulnCheck counted 495 known exploited vulnerabilities in the first half of 2026. Of these, 23.43% showed exploitation on or before the day the CVE was published, slightly down from 28.93% in 2025. Over the same period the median time from CVE publication to evidence of exploitation fell from 120 days to 80.10

Rapid7's third-quarter data puts the average gap between disclosure and exploitation of high-severity flaws at about three days, with 71% of tracked exploitation in the first week. It also found 34 of 43 newly exploited serious flaws needed no login and no user action.25

Regulators have followed the data. In June CISA replaced its old directive with BOD 26-04, which ranks flaws on exposure, known exploitation, whether attacks can be automated and impact. The most urgent tier must be fixed in three days, with a forensic check for compromise.16 The effect shows in the catalogue: most 2024 additions carried a 21-day deadline, while 29 of 36 October 2026 additions carried three days.3

The UK view is consistent. In 2025 the NCSC judged that the time between disclosure and exploitation "has shrunk to days and AI will almost certainly reduce this further".26

The shrinking window

AI on both sides of the patch

AI is finding flaws faster than ever. The patch process has to move at the same speed.

AI is changing the supply of vulnerabilities. Microsoft says it is scanning Windows with AI and expects more fixes per release as a result, calling the volume "evidence that defenders are getting better at identifying and addressing issues".12 The exploited Windows Shell flaw itself was reported by researchers including an AI company.2

PHP2Shell is the clearest case yet. Wiz says it used frontier models in discovery, exploit development and patch validation, and that one researcher did in a week what would once have taken a top team months. Its conclusion: what used to be a barrier to exploitation "is no longer a barrier".14 CyberScoop reports that a second firm, Calif, found one flaw using an OpenAI model.13

The same capability works for attackers. Amazon says AI-assisted analysis "makes it cheaper to turn a patch diff or advisory into a working exploit", one reason more actors can exploit flaws within hours of disclosure.5 Rapid7 found AI cited as an operational component in 8% of the threat reporting it tracked in the third quarter.25

The evidence is not one-sided. VulnCheck found that of 1,061 vulnerabilities attributed to AI-assisted discovery, only 14, or 1.3%, have been confirmed as exploited.10 Mandiant did not consider 2025 the year breaches were the direct result of AI, and says most intrusions still stem from basic failures.9 No source we found confirms that attackers used AI to exploit PHP2Shell.

Two conclusions follow. First, AI will keep raising the number of fixes organisations must handle, so prioritisation has to be automated and ruthless. Second, the cost of turning a fix into an exploit is falling, so the window between them will keep shrinking for the flaws that matter.

Defenders can use the same tools: to summarise advisories, map them to the estate, draft change requests and check logs. The organisations that do will close the gap faster than those that only add people.

Why organisations fall behind

Four reasons the gap stays open

In our experience, organisations rarely fall behind because they do not care. They fall behind for structural reasons.

They do not know what they run

PHP hides inside appliances and other products. CISA and the NCSC both start with finding every copy.17,18

Old software has no fix

PHP 8.1 and older get no official fix. CISA listed 2015 and 2016 CVEs as exploited on 8 October 2026.3,4

The process is built for a month

Change boards, test cycles and maintenance windows assume weeks. An exploited flaw does not wait for the next window.

Nobody owns the number

Few boards see patch latency. Without a measure and an owner, urgent fixes compete with everything else.
The fix for all four is the same: a known estate, a fast lane for urgent flaws and a number someone is accountable for.

UK organisations

A wake-up call that reached Whitehall

Fourteen days is the Cyber Essentials floor. For an exploited, internet-facing flaw, it is far too long.

PHP2Shell was the first patch emergency of 2026 to visibly disrupt UK public services. On 22 October the technology minister told the House of Lords that "32 government services are unavailable and four are partially unavailable", largely as a result of precautionary measures, and that the government was not aware of any confirmed compromise of central government systems. Peers named several councils whose services were reported offline.6

The NCSC's guidance set a clear order: find every PHP installation, internet-facing first; update; apply temporary mitigations; take systems offline if mitigations are not possible in time; and check for compromise if you cannot do any of these. It also asked organisations to track suppliers whose products bundle PHP.17 The Register reports the NCSC issued 50,000 warnings to UK organisations.6

Taking services offline was a reasonable precaution. It also showed how many organisations could not quickly say where PHP ran, which version it was, or who could patch it. That is an inventory problem before it is a patching problem.

For most UK organisations the reference point is Cyber Essentials. Version 3.3, in force from April 2026, requires critical and high-risk updates to be installed within 14 days of release and unsupported software to be removed or isolated.27 That remains the baseline. For exploited, internet-facing flaws, October showed it is a ceiling, not a target.

The NCSC has also warned of a "digital divide" between systems that keep pace with AI-enabled threats and those that do not.26 Smaller organisations relying on a managed service provider should check that their contract includes an emergency route, not just monthly patching.

The good news is that the steps are well understood. Inventory, prioritise by exposure and exploitation, run an emergency process, handle legacy honestly, and measure the result.

Recommendations

Seven steps to close the gap

Practical actions for IT leaders, in priority order.
01

Know what you run

Keep a live inventory of software and versions, including PHP inside appliances and bundled products.18
02

Prioritise by exploitation and exposure

Track CISA's exploited list and NCSC alerts daily. Exploited and internet-facing comes first.16
03

Set patch targets in days

Agree targets by risk tier: hours to days for exploited and exposed, 14 days at most for critical and high.
04

Build an emergency lane

A pre-approved process to test, deploy, mitigate or isolate within hours, without waiting for the change board.
05

Retire or ring-fence legacy

Upgrade unsupported software, or isolate it. Cyber Essentials already requires this.27
06

Hunt after you patch

Look back to the earliest known attack date for signs of compromise.5
07

Measure and report latency

Report time to patch exploited and critical flaws to the board every month.

Glossary

Words worth knowing

CVE
Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures. A unique ID for a publicly known flaw.
CVSS
A 0 to 10 severity score. Useful, but it does not say whether a flaw is being exploited.
KEV catalogue
CISA's list of vulnerabilities known to be exploited, with federal fix deadlines.
Patch Tuesday
Microsoft's monthly security release, on the second Tuesday of the month.
Critical Patch Update
Oracle's quarterly security release.
Zero-day
A flaw exploited before the vendor has released a fix.
Time to exploit
The gap between a fix or disclosure and the first exploitation.
Embargo
The period when a flaw is known privately but not yet public.
Mark of the Web
A Windows tag on downloaded files that triggers security warnings.
SQL injection
Tricking an application into running an attacker's database commands.
Compensating control
A measure that reduces risk when a patch cannot be applied yet.
End of life
Software that no longer receives security fixes from its maker.
