Executive summary

The biggest theft in crypto history started, by the victim's own early account, with one engineer's laptop.

On 29 October 2026, attackers drained Binance hot wallets across several blockchains. Binance put the loss at about $6.3 billion, a figure it described as preliminary and unaudited, and said its initial investigation suggested a compromised DevOps engineer's laptop had given the attacker a way in.1 Blockchain analysts put the total slightly higher, at about $6.4 billion.2,3

On 31 October the FBI attributed the theft to North Korea and named the group it calls TraderTraitor.4 The same name appears in FBI attributions for the Bybit theft in 2025 and the DMM Bitcoin theft in 2024, and in a 2022 US advisory that described fake recruitment messages sent to DevOps and system administration staff at crypto firms.

How the Binance laptop was compromised has not been disclosed. Nobody official has said it was a fake job offer, and we do not claim it was. But the wider pattern is well documented: North Korean groups use fake recruiters, malicious coding tests and fake remote workers to get inside the organisations that hold money and code.

For UK businesses the risk is not limited to crypto. Any firm that hires remote developers or contractors can end up paying a North Korean IT worker, and the UK sanctions regime can treat that as a breach even when the employer did not know.

The bottom line
Treat hiring, contractor onboarding and developer laptops as part of your security perimeter. The most expensive breaches now begin with a conversation, not an exploit.

What happened

What we know, and what we do not

The attacker did not need to steal the keys. It made the exchange's own systems sign for it.

The facts that are firm are few but significant. Funds began leaving Binance hot wallets on the evening of 29 October and moved across several chains within minutes.14 TRM Labs said the theft took about 20 minutes.16 Binance suspended withdrawals, kept deposits running and said users would not bear the loss.

The figures are not firm. Binance's estimate of about $6.3 billion is, in its own words, preliminary and unaudited.1 Chainalysis reported about $6.4 billion, and Elliptic $6.43 billion across 11 blockchains, after excluding money it believes Binance moved itself.2,3 Expect revisions as tokens are repriced and frozen funds are released or recovered.

The cause is less firm still. Binance's first update said the incident may involve a compromised DevOps engineer's laptop, which let an attacker reach a service container and submit withdrawal transactions.1 Its co-chief executive later said the investigation had found additional points of breach and that the attack was not carried out by one person.3 A full post-mortem has been promised.

What matters for everyone else is the shape of the attack. Binance said it found no evidence that wallet private keys leaked.1 Chainalysis described signed authorisations that let a hot wallet run code the attacker had deployed.2 In plain terms, the attacker appears to have made Binance's own systems approve the transfers.

Attribution came quickly from analysts and then from the FBI. TRM Labs and Chainalysis linked the theft to TraderTraitor within hours; the FBI followed on 31 October.16,2,4 North Korea denies it.5 The US President also said, on the same day, that it was not yet certain.15

We report the FBI's attribution because it is the formal position of the investigating agency, and we label the rest as analyst assessment. What nobody has reported is how the engineer's laptop was compromised. That gap is exactly where the rest of this report sits.

Motive and method

Why crypto, and why developers

Developers are the shortest path to the money, and a job offer is the shortest path to a developer.

North Korea treats crypto theft as a revenue stream. Chainalysis estimated that North Korean actors stole at least $2.02 billion in 2025, a record before this year.17 A monitoring group of 11 states, including the UK, found at least $1.65 billion stolen between January and September 2025 and linked the proceeds to weapons programmes.18

Crypto suits a sanctioned state. Assets move across borders in minutes, some cannot be frozen, and a single breach of an exchange can yield more than years of conventional fraud. The incentive is enormous and the pool of targets is global.

Developers are the shortest path to the money. The people who build and operate wallet infrastructure have access to signing systems, cloud consoles and deployment pipelines. As early as 2022, US agencies warned that TraderTraitor sent recruitment-themed messages to DevOps and system administration staff at crypto firms, offering high-paying jobs to get them to install trojanised apps.19

The 2024 DMM Bitcoin case shows the method end to end. Investigators said a fake LinkedIn recruiter sent an employee at a Japanese wallet software firm a malicious Python script dressed as a pre-employment test. Weeks later the attackers used stolen session cookies to manipulate a legitimate transaction.7

The FBI's 2024 warning to the industry was blunt: these campaigns are highly tailored and hard to detect, often start with research on professional networking sites, and frequently ask the target to run code or download an app.20

The lesson is that the attack surface is a person's curiosity and ambition, not a firewall. Good engineers like hard problems and better jobs. The attackers know it.

The fake IT worker

Hired, paid and inside the network

A fake candidate is not only an HR problem. It can be the first stage of an intrusion.

The second route needs no malware at all. North Korea sends skilled IT workers abroad who pose as nationals of other countries and win remote jobs and freelance contracts. US Treasury said in March 2026 that these schemes generated nearly $800 million in 2024, with the regime taking most of the wages.9

Laptop farms make the deception work. A facilitator in the target country receives company laptops and lets the workers control them remotely, so the IP address looks local. In June 2025 the US Justice Department announced searches of 29 known or suspected laptop farms across 16 states and said the workers had obtained jobs at more than 100 US companies.21

Once inside, some workers do more than draw a salary. The FBI warned in January 2025 that workers who are discovered have held stolen code and data to ransom and in some cases published it.22 The 2025 US cases also alleged theft of more than $900,000 in crypto and access to export-controlled defence data.21

The scheme is not only American. Google's threat intelligence team reported in April 2025 that the operation had expanded into Europe, found workers on UK projects, and found a laptop meant for New York operating in London.23

In July 2026, 11 governments including the UK issued a joint alert noting that the workers increasingly use AI to hide their identities.24 The September 2026 advisory recorded face-swapping software in interviews and said WaterPlum actors and IT workers used the same IP addresses.8

The overlap matters. A fake candidate is not only a fraud risk for HR. It can be the first stage of the same kind of intrusion that hit Binance.

Sanctions and liability

You can breach sanctions without knowing it

Due diligence on remote hires is now a sanctions control, not just an HR process.

HM Treasury's Office of Financial Sanctions Implementation (OFSI) published an advisory on North Korean IT workers in September 2024. It assessed that it is almost certain UK firms are being targeted by workers disguised as freelance third-country IT staff.10

The legal point is sharp. OFSI says people who employ or pay these workers could be directly or indirectly breaching financial sanctions. Civil monetary penalties can be imposed with no requirement for OFSI to show the person knew or suspected the conduct was a breach. A criminal breach can carry up to seven years in prison.10

OFSI names the sectors most exposed: IT, cryptocurrency, electronic money institutions, money service businesses and professional services.10 That covers software houses, agencies, fintechs and any business that buys development work through platforms.

Reporting duties apply too. OFSI asks firms to report suspected targeting, and businesses in the regulated sector must still make suspicious activity reports to the National Crime Agency. OFSI asks them to quote the reference "OFSI - DPRK IT Workers Advisory - 0924".10

The UK has also been part of the wider response. It joined the 11-government alert on IT workers in July 2026, sits on the monitoring team that reported on North Korean cyber theft in 2025, and the NCSC co-sealed a 2024 advisory on another North Korean group targeting defence and engineering firms.24,18,25

This is not legal advice, and outcomes depend on facts. But the practical message is clear: due diligence on remote workers and contractors is now a sanctions control, not only an HR process. Document it.

Where you are exposed

Four ways this reaches a UK business

You do not need to hold crypto to be a target.

Remote hiring

Developers, designers and support staff hired remotely or through freelance platforms can be fake personas run by North Korean workers.

Developer devices

A personal coding test run on a work laptop can hand an attacker credentials, session tokens and access to repositories.

Your supply chain

Agencies, outsourcers and subcontractors may unknowingly place a fake worker on your account.

Keys and payments

Anyone holding signing keys, treasury access or payment approval rights is a high-value target for both routes.
If someone you have never met in person can deploy code or approve payments, treat that role as high risk.

Recommendations

Seven things to do now

Based on guidance from OFSI, the FBI and the September 2026 joint advisory.
01

Verify identity properly, and keep verifying

Live video, document checks and consistent details across profiles, re-checked after hire.10,22
02

Ban personal coding tests on work devices

Never on company laptops; use a separate, disposable environment.20
03

Lock down developer tooling

Open unknown VS Code projects in Restricted Mode, review package scripts and deploy endpoint detection.8
04

Separate who can approve money from who can deploy code

Multiple approvals from separate devices for any movement of funds or keys.
05

Control contractor access tightly

Least privilege, no commercial VPNs or unapproved remote desktop tools.
06

Flag payment and shipping anomalies

Crypto pay, third-party accounts or new shipping addresses are red flags.
07

Report and record

Report to OFSI, the NCSC or Action Fraud as appropriate, and keep records.

Glossary

Terms used in this report

TraderTraitor
The FBI's name for a North Korean state-sponsored group focused on stealing crypto from exchanges and other firms.
WaterPlum / Contagious Interview
A North Korean group that uses fake job interviews and coding tests to infect developers' devices.
Hot wallet
A crypto wallet connected to the internet so it can process transactions quickly.
Laptop farm
A location where company laptops are kept online so remote workers can control them and appear local.
OFSI
HM Treasury's Office of Financial Sanctions Implementation, which enforces UK financial sanctions.
Stablecoin
A crypto token pegged to a currency. Issuers can often freeze them, which is why attackers swap them quickly.
EIP-7702
An Ethereum feature that lets an account delegate execution to code. Binance said its use bypassed normal approvals.
npm package
A reusable JavaScript code library. Malicious packages are a common way to deliver malware to developers.
Remote access trojan
Malware that gives an attacker remote control of an infected device.
Session token
Data that keeps a user logged in. Stealing it can let an attacker bypass multi-factor authentication.
