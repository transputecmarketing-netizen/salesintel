Executive summary

The high-risk deadline moved. The rest of the Act kept running.

In July 2026 the EU amended its Artificial Intelligence Act through the Digital Omnibus on AI, Regulation (EU) 2026/1744.1 The main obligations for high-risk AI systems in Annex III uses such as recruitment, credit and education now apply from 2 December 2027 instead of 2 August 2026. Those for AI in regulated products move to 2 August 2028.1

Much of the Act did not move. Prohibited practices have applied since February 2025, rules for general-purpose AI models since August 2025, and the Article 50 transparency duties since 2 August 2026.2,3 The date many reports give for transparency, 2 December 2026, is narrower than it sounds: it ends a grace period for machine-readable marking by generative AI systems already on the market before August 2026, and it is the start date of new bans on AI that creates non-consensual intimate images or child sexual abuse material.1

UK organisations are in scope when they place AI on the EU market or when their AI's output is used in the EU.2 The UK has chosen a different, principles-based path and has no AI bill before Parliament.4,5 For many UK firms the Act will arrive through suppliers and customers as much as through regulators.

This report separates adopted law from guidance, drafts and proposals, explains what the change means in practice and sets out recommendations for UK boards and IT leaders.

The bottom line
Use the extra sixteen months to build the inventory, documentation and supplier evidence high-risk compliance needs. Meanwhile, treat the bans, transparency duties and AI literacy as live today, and finish the December 2026 items now.

What changed

Why Brussels moved the clock, and why it fixed the new dates

The adopted text replaced a sliding start date with fixed ones. A known deadline is easier to plan for than a moving one.

The Digital Omnibus on AI is explicit about its reason. The regulation says the late arrival of standards, common specifications and guidance, and the delayed setting up of national competent authorities, put the original 2 August 2026 date for high-risk rules at risk and threatened a significant rise in implementation costs.1

That was a fair description of the position. By mid-2026 no harmonised standard supporting the Act had been cited. The first, EN 18286 on quality management, only reached the Official Journal in October 2026, and it covers one requirement of many.8 Without standards, providers of high-risk systems had no presumption of conformity to work towards.

The Commission's original proposal of November 2025 tried to link the start of high-risk duties to a Commission decision that support measures were available, with long-stop dates of 2 December 2027 and 2 August 2028.11 The adopted text dropped that sliding mechanism and simply fixed the long-stop dates in law.1 For businesses that is more useful: a known date beats a moving one.

The substance of the high-risk regime was not reopened. Risk management, data governance, technical documentation, logging, human oversight, accuracy and robustness, and conformity assessment all remain.3 Deployer duties under Article 26 sit in the same part of the Act, so they move to December 2027 too.1,2

The Omnibus also adjusted the edges. A new category of small mid-cap companies, with fewer than 750 staff, receives SME-style relief including simplified documentation and lower penalty caps.15 Providers and deployers may process special category data to detect and correct bias where strictly necessary, with safeguards. National regulatory sandboxes must be running by 2 August 2027.1

The pattern is familiar from other EU digital laws: legislators protect the political settlement and adjust the timetable when the supporting machinery runs late. The lesson for organisations is to plan to the adopted dates, not to hope for another delay.

What changed

How the grace periods work

The amended Article 111 decides which existing systems must change, and when.1
01
Legacy high-risk systems
High-risk systems placed on the market before the new dates are caught only if their design changes significantly afterwards. The first unit of a type and model sets the date.
02
Public authorities
Existing high-risk systems intended for public authorities must comply by 2 August 2030, even without design changes.
03
Generative content marking
Generative AI systems on the market before 2 August 2026 have until 2 December 2026 to mark output machine-readably under Article 50(2).
04
General-purpose AI models
Models placed on the market before 2 August 2025 must comply by 2 August 2027, under the original Act.2

What did not change

Four obligations that are already live

The December 2026 date ends one narrow grace period. It does not mark the start of transparency.

The first is the list of prohibited practices. Since 2 February 2025 the Act has banned AI used for social scoring, manipulative or exploitative techniques that cause significant harm, untargeted scraping of facial images, and emotion recognition in workplaces and schools, among others.2 Breaches carry the Act's highest fines: up to EUR 35 million or 7% of worldwide turnover.2

The second is the regime for general-purpose AI models. Providers have had obligations on documentation, copyright policy and training content summaries since August 2025, and the Commission's power to fine them became available on 2 August 2026.14 Models already on the market before August 2025 must comply by August 2027.2

The third is transparency. Since 2 August 2026, providers must design chatbots so people know they are dealing with AI, and must mark generated audio, images, video and text in a machine-readable way. Deployers must label deepfakes and AI-generated text published to inform the public on matters of public interest.13 The Commission published final guidelines on 20 July 2026, and its voluntary Code of Practice, with an EU icon set for labels, was finalised in June.6,12

The Article 50(2) grace period is the source of the widely reported December date. It covers only machine-readable marking, and only generative systems already on the market before 2 August 2026.1 Chatbot disclosure and deepfake labelling were not deferred.3

The fourth is AI literacy. The Omnibus softened the wording, but the duty to take measures supporting staff AI literacy still applies to every provider and deployer, whatever their risk tier.1

What most organisations get wrong is to read the headline delay as covering everything. Firms that paused AI governance work after July 2026 may already be behind on transparency, and may not know whether their marketing, customer service or product teams are using AI in ways the Act now governs.

The UK view

Outside the EU, but not outside the Act

Most UK firms will meet the AI Act through their suppliers and customers before they meet a regulator.

The AI Act follows AI into the EU market. It applies to providers placing AI systems or general-purpose models on the EU market wherever they are established, and to providers and deployers in third countries where the output of their AI system is used in the Union.2 A UK software firm selling an AI feature to EU customers, or a UK recruiter scoring candidates for EU roles, can be inside its scope.

Providers outside the EU that offer high-risk systems must appoint an authorised representative in the Union by written mandate.2 For UK firms that is a contract, a cost and a lead time to plan before December 2027, not after.

Most UK organisations will feel the Act indirectly. The AI products they buy from global vendors are being built to meet it, so instructions for use, content marking and transparency features will arrive whether or not the buyer is in scope. Contracts with EU customers will increasingly ask UK suppliers to confirm their role and their compliance.

The UK has taken a different route. Its 2024 response to the AI regulation white paper set five cross-sector principles for existing regulators to apply within their remits, on a non-statutory footing.4 The January 2025 AI Opportunities Action Plan focused on growth and adoption.16 The King's Speech of May 2026 contained no AI bill.5

That does not leave UK firms without rules. Data protection law already governs much AI use, and the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 reformed the rules on automated decision-making.17 Sector regulators expect firms to manage AI risk within existing duties.

The practical answer for most UK organisations is one governance approach that meets the stricter regime where it applies and good practice everywhere else. Running separate UK and EU programmes for the same tools doubles the effort and invites gaps.

The UK view

Four exposure profiles

Where a UK organisation sits decides how much of the Act applies, and when.

Selling AI into the EU

Provider duties. Transparency now; high-risk from December 2027 with an EU authorised representative; marking by 2 December 2026 for older generative products.

Output used in the EU

Scoring, screening or generating content for EU people or customers. Check whether the use is high-risk and who the provider is.

Operating in the EU

EU subsidiaries and branches are deployers established in the Union, with transparency, literacy and future high-risk duties.

Buying AI products

Not directly in scope for UK-only use, but suppliers' documentation, labels and limits will shape contracts and controls.
Many organisations fit more than one profile. Classify each AI system, not the company as a whole.

Recommendations

Six steps for UK organisations

In order of urgency. Each builds evidence you will need whichever regime applies.
01

Build and own an AI register now

Every system built, bought or used, with role, risk tier, EU link and a named business owner.
02

Close the December 2026 items

Confirm generative suppliers mark content, check for the new prohibited uses, and put deepfake labelling rules in place.
03

Treat high-risk as a 2027 project starting today

Classify borderline cases with legal input, appoint an EU representative if needed and start documentation.
04

Put the Act into supplier contracts

Ask for role, risk tier, instructions for use, marking approach, incident contacts and change notices.
05

Make AI literacy role-based and recorded

Different guidance for recruiters, marketers, developers and approvers, with training records kept.
06

Choose a management system

Use ISO/IEC 42001 as the governance backbone; EN 18286 is built to sit on top of it for high-risk providers.8

Glossary

Terms used in this report

AI Act
Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, the EU's horizontal law on AI systems and general-purpose AI models.
Digital Omnibus on AI
Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, which amended the AI Act's timetable and some duties from July 2026.
Annex III
The list of high-risk use areas, such as employment, education, credit and essential services.
Annex I
EU product safety laws whose products can contain high-risk AI as a safety component.
Provider
The organisation that develops an AI system or model and places it on the market under its own name.
Deployer
An organisation using an AI system under its authority in a professional context.
Article 50
Transparency duties: chatbot disclosure, machine-readable marking, deepfake and public-interest text labelling.
Grace period
Extra time for systems already on the market before a new rule applies, set out in Article 111.
Harmonised standard
A European standard cited in the Official Journal that gives a presumption of conformity.
Small mid-cap
A company that is not an SME, has fewer than 750 staff and meets turnover or balance sheet limits.
