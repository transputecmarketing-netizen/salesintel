Foreword

Most AI business cases fail not because the value is missing, but because nobody measured it.

Boards are being asked to fund AI on the strength of demos, vendor claims and enthusiasm. Some of those investments are paying back handsomely. Others are quietly costing money with little to show. In many organisations, nobody can say which is which.

The problem is rarely the technology. It is that the case was never framed in a way that could be tested: no baseline, no agreed measures, no point at which anyone would say this is not working.

This playbook sets out a plain, honest method. Be specific about the type of value, count all the costs, estimate in ranges, prove it with a well-designed pilot and report it in terms a board recognises. It will not make every project succeed. It will make it clear, early, which ones are.

Introduction

Real value, honestly counted.

A good AI business case is not a big number. It is a clear story about what will change, how you will know and what it will cost to get there, built on assumptions anyone can challenge.

Name the value precisely

Time released, better quality, faster service, more revenue or lower risk. Each is measured differently, so be specific.

Count every cost

Licences are the visible part. Integration, training, change and governance often cost as much again.

Prove it before you scale it

A baseline, a pilot with success criteria agreed in advance and a willingness to stop if the evidence is not there.

The problem

Why so many AI business cases fall apart

If the case cannot say what will be different, for whom, and how you will see it, it is not a case yet.

Most weak AI business cases follow the same pattern. Someone estimates how many minutes a tool saves per person per day, multiplies by headcount and salary, and arrives at an impressive annual figure. The licences look cheap by comparison, and the case is approved.

Twelve months later, nobody can find the savings. Headcount has not changed, costs have not fallen and the finance team is asking awkward questions. The tool may well have helped. But the case promised cash, and released time is not cash unless it is redeployed, removes cost or allows growth without extra hiring.

The second common flaw is ignoring adoption. A business case that assumes everyone uses a tool well from day one is describing an organisation that does not exist. In practice, use builds slowly, varies widely between teams and depends heavily on training and leadership.

The third is leaving out costs. Licences are easy to see. Connecting tools to your systems, preparing data, training people, changing processes and governing the whole thing are less visible, and they are often where projects run over.

None of this means AI value is not real. It means value has to be named precisely, measured against a baseline and tied to a decision about what changes. The rest of this playbook shows how.

The full cost

Four kinds of cost to include

Most cases undercount cost. Include all four, over the full life of the project, not just the first year.

Licences and usage

Per-user subscriptions, plus usage-based charges for APIs and agents, which rise with volume and need monitoring and caps.

Integration and data

Connecting tools to your systems, preparing and cleaning data, and the engineering time to build and maintain it.

People and change

Training, champions' time, process redesign and the dip in productivity while people learn new ways of working.

Governance and security

Policy, risk assessments, access reviews, monitoring and the ongoing effort to keep it all safe and compliant.
A cheap licence on an expensive process change is not a cheap project. Cost the change, not just the tool.

Baselining

Six steps to a baseline you can trust

Without a baseline, every result is an opinion. It need not be elaborate, but it must exist before the tool arrives.
01

Define the task precisely

Name the process, where it starts and ends, and who does it.
02

Measure volume

How many times it happens per week or month, from system data where possible.
03

Measure effort and time

Sample how long it takes and how long customers or colleagues wait.
04

Measure quality

Capture current error, rework or complaint rates.
05

Note the context

Record staffing, seasonality and anything else that could skew comparisons.
06

Agree it with the owner

The process owner signs off the baseline before the pilot starts.
Remember
A rough baseline taken before the pilot is worth far more than a precise one reconstructed afterwards.

Estimating

Ranges, assumptions and the discipline of saying what you do not know

Estimate in ranges. State every assumption. Name the one that matters most.

A single number in a business case invites false confidence. A range invites the right conversation. For each benefit, estimate a cautious, an expected and an optimistic outcome, and write down the assumptions that drive each one.

Start from the baseline. If a task happens V times a month and takes T minutes each time, the current effort is V multiplied by T. The pilot tells you the new time per task, and the share of tasks where the tool is actually used. Those two numbers, not a vendor's claim, drive your estimate.

Then ask the question most cases skip: what happens to the released time? It might absorb growth without new hires, clear a backlog, shorten response times or fund more valuable work. Each of those has a different financial meaning. Only some of them reduce cost, and the case should say which.

Build adoption into the numbers. Use will rise over months, not days, and will differ between teams. A realistic ramp, based on what the pilot showed, protects the case from the most common source of disappointment.

Finally, test the assumptions. Which one, if wrong, would sink the case? Volume, time saved, adoption or cost per task? Name it, and plan to measure it early. Boards trust a case that knows its weak points far more than one that pretends it has none.

Pilot design

A pilot that produces a decision

A pilot is an experiment, not a soft launch. Design it to answer a question, and agree in advance what answer would mean what.

Frame the question

Write down what the pilot must show, for which process, and the decision it will inform.

Set success criteria

Agree the thresholds for scale, adjust or stop before the pilot starts, with the sponsor.

Choose participants

Pick a representative group, not just enthusiasts, and a comparison group where you can.

Run for long enough

Allow time for people to learn and for lagging measures to move, usually more than a few weeks.

Decide and record

Compare results with the criteria, make the call and write down why.
Agree the stop criteria at the start. It is much harder to set them once people are invested.

Reporting to the board

Boards do not need the technology explained. They need to know what was promised, what has happened, what it cost and what you recommend next.
Promise and progress
Restate the original case in one line, then show progress against the agreed measures, leading and lagging.
Cost to date
Show all costs against budget, including usage-based charges, integration and people time, not just licences.
Risks and issues
Report incidents, near misses and any data or compliance concerns, and what has been done about each.
Recommendation
Scale, adjust or stop, with the evidence for it and the next decision point clearly dated.
Owner
The executive sponsor, supported by finance
Bring this to the monthly review, with a date for each action.

Scale, fix or stop

What to do with each AI project

Review each project against two questions: is value showing up, and are people actually using it?
Fix adoption

Value proven, low use

It works where used. Invest in training, champions and process change before buying more.
Scale

Value proven, high use

Expand to more teams, renegotiate on volume and keep measuring.
Stop

Little value, low use

Close it, release the budget and record the lessons for the next project.
Rethink

Little value, high use

People like it but it does not move outcomes. Re-target it or reduce the spend.

Common traps

Six traps that undermine AI business cases

Every one of these is common, and every one is avoidable.
01

Counting minutes as cash

Released time is capacity. It becomes money only through a decision about how it is used.
02

Ignoring adoption

Value scales with real use, which builds slowly and unevenly.
03

Forgetting the hidden costs

Integration, training, change and governance can match the licence bill.
04

No baseline

Without one, results are anecdotes and every claim can be disputed.
05

Pilots that never end

Without success criteria and a decision date, pilots drift indefinitely.
06

Never stopping

Projects that are not working keep consuming budget and goodwill.

Glossary

Words worth knowing

Business case
A structured argument for investment, setting out value, cost, risk and how success will be judged.
Baseline
A measurement of how a process performs before a change is made.
Time released
Effort freed by a change. Capacity, not cash, until it is redeployed or removes cost.
Leading indicator
An early measure that shows whether value is likely, such as active use.
Lagging indicator
A later measure that shows whether value was delivered, such as cost per case.
Adoption ramp
The gradual rise in real use of a tool over time.
Control group
A comparable team not using the tool, used to separate its effect from other changes.
Usage-based pricing
Charges that rise with how much a tool or API is used.
Total cost of ownership
All costs over the life of a solution, not just the purchase price.
Success criteria
Thresholds agreed in advance that decide whether to scale, adjust or stop.
Sponsor
The senior leader accountable for a project delivering its intended value.
