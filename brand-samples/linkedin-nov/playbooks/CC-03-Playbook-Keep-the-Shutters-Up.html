Foreword

Retail crises rarely start at head office. They start at a till, a freezer or a shop door.

In April 2025 a cyber attack forced Marks and Spencer in the UK to pause online orders for 46 days, and the retailer estimated the hit to operating profit at around £300m before mitigation.1,2 In October 2026 an incident at Ahold Delhaize took online grocery services offline in the Benelux and the US; the group expects fourth-quarter sales to be €200m to €250m lower.3

The same month, attackers switched supermarket refrigeration into defrost from the internet. France's CERT-FR reported numerous compromised controllers, and US agencies including CISA and the FBI later warned of ongoing attacks on refrigeration and cold chain monitoring systems.4,5 On the shop floor, the British Retail Consortium counts more than 1,600 incidents of violence and abuse against UK retail staff every day.6

This playbook is for the people who have to hold it all together: resilience, security, store operations, IT and communications leaders, wherever their stores trade. It sets out how a store, its region and head office work as one team on the busiest day of the year, who decides what, what to have ready before each peak and the messages to send in the first hour. It ends with a practical toolkit you can use straight away. The companion intelligence report sets out the evidence in full.

Executive summary

Stores, regions and head office must run one incident, not three.

01 · The operational challenge
Head office decisions, such as switching systems off, land on stores within minutes, often before anyone has told them.
Peaks such as Black Friday, White Friday, Christmas and the end of Ramadan multiply queues, staffing gaps and pressure.
Store managers see problems first but often lack a fast way to raise them and know they have landed.
02 · Incidents and risks to prepare for
Card payment or till outages across many stores or online, from a provider fault or an attack.
Cyber attacks that force systems offline and can start 72-hour breach notification clocks in several jurisdictions.
Violence and abuse against colleagues, and incidents that need evacuation, invacuation or lockdown.
Refrigeration and cold-chain failures, including controllers tampered with remotely, that put food safety at risk.
03 · Key recommendations
Use one severity ladder and one escalation workflow across store, region and head office.
Agree decision rights now: who closes a store, who switches to cash, who speaks to the media.
Pre-approve messages for stores, colleagues, customers, regulators, suppliers and media, each with a next update time.
Map breach notification deadlines, card acquirer contacts and food temperature limits for every country you trade in.
Rehearse a payment outage, a lockdown and a cold-chain incident with stores before every peak.
04 · What effective coordination looks like
Every store acknowledges within minutes, and silent stores are chased by their regional manager.
Store managers act on safety at once; head office hears straight after.
One log holds every decision, owner and reason, from first report to stand down.
A store-by-store view shows who is trading, cash only, closed or in need of help.
05 · How Crises Control supports the response
Most organisations can send alerts. Few can manage incidents end-to-end.
Crises Control is an Operational Incident Coordination Platform. Ping reaches store and regional managers on several channels, and acknowledgements show which stores have heard.
Plans you build turn the cash procedure, lockdown and cold-chain checks into owned tasks, with escalation on no response under rules you configure.

One incident, one team

Ready for peak

One incident, two worlds: the store and head office

Every head office decision has a store consequence, and someone must own telling stores about it.

A retail incident is lived in two places at once. At head office, it is a dashboard turning red, a bridge call and a decision about which systems to switch off. In the store, it is a queue that will not move, a customer who wants to know why and a manager who has heard nothing.

The 2025 attacks on UK retailers showed how quickly the two worlds connect. Marks and Spencer first disclosed a cyber incident after problems with contactless payments and click and collect over the Easter weekend, and stopped taking online orders three days later.2

The decisions that protect the business at head office are often the ones that hurt stores most. Taking systems offline can stop an attacker. It can also stop stock files, price updates, loyalty scanners and delivery slots. When Ahold Delhaize took systems offline in October 2026, it said disruption to online capabilities and to Benelux supply chain operations would weigh on fourth-quarter sales.3

So the first rule of retail incident management is that every head office decision has a store consequence, and someone must own telling stores about it. The second is that information must flow upwards as well as down. Store managers see the effects first: the first declined card, the first fridge alarm, the first group of shoplifters who turn aggressive. They need a fast, simple way to raise it and to know it has landed.

That means one severity scale that stores, regions and head office all use, one place where the facts live, scheduled updates even when there is nothing new, and acknowledgement from every store so you know who has not heard. It also means trusting store managers with clear authority to act on safety without waiting for permission: to close a door, stop a queue or take stock off sale.

None of this needs to be built during an incident. It needs to be agreed, written down and rehearsed before the busiest weeks of the year, whichever calendar your stores trade to.

Regional notes · Breach notification

After a cyber attack: who you must tell, and how fast

Retail attacks often expose customer or loyalty data and card payments. Deadlines start when you become aware of a breach, so the decision log must record that moment.
Regional note · United Kingdom
UK GDPR Article 33: notify the regulator without undue delay and, where feasible, within 72 hours of becoming aware of a personal data breach, unless it is unlikely to result in a risk to people. From 30 September 2026 the text names the Information Commission, which took over from the ICO.7
IN PRACTICE Record the time of awareness and your risk assessment in the Incident decision log. If you notify late, give reasons.
Regional note · United States
All states, the District of Columbia, Puerto Rico and the Virgin Islands have breach notification laws; triggers and deadlines vary by state.8 For card data, PCI DSS Requirement 12.10.1 (an industry standard, applied through your acquirer) requires an incident response plan that covers notifying payment brands and acquirers.9
IN PRACTICE Map which states your customers live in. Keep acquirer and payment brand contacts in the plan, wherever you trade.
Regional note · European Union
GDPR Article 33: notify the competent supervisory authority without undue delay and, where feasible, within 72 hours. Article 34: tell affected people without undue delay where the breach is likely to result in a high risk to them.10
IN PRACTICE Know your lead supervisory authority in advance if you trade in several member states.
Regional note · Middle East
UAE: Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 requires controllers to report breaches to the UAE Data Office; timing is left to Executive Regulations, which legal summaries report as not yet published. DIFC and ADGM have their own laws.11 Saudi Arabia: the PDPL is fully enforceable from 14 September 2024; notify SDAIA within 72 hours where a breach is likely to cause harm. A draft amendment would drop that harm test (proposed).12
IN PRACTICE Check which law applies to each store, including free zone sites, and name who files the notice.
A summary for orientation, not legal advice. Requirements depend on sector, size, licence and jurisdiction, and change over time. Confirm the current text with your legal and compliance teams.

Regional notes · Cold chain

The temperature rules behind the fridge

When a controller cannot be trusted, the question is simple: can you show the food stayed within the limit that applies where you trade? If not, it comes off sale.
Regional note · United Kingdom
Chilled food must legally be kept at 8°C or below in England, Wales and Northern Ireland, according to the Food Standards Agency.13
IN PRACTICE Probe the food, not the display, and record readings on the cold-chain quarantine record. For stores in Scotland, check Food Standards Scotland guidance.
Regional note · United States
The FDA Food Code is a model code that state and local jurisdictions adopt. The 2022 edition requires time/temperature control for safety food to be cold-held at 5°C (41°F) or less.14
IN PRACTICE Check the version your state or county has adopted; editions and local amendments differ. Keep probe records with the time each reading was taken.
Regional note · European Union
Regulation (EC) No 852/2004, Annex II, Chapter IX: foods that can support pathogens must not be kept at temperatures that risk health, and the cold chain is not to be interrupted, with limited periods allowed for handling.15
IN PRACTICE Use the limits in your national rules and your own food safety procedures as the trigger for taking stock off sale, and record each decision with the reading behind it.
Regional note · Middle East (UAE)
The Dubai Municipality Food Code says high-risk food should be kept at or below 5°C, or at or above 60°C, and calls 5°C to 60°C the temperature danger zone.16 Other emirates and GCC states set their own rules.
IN PRACTICE Confirm the authority and limits for each emirate or country you trade in, and brief night managers, who often find the alarm first.
A summary for orientation, not legal advice. Requirements depend on sector, size, licence and jurisdiction, and change over time. Confirm the current text with your legal and compliance teams.

Violence and abuse

After an assault on a colleague: six steps that matter

Abuse is a daily reality on shop floors everywhere. United Kingdom: the BRC estimates over 1,600 incidents of violence and abuse against retail staff a day, 118 of them physically violent.6
01

Make safe and get help

Call emergency services if anyone is hurt or in danger. Do not detain or follow the offender.
02

Look after the colleague

First aid, a quiet room, someone with them, a safe way home. Not back on the till.
03

Keep the evidence

Secure CCTV and body-worn video, note witnesses, write it up within the shift.
04

Report it, every time

Report to police and keep the reference. Every report builds the evidence for local policing and store risk reviews.
05

Tell head office within the hour

One message, one channel: area manager, security and HR hear the same facts.
06

Follow up and learn

Call the colleague next day, review staffing and layout, share intelligence locally.

Protective procedures

Evacuate, invacuate, lock down, communicate

Four procedures every large store needs, wherever it trades. Brief them, walk them through, and agree who decides on each.

Evacuation

Getting people away from danger by moving them out of the premises. Know your exits, assembly points and who sweeps each area.

Invacuation

Moving people to a safer place inside, such as a stockroom or back corridor away from glass and entrances.

Lockdown

Securing the premises to stop people entering or leaving. Know which doors lock, who locks them and how fast.

Communication

Getting information to everyone on site: colleagues, customers and contractors, with a way to reach staff who are off the shop floor.
United Kingdom: Martyn's Law will require shops where 200 or more people can be expected at once to have these four procedures; the duties are not yet in force.17 United States: where an OSHA standard requires an emergency action plan, 29 CFR 1910.38 sets minimum elements, including reporting and evacuation procedures, accounting for employees and an alarm system.18

Cold chain

When you cannot trust the fridge

Refrigeration controllers were tampered with remotely across Europe in autumn 2026. Treat an unexplained defrost as a possible attack until you know otherwise.

Spot it

An alarm, a case warming for no reason or a defrost cycle outside its schedule.

Check the food, not the display

Probe product temperatures by hand and compare them with the legal limit where you trade (see the regional notes in Part 01).

Take it off sale

Remove anything you cannot show stayed at a safe temperature. Record what, where and when for the insurer and food safety team.

Cut the remote link

With the contractor, take controllers off the internet and allow access only through a secure VPN. CERT-FR warns a factory reset alone is not enough for end-of-life kit.4

Restore and check every site

Reconfigure, check defrost schedules, then look for the same controllers at every other store and depot.
Check your insurance conditions before cutting data links to the refrigeration contractor, as CERT-FR advises.4

Before every peak

Peak readiness in six steps

Finish these before every peak: Black Friday (White Friday in the Gulf),19 Christmas, Ramadan and Eid. In Ramadan 2024, Visa found late-night transactions doubled their share in Saudi Arabia, to 35%.20
01

Freeze change and name the on-call team

No non-essential changes to payments, tills, e-commerce or networks. Publish who is on call, day and night.
02

Test the offline and cash procedure

Confirm what each till and terminal can do offline, how much cash float stores hold and who decides to go cash only.
03

Rehearse one outage and one lockdown

A 30-minute exercise with store and regional managers: a payment failure at the busiest hour, then a lockdown call.
04

Check every contact list

Managers, suppliers, payment providers, acquirers and contractors, each with a tested out-of-hours number.
05

Close the doors you forgot

List fridge controllers, building systems and supplier remote access that face the internet, and take them off it.4,5
06

Brief colleagues on safety

De-escalation, raising an alarm, when not to intervene and who to call.

Toolkit 04 · Escalation workflow

Escalating from store to region to head office

Four levels that match the severity ladder. The times are suggested starting points; set your own and write them into your alerting rules, so escalation does not depend on someone remembering.
LEVEL 1
Store
Trigger
One store: tills failing, an aggressive customer, a single fridge alarm, a false fire alarm.
Who is alerted
Store manager, colleagues on shift, the service desk or facilities helpdesk.
Who decides
Store manager: pause entry, go cash only, take stock off sale, call emergency services.
↓ESCALATE IF TWO OR MORE STORES ARE HIT, ANYONE IS HURT, OR THERE IS NO FIX IN 30 MINUTES
LEVEL 2
Region
Trigger
The same fault in several stores in one region, a serious assault, stock at risk or a credible threat.
Who is alerted
Regional manager, area managers, IT or facilities on call, loss prevention.
Who decides
Regional manager: close stores in the region, move colleagues, ask head office for support.
↓ESCALATE IF MORE THAN ONE REGION OR ONLINE IS HIT, OR ANY STORE IS SILENT AFTER 10 MINUTES
LEVEL 3
Head office
Trigger
Payments, tills or core systems down across regions or online, a suspected cyber attack, a lockdown or life-safety incident.
Who is alerted
Head office incident lead, IT and security, communications, loss prevention, every affected regional manager.
Who decides
Head office incident lead: estate-wide cash procedure, systems offline, customer messages.
↓ESCALATE IF TRADING CANNOT RESUME BY THE NEXT UPDATE, OR DATA OR MEDIA ARE INVOLVED
LEVEL 4
Executive crisis team
Trigger
Business-wide disruption, a data breach, serious injury or death, sustained media attention.
Who is alerted
Executive team, legal, communications director, insurer and incident response provider.
Who decides
Executive lead: closing the estate or online, public statements, regulator, acquirer and insurer notifications.
Silence is a signal. A store that has not acknowledged within ten minutes is chased by its regional manager; a regional manager who has not acknowledged is replaced by their deputy and the head office incident lead is told.

Toolkit 05 · Communication templates

Six messages ready to send

Short, specific and honest. Fill the brackets, never guess the cause, and always give the time of the next update.
SMS · Store managers146 chars
SEV1: Card payments down in many stores. Switch to cash and offline procedure now. Do not restart terminals. Reply 1 when done. Next update 08:30.
Keep it under 160 characters so it arrives as one message.
Push · All store colleagues
Some payment systems are down. Your safety comes first. Stay calm, use the till script, call security if anyone becomes aggressive. Please do not post about this online.
Web banner · Customers
We're sorry: some card payments aren't working in our stores right now. Stores are open and accepting [cash]. We're working to fix it and will update here at [10:00].
Only say what you know is true. Add what still works.
Email · Data protection regulator
Initial notice of a personal data breach. Controller: [name, ref]. Became aware: [date, time]. What happened: [summary]. Data and people affected: [types, approx. numbers]. Measures taken: [summary]. Contact: [lead]. More to follow by [date].
Use the regulator's own form where there is one. Send what you know now and the rest in phases.
Email · Suppliers and hauliers
We are managing an IT incident affecting [systems]. Deliveries continue as booked unless we call you. Do not act on any request to change bank details or give access. Verify on [known number].
Attackers often follow up with fake supplier requests.
Holding line · Media
We are aware of an issue affecting payments in some of our stores and are working to resolve it as quickly as possible. Our stores remain open. We will share more information as soon as we can.

Toolkit 06 · Tabletop exercise

Exercise: tills down on the busiest Saturday of peak

An illustrative 90-minute exercise for store, regional and head office leads. Read each inject aloud, give the room five minutes, and record every decision, owner and gap.
Duration
90 minutes, six injects
Participants
Store and regional managers, head office lead, IT, comms, loss prevention
Objective
Test decision rights and store acknowledgement across many stores
Facilitator
Head of resilience or an external facilitator

Card payments fail in 120 stores across three regions. Online checkout is slow.
Discuss
Who declares, at what level? What is the first instruction to stores, and by when?
10:10
Inject 2
Eighteen stores have not acknowledged. Two regional managers are on leave.
Discuss
How do you know who is silent? Who covers for the absent regional managers?
10:30
Inject 3
A queue in a large store turns hostile and a colleague is pushed.
Discuss
Who decides to pause entry or close? What support does the colleague get, and who checks?
10:50

IT finds signs of intrusion and wants to take the store network offline.
Discuss

11:15

A journalist calls a store directly. A video of the queue is spreading online.
Discuss
Who speaks for the business? What does the store say, word for word?
13:00

Freezer alarms in six stores. They share the same controller model.
Discuss
Is it connected? Who decides on stock, and who checks every other site?

Glossary

Words worth knowing

Severity level
A shared grade (SEV 1 to SEV 4) that sets who is alerted, how fast and how often.
Offline payment mode
A terminal setting that stores some card transactions for later authorisation when the network is down.
Acquirer
The bank or provider that processes card payments for a retailer and receives incident reports about card data.
Change freeze
A period, usually over peak, when only essential changes to systems are allowed.
Supervisory authority
The data protection regulator that receives breach notifications, for example the Information Commission in the UK.
Invacuation
Moving people to a safer place inside a building, away from danger outside.
Lockdown
Securing a building so people cannot enter or leave until it is safe.
Martyn's Law (UK)
The Terrorism (Protection of Premises) Act 2025, which sets duties for larger public premises in the UK.
Cold chain
Keeping chilled and frozen food at safe temperatures from supplier to shelf.
Refrigeration controller
A device that runs fridges and freezers and can often be managed remotely.
Defrost cycle
A scheduled warm-up that clears ice. Stretched or repeated, it spoils stock.
Acknowledgement
A recipient's confirmation that an alert has been read and acted on.
Incident manager
The person who runs the response, keeps the log and sets the rhythm of updates.
Holding line
A short, accurate statement used while facts are still being established.
White Friday
The name used for Black Friday sales in much of the Gulf.
End of life
When a manufacturer stops issuing security fixes for a product.

Does it stop at the message?
