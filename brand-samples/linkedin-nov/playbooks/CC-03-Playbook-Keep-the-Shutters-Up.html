Foreword

Retail crises rarely start at head office. They start at a till, a freezer or a shop door.

In April 2025 a cyber attack forced Marks and Spencer to pause online orders for 46 days, and the retailer estimated the hit to operating profit at around £300m before mitigation.1,2 The Co-op shut down systems to contain a parallel attack, lost an estimated £206m of sales in six months and later confirmed that data on all 6.5 million members had been taken.3,4 In October 2026 an incident at Ahold Delhaize took online grocery services offline in the Benelux and the US; the group now expects fourth-quarter sales to be €200m to €250m lower.5

The same month, attackers switched supermarket refrigeration into defrost from the internet. France's CERT-FR reported numerous compromised controllers, and on 2 November a second wave reached stores in Ireland, Belgium and Sweden and food suppliers in the UK.6,7 Meanwhile, the BRC counts more than 1,600 incidents of violence and abuse against retail staff every day.8

This playbook is for the people who have to hold it all together: resilience, security, store operations, IT and communications leaders. It sets out how a store and head office should work as one team on the worst day of the year, what to have ready before Black Friday, and the messages to send in the first hour. The companion intelligence report sets out the evidence in full.

Introduction

Three things keep a store trading on a bad day.

Retail incidents move fast and land on the shop floor first. The retailers that cope best are not the ones with the thickest manuals. They are the ones where stores and head office already know who decides, how they talk and what each store does without being asked.

One incident, one team

A store manager and the head office incident lead share the same severity scale, the same facts and the same clock. Nobody waits for a meeting to start.

Fast, two-way messages

Head office can reach every store, and every store can reach head office, in minutes, with a record of who has read what and who has not.

People before stock

Every procedure starts with colleague and customer safety, from a queue that turns hostile to a lockdown call or a freezer that cannot be trusted.

Ready for peak

One incident, two worlds: the store and head office

Every head office decision has a store consequence, and someone must own telling stores about it.

A retail incident is lived in two places at once. At head office, it is a dashboard turning red, a bridge call and a decision about which systems to switch off. In the store, it is a queue that will not move, a customer who wants to know why and a manager who has heard nothing.

The 2025 attacks showed how quickly the two worlds connect. M&S first disclosed a cyber incident after problems with contactless payments and click and collect over the Easter weekend, and stopped taking online orders three days later.2 The Co-op said it kept trading while it contained its attack, but had to deal with payment problems and empty shelves along the way.3

The decisions that protect the business at head office are often the ones that hurt stores most. Taking systems offline can stop an attacker. It can also stop stock files, price updates, loyalty scanners and delivery slots. When Ahold Delhaize took systems offline as a precaution in October 2026, stores stayed open but some shelf labels showed out-of-date promotional prices and loyalty scanners failed.9

So the first rule of retail incident management is that every head office decision has a store consequence, and someone must own telling stores about it. The second is that information must flow upwards as well as down. Store managers see the effects first: the first declined card, the first fridge alarm, the first group of shoplifters who turn aggressive. They need a fast, simple way to raise it and to know it has landed.

That means one severity scale that stores and head office both use, one place where the facts live, scheduled updates even when there is nothing new, and acknowledgement from every store so you know who has not heard. It also means trusting store managers with clear authority to act on safety without waiting for permission: to close a door, stop a queue or take stock off sale.

None of this needs to be built during an incident. It needs to be agreed, written down and rehearsed before the busiest weeks of the year.

Severity

A retail severity ladder that stores and head office share

Grade early and regrade often. If in doubt, go one level higher and step down later.
SEV 1
Critical
Triggers
Payments, tills or core systems down across many stores or online. A weapon, terror or life-safety incident at any store. Controller tampering at several sites.
Who is alerted, how
Crisis team, all affected store and area managers, IT and security leads, communications and legal.
Voice · SMS · push · bridge in 15 min
SEV 2
Major
Triggers
Outage in one region or one channel. Serious assault on a colleague. Fridge or freezer failure with stock at risk. A credible threat to a store.
Who is alerted, how
Incident manager, area and regional managers, IT or facilities on call, communications on standby.
SMS · push · escalate after 10 min
SEV 3
Moderate
Triggers
Problem at a single store: tills failing, a violent incident without injury, one fridge alarm, an evacuation for a false alarm.
Who is alerted, how
Store manager, area manager, service desk or facilities helpdesk.
Push · email
SEV 4
Minor
Triggers
Verbal abuse logged, a single device fault, a near miss or a suspicious approach to staff.
Who is alerted, how
Store manager, recorded for trend reporting and weekly review.
App · email

Before Black Friday

Peak readiness in six steps

Black Friday falls on 27 November this year. Finish these before then, and repeat the checks before the last trading weekend before Christmas.
01

Freeze change and name the on-call team

No non-essential changes to payments, tills, e-commerce or networks over peak. Publish who is on call for every day, including weekends.
02

Test the offline and cash procedure

Confirm what each till and terminal can do offline, how much cash float stores hold and who decides to go cash only.
03

Rehearse one outage and one lockdown

A 30-minute exercise with store and area managers: a payment failure at 10am on a Saturday, then a lockdown call.
04

Check every contact list

Store, area and regional managers, key suppliers, payment providers and contractors, each with a tested out-of-hours number.
05

Close the doors you forgot

List fridge controllers, building systems and supplier remote access that face the internet, and take them off it.6,12
06

Brief colleagues on safety

De-escalation, how to raise an alarm, when not to intervene and who to call. Staff the hotspots, not just the tills.

Violence and abuse

After an assault on a colleague: six steps that matter

The BRC estimates over 1,600 incidents of violence and abuse against retail staff a day, 118 of them physically violent.8
01

Make safe and get help

Call 999 if anyone is hurt or in danger. Do not detain or follow the offender.
02

Look after the colleague

First aid, a quiet room, someone with them, a safe way home. Not back on the till.
03

Keep the evidence

Secure CCTV and body-worn video, note witnesses, write it up within the shift.
04

Report it, every time

Report to police and keep the reference. The new offence of assaulting a retail worker is enacted but not yet in force.13
05

Tell head office within the hour

One message, one channel: area manager, security and HR hear the same facts.
06

Follow up and learn

Call the colleague next day, review staffing and layout, share intelligence locally.

Martyn's Law procedures

Evacuate, invacuate, lock down, communicate

The Terrorism (Protection of Premises) Act 2025 will require shops where 200 or more people can be expected at once to have these four procedures, so far as is reasonably practicable. The duties are not yet in force.14

Evacuation

Getting people away from danger by moving them out of the premises. Know your exits, assembly points and who sweeps each area.14

Invacuation

Moving people to a safer place inside, such as a stockroom or back corridor away from glass and entrances.14

Lockdown

Securing the premises to stop people entering or leaving. Know which doors lock, who locks them and how fast.14

Communication

Getting information to everyone on site: colleagues, customers and contractors, with a way to reach staff who are off the shop floor.14
The implementation period is at least 24 months from Royal Assent on 3 April 2025. Premises expecting 800 or more people fall into the enhanced tier, with wider duties.14

Cold chain

When you cannot trust the fridge

Refrigeration controllers have been tampered with remotely across Europe this autumn. Treat an unexplained defrost as a possible attack until you know otherwise.

Spot it

An alarm, a case warming for no reason or a defrost cycle outside its schedule.

Check the food, not the display

Probe product temperatures by hand. In England, Wales and Northern Ireland chilled food must legally be kept at 8°C or below.10

Take it off sale

Remove anything you cannot show stayed at a safe temperature. Record what, where and when for the insurer and food safety team.

Cut the remote link

With the contractor, take controllers off the internet and allow access only through a secure VPN. CERT-FR warns a factory reset alone is not enough for end-of-life kit.6

Restore and check every site

Reconfigure, check defrost schedules, then look for the same controllers at every other store and depot.6,12
Check your insurance conditions before cutting data links to the refrigeration contractor, as CERT-FR advises.6

Messages

Six messages ready to send

Short, specific and honest. Fill the brackets, never guess the cause, and always give the time of the next update.
SMS · Store managers146 chars
SEV1: Card payments down in many stores. Switch to cash and offline procedure now. Do not restart terminals. Reply 1 when done. Next update 08:30.
Keep it under 160 characters so it arrives as one message.
Push · All store colleagues
Some payment systems are down. Your safety comes first. Stay calm, use the till script, call security if anyone becomes aggressive. Please do not post about this online.
Web banner · Customers
We're sorry: some card payments aren't working in our stores right now. Stores are open and accepting [cash]. We're working to fix it and will update here at [10:00].
Only say what you know is true. Add what still works.
Email · Suppliers and hauliers
We are managing an IT incident affecting [systems]. Deliveries continue as booked unless we call you. Do not act on any request to change bank details or give access. Verify on [known number].
Attackers often follow up with fake supplier requests.
Holding line · Media
We are aware of an issue affecting payments in some of our stores and are working to resolve it as quickly as possible. Our stores remain open. We will share more information as soon as we can.
SMS · Store manager, cold chain153 chars
SEV2: Fridge controller alarm at your store. Probe food temps now. Do not reset the controller. Take unsafe stock off sale. Reply 1, then call [FM line].

Glossary

Words worth knowing

Severity level
A shared grade (SEV 1 to SEV 4) that sets who is alerted, how fast and how often.
Offline payment mode
A terminal setting that stores some card transactions for later authorisation when the network is down.
Change freeze
A period, usually over peak, when only essential changes to systems are allowed.
Invacuation
Moving people to a safer place inside a building, away from danger outside.
Lockdown
Securing a building so people cannot enter or leave until it is safe.
Martyn's Law
The Terrorism (Protection of Premises) Act 2025, which sets duties for larger public premises.
Cold chain
Keeping chilled and frozen food at safe temperatures from supplier to shelf.
Refrigeration controller
A device that runs fridges and freezers and can often be managed remotely.
Defrost cycle
A scheduled warm-up that clears ice. Stretched or repeated, it spoils stock.
Acknowledgement
A recipient's confirmation that an alert has been read and acted on.
Incident manager
The person who runs the response, keeps the log and sets the rhythm of updates.
Holding line
A short, accurate statement used while facts are still being established.
Body-worn video
A camera worn by colleagues or security staff that records incidents as evidence.
Responsible person
Under Martyn's Law, the person or organisation in control of qualifying premises.
Remote access
A route for suppliers or engineers to manage systems from outside the store.
End of life
When a manufacturer stops issuing security fixes for a product.
