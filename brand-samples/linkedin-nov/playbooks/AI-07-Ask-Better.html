Foreword

The quality of the answer starts with the quality of the ask.

Two people can use the same AI assistant on the same task and get completely different results. One gets a generic paragraph that needs rewriting. The other gets a sharp, usable draft in the right tone, built on the right facts. The difference is rarely the tool. It is the brief.

That is good news for business leaders, because briefing is a skill you already have. You brief new starters, agencies, consultants and colleagues every week. The same habits that get good work from people, a clear goal, the right background and a defined outcome, get good work from AI.

This playbook shows how to apply those habits to assistants such as Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini and Copilot. It covers how to write a strong brief, how to iterate and check the result, and how to turn individual skill into shared templates your whole team can use.

Introduction

Brief it like a capable colleague.

An AI assistant is quick, well read and tireless, but it knows nothing about your business, your client or your standards unless you tell it. Most disappointing results come from a missing piece of the brief, not a weakness in the tool.

Say what good looks like

Describe the goal, the audience and the outcome you need, not just the topic.

Give it the material

Paste or attach the documents, data and examples it needs. Do not make it guess.

Treat the first answer as a draft

Ask for changes, ask it to critique itself, and check anything that matters before you use it.

The brief

The best prompters are good managers

If you cannot describe what good looks like, neither the assistant nor a colleague can deliver it.

Think about the last time you briefed someone new on an important piece of work. You probably explained why it mattered, who it was for, what you already knew, what to avoid and what the finished product should look like. You may have shown them an example of something similar that worked well.

That is exactly what a good prompt contains. AI assistants are remarkably capable, but they start every task without your context. They do not know your client is sensitive about price, that your board prefers one page, or that your house style avoids jargon. Leave those things out and the assistant fills the gaps with safe, average assumptions.

This is why prompting is a management skill rather than a technical one. Clear thinking about the outcome matters more than clever wording. If you cannot describe what good looks like, the assistant cannot produce it, and neither could a human colleague.

Length is not the enemy. A prompt of several paragraphs with attached documents will usually beat a single line. The assistant can handle the detail, and every relevant fact you add narrows the range of possible answers towards the one you need.

Good briefs also say what not to do. Tell the assistant to avoid certain claims, keep to a word count, stick to the facts in the attached report or flag anything it is unsure about. Constraints are not a limitation. They are how you get output you can use without heavy editing.

Finally, expect a conversation. Even a strong brief rarely produces the perfect answer first time. The skill is in reviewing the draft, saying clearly what to change and building towards the result, just as you would with a colleague.

The six-part brief

Six ingredients of a prompt that works

You will not need all six every time, but the more that matter to the task, the more you should include.
01

Goal

What you are trying to achieve and why. A decision, a reply, a plan, a first draft.
02

Context

The background the assistant cannot know: the client, the history, the documents and the facts.
03

Audience

Who will read the output, what they already know and what they care about.
04

Constraints

Length, tone, things to avoid, facts to stick to and anything that must be included.
05

Examples

A past piece of work you liked, or a sample of the style and structure you want.
06

Format

Bullets, table, email, one-page memo, headings. Say exactly how you want it laid out.
Remember
A quick test: would a capable new colleague have enough to do this well? If not, neither does the assistant.

The loop

From first draft to something you can use

Strong users treat AI as a back-and-forth, not a vending machine.

Brief

Give the goal, context, audience, constraints, examples and format.

Draft

Let the assistant produce a first version. Read it as you would a junior colleague's work.

Critique

Ask it to review its own draft against your brief and list weaknesses.

Refine

Say specifically what to change: shorter, firmer, more evidence, different structure.

Verify

Check facts, figures, names and anything that will reach a client or a decision.
Specific feedback beats starting again. Tell it what to keep as well as what to change.

Ask Better · AI Playbook Series13 / 24
01

Give it the documents

The single biggest improvement most people can make is to stop asking from memory and start giving the assistant the actual material.
What to include
The source report, the email thread, the policy, the data or a past example. Approved tools only.
How to frame it
Say what each document is and how to use it. Ask it to rely only on what you have provided.
What to leave out
Personal or confidential data your policy does not allow, and anything irrelevant.
How to check
Ask it to quote the passage behind each key point, then spot-check those quotes yourself.
Owner
Every user, guided by your AI policy
Bring this to the monthly review, with a date for each action.

Verify

Five rules for checking AI output

AI can be confidently wrong. These habits catch most problems before they matter.
01
Check every number
Figures, dates and calculations come from your systems or sources, never from the assistant alone.
02
Ask for the source
Where it states a fact, ask where it came from. Be wary of anything it cannot point to.
03
Read it as the recipient
Would the client, the board or the regulator be happy with every sentence?
04
Test the reasoning
Ask it to argue the opposite case. Weak conclusions tend to show themselves quickly.
05
Keep a person accountable
Whoever sends or uses the output owns it, whether or not AI drafted it.

Team habits

From personal trick to team capability

Capture your best prompts once, and everyone starts from your best work.

In most organisations, prompting skill is unevenly spread. A few people have worked out what gets great results, and the rest are still typing one-line questions. That gap is an opportunity: the best prompts in your business can be captured once and used by everyone.

Start with templates for the tasks your teams do most often. A good template is a brief with blanks: the goal, context and format already written, with spaces for the specific client, document or figures. People fill in the blanks and get a consistent, high-quality starting point every time.

Store templates in one shared library that everyone can find, with an owner who keeps it tidy. Note which tool each template was tested in, what it is good for and what to check in the output. Retire templates that stop working, because models change and so do your needs.

Most business assistants also let you save standing instructions so you do not repeat yourself. Some group conversations, files and instructions into projects or shared workspaces, so an assistant always has your style guide, product details or client background to hand. Names and features vary by tool and change often, so check what yours offers.

Use these features for the context that never changes: your tone of voice, your house style, your service descriptions and the standards every output should meet. That frees each prompt to focus on the task in hand.

Finally, make sharing normal. Ask champions to post a good prompt each week, review the library quarterly and include prompting in new starter training. Skill that lives in a shared library survives people changing roles. Skill that lives in one person's head does not.

Standing context

Set it once, use it every time

Store the context that rarely changes, so every prompt can focus on the task in hand.

Standing instructions

Your role, your organisation and how you like answers laid out, saved so you do not repeat them in every chat.

Projects and workspaces

Shared files and instructions for a client, product or team, so the assistant always has the right background.

Common mistakes

Six habits that hold teams back

Each is easy to spot and quick to fix once people know to look for it.
01

The one-line ask

A topic with no goal, audience or format produces a generic answer.
02

Asking from memory

Expecting the assistant to know your facts instead of giving it the documents.
03

Accepting the first draft

Stopping at version one instead of giving specific feedback.
04

Starting over too soon

Abandoning a conversation that only needed one clear correction.
05

Trusting numbers and names

Using figures, quotes or citations without checking them against a source.
06

Keeping good prompts private

Great briefs stay in one inbox instead of the shared library.
Remember
Most of these disappear with one hour of hands-on practice using real work, not generic examples.

Glossary

Words worth knowing

Prompt
The instructions, questions and material you give an AI assistant.
Brief
A complete prompt covering goal, context, audience, constraints, examples and format.
Context
The documents, data and background the assistant can see when it answers.
Context window
How much text an assistant can take into account at once.
Iteration
Improving an output through rounds of specific feedback.
Role prompt
Asking the assistant to respond from a particular perspective or expertise.
Standing instructions
Saved guidance an assistant applies to every conversation.
Project or workspace
A space that groups chats, files and instructions for one purpose.
Template
A reusable prompt with blanks for the details of each task.
Prompt library
A shared, owned collection of tested templates for a team or organisation.
Hallucination
A confident answer that is wrong or made up.
Grounding
Asking the assistant to base its answer only on material you have provided.
