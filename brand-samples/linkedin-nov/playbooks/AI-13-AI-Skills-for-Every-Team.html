Foreword

The difference is not the tool. It is the person using it.

Give two colleagues the same AI assistant and you will often see very different results. One gets a vague, generic answer and decides the tool is overrated. The other gives clear context, pushes back on the first draft, checks the facts and ends up with something genuinely useful in a fraction of the usual time.

That gap is a skills gap, and it is the single biggest factor in whether AI investment pays off. It is also very fixable. The skills involved are learnable, mostly common sense, and closer to good management and good writing than to anything technical.

This playbook sets out the skills that matter, how to build them for different roles, how to support people who are worried about what AI means for their jobs, and how to keep capability current as the tools keep changing.

Introduction

Capability, not just access.

Rolling out licences gives people access. Capability comes from practice, good examples, honest conversations and support close to the work. That takes deliberate effort, but far less than most leaders expect.

Teach judgement, not tricks

Prompt formulas date quickly. The lasting skills are briefing clearly, judging output and knowing when not to use AI.

Learn close to the work

People learn fastest on their own tasks, with colleagues who do the same job showing them what works.

Be honest about change

People adopt AI faster when leaders talk openly about what will change, what will not and how they will be supported.

Why skills matter

The same tool, very different results

Using AI well is less like programming and more like delegating to a capable new colleague.

Most organisations that roll out AI see the same pattern. A small group of enthusiasts quickly gets impressive results. A larger group tries the tool a few times, gets something mediocre and quietly goes back to old habits. Leaders look at overall usage and wonder whether the investment was worth it.

The difference between those groups is rarely intelligence or seniority. It is a handful of practical habits. The enthusiasts give the assistant context: who the audience is, what good looks like, what to avoid. They treat the first answer as a draft. They ask follow-up questions. They check anything that matters before they use it.

None of this is technical. It is much closer to briefing a capable new colleague than to programming a computer. That is good news, because it means almost everyone can learn it, and the people who are good at delegating, writing and reviewing work tend to pick it up fastest.

There is also a skill that rarely gets taught: knowing when not to use AI. Some tasks need original judgement, sensitive handling or a personal touch. Some involve data that should not go into any tool. Good users can tell the difference, and that is what keeps AI use safe as well as productive.

The tools will keep changing. New features arrive constantly and today's tips can be obsolete within months. Build your learning around durable skills and real work, not around a particular interface, and your people will adapt as the tools evolve.

Finally, skills spread socially. People copy what respected colleagues do. A champion in the team who shares a good example on a Monday morning often does more for adoption than a formal course.

Core skills

Five skills every colleague needs

These apply whatever the tool, the role or the level of experience.
01
Briefing
Give clear context, the audience, the goal, examples of good and any constraints. Better input, better output.
02
Judging output
Read critically. Is it accurate, complete, on tone and actually useful, or just fluent?
03
Verifying
Check facts, figures, quotes and references against reliable sources before anything goes out.
04
Knowing when not to
Recognise tasks that need human judgement, care or confidentiality, and leave AI out of them.
05
Data care
Know which tools are approved and what information can and cannot go into them.

01

A champions network that actually works

Champions are the fastest way to spread good practice, provided they have time, support and a clear role.
Who to pick
Respected colleagues who do the day job well and are curious about AI. Enthusiasm matters more than technical skill.
What they do
Share examples, help colleagues one to one, collect questions and feed back what is and is not working.
What they need
Protected time, early access to features, a direct line to IT and a regular forum with other champions.
How to sustain it
Recognise the role in objectives, rotate people over time and share their wins with the wider business.
Owner
AI adoption lead, with a sponsor on the leadership team
Bring this to the monthly review, with a date for each action.

Learning formats

Formats that build real skill

People learn AI by doing it on their own work. Slide decks alone rarely change behaviour.

Hands-on sessions

Short workshops where people bring a real task and finish it with AI, with a coach in the room.

Show-and-tell

Regular slots where colleagues demonstrate something that worked, including what they had to fix.

Prompt libraries

A shared, curated collection of proven prompts and examples for common tasks in each team.

Office hours

Drop-in time with a champion or expert for questions, troubleshooting and ideas.
Aim for little and often. A monthly habit of sharing examples beats a single day of training.

In practice

What good looks like in three teams

Finance

From reluctance to routine

Starting point
A team cautious about accuracy and wary of putting figures into AI tools.
What helped
Clear data rules, then sessions on commentary drafting using figures from their own systems.
The skill built
Briefing with context, and verifying every number against the source.
What changed
Month-end narratives start as AI drafts, with finance reviewing rather than writing.
Customer service

Consistent answers, confident agents

Starting point
Agents unsure whether AI suggestions were safe to send to customers.
What helped
Champions shared side-by-side examples of good and poor suggestions each week.
The skill built
Judging output quickly and knowing when a human reply is needed.
What changed
Agents use suggestions as drafts and escalate sensitive cases themselves.
Sales

Better preparation, less admin

Starting point
A few enthusiasts with great results, most of the team not using AI at all.
What helped
A shared library of call preparation and proposal prompts from top performers.
The skill built
Reusing proven prompts and adapting them to each account.
What changed
Preparation became more consistent across the team, not just the enthusiasts.

Fear and job concerns

Talk about jobs honestly, early and often

People do not resist AI. They resist uncertainty about what it means for them.

Many people have mixed feelings about AI at work. Some are excited. Many are worried: about being replaced, about being judged for not keeping up, or about being blamed when AI gets something wrong. Ignoring those worries does not make them go away. It just pushes them underground, where they slow adoption.

The most effective leaders talk about it directly. They are honest about what they know and what they do not. They explain how the organisation intends to use AI, which tasks it is likely to change, and how people will be supported. Vague reassurance that nothing will change is rarely believed, and rarely true.

Be specific where you can. If the aim is to remove repetitive work so people can spend more time with customers, say so and show it happening. If roles will change, explain how, give people time and invest in helping them move with it.

Make it safe to learn. People need permission to experiment, to admit they are unsure and to get things wrong while they practise. Leaders who use AI openly, including sharing their own mistakes, do a great deal to lower the temperature.

Be equally clear about accountability. AI-assisted work is still the person's work. That is not a threat. It is a statement that judgement and responsibility remain human, which is often what worried colleagues most want to hear.

Finally, listen. Frontline staff often spot both the best uses and the real risks before anyone else. A regular, genuine channel for feedback turns concern into contribution.

Leading the change

A change journey people can follow

Treat AI adoption as a change programme, not a software rollout.

Explain why

Set out what AI is for in your organisation, what will change and what will not.

Show it working

Start with champions on real tasks, and share concrete examples from familiar teams.

Equip everyone

Provide approved tools, the core skills training and a prompt library for each team.

Lead by example

Managers and leaders use AI visibly and talk about how they use it.

Reinforce

Recognise good use, fix friction quickly and build AI into how processes are designed.
Change sticks when people can see it helping colleagues like them, not when it is announced.

Staying current

Keeping skills fresh as the tools change

AI tools change faster than most training programmes. Build in renewal from the start.
01

Refresh the prompt library

Champions retire stale prompts and add new ones each quarter.
02

Share what changed

A short monthly note on new features that matter to your teams, and what to ignore.
03

Revisit the policy

Review your AI policy regularly as tools, risks and guidance develop.
04

Re-run the basics

Include AI skills in induction and refresh them for existing staff.
05

Learn from mistakes

Share near misses and errors openly, so everyone learns from them.
Remember
Capability is a habit, not a course. Keep the habit going and the skills keep up with the tools.

Glossary

Words worth knowing

AI literacy
A working understanding of what AI tools can and cannot do, and how to use them responsibly.
Briefing
Giving an AI assistant the context, goal and constraints it needs to do a task well.
Prompt
The instructions and context you give an AI assistant.
Prompt library
A shared collection of proven prompts and examples for common tasks.
Champion
A colleague who helps their team adopt AI well and shares what works.
Power user
Someone who uses AI tools heavily and in advanced ways.
Builder
Someone who designs assistants, automations or integrations for others to use.
Verification
Checking AI output against reliable sources before it is used.
Hallucination
A confident AI answer that is wrong or made up.
Human in the loop
A person reviews AI output before it is used or acted on.
Change management
The structured approach to helping people adopt new ways of working.
Acceptable use policy
The rules for how staff may use AI tools and data.
