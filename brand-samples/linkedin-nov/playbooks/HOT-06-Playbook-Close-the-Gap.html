Foreword

Patching used to be a monthly chore. It is now a race.

In October 2026 Microsoft released more than 1,400 security fixes in a single month, one of them for a flaw already under attack. Eight days later the PHP project shipped emergency fixes for two critical flaws in the language behind a large share of the web, and Amazon reported an exploitation attempt four days before they were made public. Within a week of Oracle's quarterly update, decoy servers were recording attempts against a new E-Business Suite flaw.

The pattern is clear. The time between a fix being released and attackers using the flaw has collapsed from weeks to days, and sometimes it is negative. AI is helping researchers find more flaws, which means more fixes, and it is making it cheaper to turn a fix into an exploit.

Most organisations do not fall behind because they do not care. They fall behind because their process was built for a slower world: monthly cycles, long test windows and no clear view of what they run. This playbook shows how to change that, step by step, with owners, checks and measures you can report to the board.

Our companion report, The Patch Gap, sets out the evidence in detail, with every figure sourced.

Know what you run

You cannot patch what you cannot see

When PHP2Shell broke, the first question was not how to patch. It was where PHP was running.

When PHP2Shell was announced on 21 October, the first instruction from both the NCSC and CISA was the same: find every PHP installation. Not patch, not mitigate. Find. Many organisations discovered they could not answer the question quickly, because PHP was running inside appliances, vendor products and forgotten web servers as well as their own sites.

That is the heart of the patch gap. Most organisations know their Windows laptops well, because a management tool reports on them every day. They know much less about web servers, appliances, cloud workloads, the software a supplier installed years ago, and the components buried inside other products.

A useful inventory answers four questions for every system: what it is, what software and versions it runs, whether it can be reached from the internet, and who owns it. The last question matters as much as the first. A system with no owner is a system nobody will patch at 9pm on a Wednesday.

You do not need a perfect inventory to start. Pull together what your device management, vulnerability scanner, cloud console and firewall already know. Add an external scan of your own internet-facing estate, because that is the view attackers have. Then fill the gaps system by system.

Keep it live. An inventory built for an audit and then left alone is out of date within weeks. Connect it to the tools that already see change, and review exceptions monthly.

Finally, include suppliers. Ask your managed service provider and key software vendors to tell you which of their products contain common components such as PHP, Java or OpenSSL, and to notify you when those components need urgent fixes.

Emergency process

The emergency lane, from alert to assurance

Agree this in advance, so nobody needs to convene a meeting to get permission when a critical flaw lands.

Detect

Watch CISA's exploited list, NCSC alerts and vendor advisories daily. Your MSP should alert you within hours.

Scope

Use the inventory to find every affected system within hours, internet-facing first.

Contain

If you cannot patch at once, apply vendor mitigations, block exposure or take the system offline.

Patch

Deploy to a small test group, then everywhere. Change approval is pre-agreed for this lane.

Hunt

Check for signs of compromise, looking back to the earliest known attack date.

Confirm

Verify every system is fixed, record the time taken and close the exceptions.
Pre-approval is the key. The emergency lane is a standard change, not a special one.

Testing

Testing that protects you without slowing you down

Testing exists to catch breakage. It should never become the reason an exploited flaw stays open for weeks.
01

Patch in rings

Deploy to IT staff and a pilot group first, then a wider group, then everyone. Each ring takes hours for urgent fixes, days for routine ones.
02

Keep a test copy of key systems

A staging copy of business-critical applications lets you test vendor fixes the day they arrive.
03

Automate the basics

Scripted smoke tests confirm that core services start and key pages load, so you are not waiting on manual checks.
04

Have a rollback ready

Snapshots, backups and documented rollback steps make it safe to move fast.
05

Use rebootless updates where offered

Microsoft offers hotpatching for supported Windows 11 devices and some servers, cutting disruption.
Remember
For an exploited, internet-facing flaw, the risk of not patching is almost always bigger than the risk of a failed update.

The situation
Critical PHP flaws, attempts seen before disclosure, old versions with no fix.

Find every PHP install, patch exposed ones first, isolate what cannot be patched.

All exposed PHP fixed or offline within days, with logs checked for compromise.

Business systems

An Oracle E-Business Suite flaw

The situation
Unauthenticated SQL injection, fixed in October, probed within a week.
The response
Patch test, live and copies. Restrict the web tier to trusted networks meanwhile.

Fix applied before attacks spread, with database activity monitored.
Watch out
Disaster recovery and test copies are often missed.

04

Legacy and end-of-life systems

Software that no longer gets security fixes is where the gap never closes. PHP 8.1 and older got no official fix for PHP2Shell.
The problem
Old systems often run critical processes, have no vendor support and cannot simply be switched off.
Find them
Flag every unsupported operating system, runtime and application in the inventory, with its owner.
Decide
For each one: upgrade, replace, isolate or accept the risk formally, with a date and an executive sign-off.
Isolate
Remove internet access, restrict who can reach it and monitor it closely. Cyber Essentials requires this for unsupported software.
Owner
IT lead, with business owners of each legacy system
Bring this to the monthly review, with a date for each action.

Compensating controls

When you cannot patch today

Mitigations buy time. They never replace the fix, and every one should have an end date.

Take it offline

The safest option for an exposed system with an exploited flaw and no fix. UK public bodies did this for PHP2Shell.

Block the attack path

Web application firewall rules, blocked endpoints or disabled features. Cloudflare called its PHP2Shell rules a stopgap.

Reduce exposure

Restrict access to trusted networks or a VPN, and remove internet access where it is not needed.

Watch closely

Raise logging and alerting on the system, and look for unusual processes, files and outbound traffic.
Record every mitigation as an exception with an owner and a date to remove it.

Pitfalls

Six ways the gap stays open

Each is common and each is avoidable.
01

Waiting for the monthly window

Exploited flaws are handled in the same slot as routine updates.
02

Trusting the severity score alone

A medium flaw being exploited on a public server matters more than a critical one nobody can reach.
03

Forgetting the hidden copies

Test, disaster recovery and vendor-supplied systems are missed.
04

Treating mitigation as a fix

Firewall rules stay in place for months and the patch never happens.
05

Patching without hunting

Systems are fixed after attackers are already inside.
06

Measuring nothing

Nobody can say how long fixes take, so nothing improves.

Glossary

Words worth knowing

Patch latency
The time between a vendor releasing a fix and it being installed on your systems.
CVE
A unique ID for a publicly known security flaw.
CVSS
A 0 to 10 severity score. It does not tell you whether a flaw is being exploited.
Known exploited vulnerability
A flaw with evidence of real attacks. CISA keeps a public list.
Zero-day
A flaw exploited before a fix exists.
Emergency patch
A fix released or deployed outside the normal schedule because of urgent risk.
Patch rings
Deploying updates in waves, starting with a small pilot group.
Hotpatching
Applying security updates without a restart.
Compensating control
A temporary measure that reduces risk until a fix is installed.
End of life
Software that no longer receives security fixes.
Attack surface
Everything an attacker could reach, especially from the internet.
MSP
Managed service provider. A partner that runs some or all of your IT.
