Executive summary

Retail disruption now starts in systems most shoppers never see, and ends on the shop floor.

In April 2025 a cyber attack forced Marks and Spencer to pause online orders for 46 days; it estimated the hit to operating profit at around £300m before mitigation and later booked £131.3m of incident costs against £100m of insurance proceeds.1,2,3 The Co-op lost an estimated £206m of sales in six months and confirmed that data on all 6.5 million members had been taken.4,5

Autumn 2026 brought new patterns. Ahold Delhaize took systems offline in October and expects fourth-quarter sales to be €200m to €250m lower.6 ASOS app users received an unauthorised push notification after an employee's credentials were obtained by impersonation.7 And attackers switched supermarket refrigeration into defrost: around 700 French supermarkets were affected in October, and a second wave on 2 November reached Ireland, Belgium, Sweden and UK food suppliers.8,9,10

On the shop floor in the UK, the BRC estimates over 1,600 incidents of violence and abuse against retail staff a day.11 A UK offence of assaulting a retail worker is now in the Crime and Policing Act 2026 but is not yet in force, and Martyn's Law will require larger UK shops to plan for evacuation, invacuation, lockdown and communication.12,13

This report sets out what happened, separates disclosed figures from claims, compares the breach notification and cold-chain rules in four regions, and ends with practical steps for retail leaders wherever they trade.

The bottom line
Treat payment, store systems, refrigeration controllers and colleague safety as one resilience problem, owned at board level, with a single way to alert and coordinate every store and a plan rehearsed before peak.

The outage

2025: the spring that emptied the shelves

Every shutdown decision has a store and supply chain consequence that must be planned for in advance.

M&S first disclosed a cyber incident on 22 April 2025, after problems with contactless payments and click and collect over the Easter weekend. On 25 April it stopped taking online orders for clothing and home, and did not restart them for 46 days. It said attackers had got in by tricking staff at a third-party contractor.2

The company's own account shows how a cyber response becomes an operational one. Containing the attack meant disconnecting warehouse management systems, which paused online orders, click and collect and in-store ordering. Manual processes were introduced to keep forecasting, ordering and replenishment going, and customers could shop in stores throughout.14

The cost was large but smaller than first feared. In May 2025 M&S estimated a hit to operating profit of around £300m before mitigation.1 A year later it reported £131.3m of incident costs within adjusting items and £100m of insurance proceeds, describing 2025/26 as a year of two halves.3 Its online offer was restored in August 2025.14

The Co-op took a different path to the same place. It shut down systems to contain its attack and kept stores trading, but faced payment problems and stock shortages.4 It estimated lost sales of at least £206m and an £80m profit hit in the six months to 5 July 2025, with around £120m expected for the full year.4 It later confirmed that names, addresses and contact details of all 6.5 million members had been taken, with no financial data involved.5

In July 2025 the National Crime Agency arrested four people in connection with attacks on M&S, the Co-op and Harrods.5 The NCSC called the incidents a wake-up call for all organisations.17

The lesson for retailers is not that systems should never be switched off. It is that every shutdown decision has a store and supply chain consequence that must be planned for in advance.

The outage

2026: the back office and the customer's phone

The first public sign of an attack may be a message your customers see before your own teams do.

On 11 October 2026 Ahold Delhaize took some systems offline after identifying a security issue in its network. It said stores remained open and that it had no evidence that customer or associate data had been stolen.15,20

The effects were felt most online and in logistics. Albert Heijn's online ordering reopened on 23 October, with delivery fleets initially running at about a third of normal capacity. In stores, some electronic shelf labels showed outdated promotional prices and digital loyalty card scanners temporarily failed.15

On 4 November the group set out the bill. It expects the incident to cut fourth-quarter net sales by €200m to €250m and operating income by €75m to €100m, mostly through disruption to online capabilities in the Benelux and the US and to Benelux supply chain operations. Its chief executive said most of the disruption was contained within two weeks.6

ASOS faced a different kind of incident. On 6 October 2026 app users received a push notification claiming the retailer had been hacked and urging staff to make contact.7 ASOS said an attacker had obtained an employee's login credentials by impersonating a trusted contact, then used them to access information on certain third-party platforms and send the notification. It said names and contact details may have been accessed, but not payment card details or passwords.7

The market reacted before the facts were known: ASOS shares fell as much as 15% in London that day.19 The attacker's claim of a wider data compromise had not been confirmed when this report was written.

Two lessons stand out. Customer channels such as push notifications are now part of the incident surface. And the first public sign of an attack may be a message your customers see before your own teams do.

Regional notes · Breach notification

Who a retailer must tell after a breach, and how fast

Two of the four incidents involved customer data. Wherever a retailer trades, deadlines run from awareness, so the moment of awareness must be logged.
Regional note · United Kingdom
UK GDPR Article 33: notify the regulator without undue delay and, where feasible, within 72 hours of becoming aware of a personal data breach, unless it is unlikely to result in a risk to people. From 30 September 2026 the text names the Information Commission, which took over from the ICO.23
IN PRACTICE Record the time of awareness and the risk assessment. A late notice must give reasons.
Regional note · United States
All states, the District of Columbia, Puerto Rico and the Virgin Islands have breach notification laws; triggers and deadlines vary.24 For card data, PCI DSS Requirement 12.10.1 (an industry standard, applied through acquirers) requires an incident response plan covering notification of payment brands and acquirers.25
IN PRACTICE Map customers by state, and keep acquirer and payment brand contacts in the plan, wherever you trade.
Regional note · European Union
GDPR Article 33: notify the competent supervisory authority without undue delay and, where feasible, within 72 hours. Article 34: tell affected people without undue delay where the breach is likely to result in a high risk to them.26
IN PRACTICE Groups trading in several member states should know their lead supervisory authority in advance.
Regional note · Middle East
UAE: Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 requires controllers to report breaches to the UAE Data Office; timing is left to Executive Regulations, which legal summaries report as not yet published. DIFC and ADGM have their own laws.27 Saudi Arabia: the PDPL is fully enforceable from 14 September 2024; notify SDAIA within 72 hours where a breach is likely to cause harm. A draft amendment would drop that harm test (proposed).28
IN PRACTICE Check which law applies to each store, including free zone sites.
A summary for orientation, not legal advice. Requirements depend on sector, size, licence and jurisdiction, and change over time. Confirm the current text with your legal and compliance teams.

The fridge attacks

When the freezer is switched off from the internet

A store that cannot prove its cold chain held has to assume it did not.

On 12 October 2026 France's CERT-FR warned that it knew of numerous compromises of remote controllers for refrigeration installations: Danfoss AK-SC and AK-SM series and Copeland and Dixell XWEB systems. It said exposing a management interface on the internet runs against good practice, and that attack campaigns were probably still under way.16

The French finance minister told lawmakers that about 700 supermarkets were affected. Retailers including Carrefour, Coopérative U, E.Leclerc, Intermarché and Monoprix confirmed impact, and two supermarkets in Alsace reported combined losses of €270,000. Danfoss said most affected units were end-of-life models no longer receiving patches.8

A second wave on 2 November reached other countries. Le Monde Informatique reported around 40 Delhaize stores in Belgium, ICA stores in Sweden and at least 13 SuperValu stores in Ireland, with attackers again triggering defrost cycles.9 SuperValu's owner took chilled and frozen products off sale as a precaution and said all stores were trading normally by that evening.21

The UK was not untouched. Members of the Association of Independent Meat Suppliers and a London fruit and vegetable wholesaler had equipment switched to defrost, but no stock was lost.10,29 The UK's NCSC said it was working with affected organisations and that the impact so far was minimal; no UK supermarket chain had been confirmed as directly affected.10,29

On 5 November US agencies including CISA and the FBI warned of ongoing attacks on refrigeration and cold chain monitoring systems, citing inventory loss, fire hazards and unsafe storage conditions.22

For food retailers this is a food safety issue as much as a cyber one. In England, Wales and Northern Ireland chilled food must legally be kept at 8°C or below.30 A store that cannot prove its cold chain held has to assume it did not.

Regional notes · Cold chain

The temperature rules a tampered fridge can break

After a remote defrost, a store has to show the food stayed within the limit that applies where it trades. These are the reference points in four regions.
Regional note · United Kingdom
Chilled food must legally be kept at 8°C or below in England, Wales and Northern Ireland, according to the Food Standards Agency.30
IN PRACTICE Probe the food, not the controller display, and keep the readings. Stores in Scotland should check Food Standards Scotland guidance.
Regional note · United States
The FDA Food Code is a model code adopted by state and local jurisdictions. The 2022 edition requires time/temperature control for safety food to be cold-held at 5°C (41°F) or less.31
IN PRACTICE Check the edition your state or county has adopted; local amendments differ.
Regional note · European Union
Regulation (EC) No 852/2004, Annex II, Chapter IX: foods that can support pathogens must not be kept at temperatures that risk health, and the cold chain is not to be interrupted, with limited periods allowed for handling.32
IN PRACTICE National rules and each business's own food safety procedures set the working limits.
Regional note · Middle East (UAE)
The Dubai Municipality Food Code says high-risk food should be kept at or below 5°C, or at or above 60°C, and calls 5°C to 60°C the temperature danger zone.33 Other emirates and GCC states set their own rules.
IN PRACTICE Confirm the authority and limits for each emirate or country.

Violence and the law

Violence, abuse and the UK laws catching up

A specific offence of assaulting a retail worker is now law, but is not yet in force.

In the UK, the BRC Crime Report 2026 estimates more than 1,600 incidents of violence and abuse against retail staff a day, about 590,000 in the year to August 2025. That is down from over 2,000 a day the year before, but far above the 455 a day recorded in 2019 to 2020.11 Around 118 incidents a day involved physical violence, and incidents involving a weapon fell from 68 to 36 a day.11

Reporting remains patchy. Only 15% of all incidents of abuse, threats and violence were reported to the police, although retailers reported 55% of physically violent incidents.11 Usdaw's own survey of 9,481 retail staff found 77% had been verbally abused in the previous year, 53% threatened and 10% assaulted.34

UK law is changing. The Crime and Policing Act 2026 received Royal Assent on 29 April 2026.18 Section 45 makes it an offence to assault a retail worker at work, covering anyone working on or about retail premises for the owner or occupier, including unpaid work, and is triable summarily with up to six months in prison, a fine or both.12 The legislation.gov.uk text still marks the section as prospective: it is not yet in force, and no commencement date has been published.12,18

Section 46 adds a duty on courts to make a criminal behaviour order for the offence, subject to conditions in the Act, and section 47 makes theft from a shop triable either way whatever the value of the goods, ending the £200 threshold.11,35

In the UK, Martyn's Law adds a different duty. The Terrorism (Protection of Premises) Act 2025 applies to premises wholly or mainly used for listed purposes, including shops, where 200 or more people can reasonably be expected at the same time.13 Standard tier premises will need evacuation, invacuation, lockdown and communication procedures, so far as is reasonably practicable, and must notify the SIA.13 The implementation period is at least 24 months from Royal Assent on 3 April 2025.13

What it means

What it means for organisations in Retail

A cyber incident in retail becomes a store, supplier, customer and food safety event within hours.

Retail disruption does not respect borders. The incidents in this report hit retailers and suppliers in the UK, the Netherlands, Belgium, France, Sweden, Ireland and the US, and the refrigeration attacks show the threat now reaches equipment most IT teams have never inventoried.6,8,9,10,22 In the UK alone, the NCSC's Annual Review records 80 nationally significant incidents in the year to August 2026, out of 337 that needed its support.36

A cyber incident in retail is rarely just an IT event: it becomes a store event, a supplier event, a customer event and often a food safety event within hours. It can quickly become a legal one too. Personal data breach notification deadlines of 72 hours apply under UK and EU GDPR and, where harm is likely, under Saudi Arabia's PDPL, and every US state has its own notification law.23,24,26,28 UK retailers in the BRC survey ranked cyber crime as their second biggest crime-related financial concern, after customer theft.11

The common thread is coordination. In each case, the hardest work was keeping stores trading safely while head office contained the problem: telling managers what to do, telling customers what still worked and making sure nobody was left guessing. That work depends on fast, two-way communication with every store and a clear record of who has been told what, and when.

Colleague safety sits alongside this. Violence and abuse are a daily reality in stores, and peak trading raises the pressure. In the UK, two new legal frameworks are on the way: retailers that improve reporting now will be ready to use the new offence when it commences, and those that write and rehearse their four Martyn's Law procedures will not be scrambling when the duties begin.

Peaks differ by market: Black Friday, called White Friday in parts of the Gulf, Christmas, and Ramadan and Eid, when Visa found late-night transactions doubled their share in Saudi Arabia.37,38 The test before each one is practical. Could every store acknowledge one clear instruction within ten minutes? Could you list every internet-facing fridge controller? Would you know who to notify, and by when, in each country you trade in?

Recommendations

Seven steps before peak

In priority order.
01

Give stores and head office one severity scale

Shared triggers, owners and update rhythm for stores and the crisis team.
02

Make every shutdown decision store-aware

Map what stores, online and supply chain lose with each system, and pre-write the store instruction.14,15
03

Get operational technology off the internet

Inventory fridge controllers and building systems, remove internet exposure and replace end-of-life kit.16,22
04

Harden people-based access

Train help desks and staff against impersonation and tighten contractor access.2,7
05

Pre-approve messages for every audience

Stores, colleagues, customers, regulators, suppliers and media, each with the next update time.
06

Report and support every assault

Make reporting easy, follow up every incident, prepare for the new offence.11,12
07

Rehearse before every peak

Exercise a payment outage, a cold-chain incident and a lockdown; in the UK, draft Martyn's Law procedures.13

Glossary

Terms used in this report

Push notification
A message sent by an app straight to a customer's phone screen.
Social engineering
Tricking a person into giving access, for example by impersonating a colleague or supplier.
Refrigeration controller
A device that runs fridges and freezers, often reachable remotely by engineers.
Defrost cycle
A scheduled warm-up that clears ice. Stretched or repeated, it spoils stock.
End of life
When a manufacturer stops issuing security fixes for a product.
Operational technology
Systems that control physical equipment, such as refrigeration, lighting or access.
Adjusting items
Costs a company reports separately from underlying profit, such as one-off incident costs.
Prospective
A provision that is law but has not yet been brought into force.
Invacuation
Moving people to a safer place inside a building, away from danger outside.
SIA
The UK Security Industry Authority, which will regulate Martyn's Law.
Standard and enhanced tier
UK Martyn's Law categories for premises expecting 200 to 799 people, and 800 or more.
Cold chain
Keeping chilled and frozen food at safe temperatures from supplier to shelf.
Supervisory authority
The data protection regulator that receives breach notifications.

Buyer's guide

Eight questions to ask any incident platform supplier

Many tools can send an alert. These questions separate a notification tool from a platform that coordinates the whole response.
01
Does it stop at the message?
Ask what the platform does after the alert is sent: tasks, log, escalation, recovery.
02
Can it prove who acknowledged?
Delivery is not acknowledgement. Ask how silence is spotted and escalated, and what you must configure.
03
Does it run your procedures?
Ask whether your plans become owned tasks with deadlines, or stay as documents.
04
Is there one shared picture?
Ask what leaders see live, and whether it needs someone to update a spreadsheet.
05
Does it work when your systems do not?
Ask how teams coordinate if email, chat or your network is down.
06
Can frontline staff raise the alarm?
Ask how a colleague on site escalates, with their location where available, in one action.
07
What evidence does it leave behind?
Ask for a sample timeline of the kind a regulator, insurer or inquiry would want.
08
Can you rehearse in it?
Ask whether you can run exercises on the same platform you would use for real.
