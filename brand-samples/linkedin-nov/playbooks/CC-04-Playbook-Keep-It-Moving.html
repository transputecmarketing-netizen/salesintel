Foreword

In transport, nobody fails alone. One fault becomes everyone's problem within the hour.

On 21 March 2025 Heathrow closed for most of a day after a fire at the North Hyde substation in west London. More than 270,000 passenger journeys were disrupted.1 The official review found that losing one of the airport's three supply points cut power to some operationally critical systems, and that reconfiguring the airport's network to run on the other two would take an estimated 10 to 12 hours.2

Six months later a ransomware attack on a check-in system supplier forced staff at Heathrow, Brussels and Berlin back to handwritten boarding passes.3 A cyber attack halted production at Jaguar Land Rover for five weeks and left its suppliers waiting for orders and payments.4,5 And on 8 September 2026 a software defect in the UK's air traffic system delayed, cancelled or diverted more than 2,000 flights.6

This playbook is for the people who hold it together when that happens: operations, resilience, security, safety and communications leaders in airports, ports, rail, bus and coach, freight and logistics. It sets out how to grade and escalate an incident, who decides what, what to do in the first hour, how to keep drivers and lone workers safe and how to bring contractors and partners into one response. It also gives you tools to use: a roles matrix, check-in and notification logs, a tabletop exercise, an actions tracker and a review template. The companion intelligence report sets out the evidence in full.

Introduction

Three things keep a network moving on a bad day.

Transport and logistics run on tight timings, shared assets and many hands. When something breaks, the operators that recover fastest are not the ones with the thickest plans. They are the ones that know where the network is fragile, who must hear first and how to reach every person in it.

Know your single points of failure

One power intake, one planning system, one supplier or one control room can stop a whole network. Find them, test the fallback and know how long it lasts.

Reach everyone, by more than one route

Staff, drivers, contractors, partners, customers and passengers each need the right message, fast, with a record of who has acknowledged and who has not.

People before payload

Every decision starts with the safety of passengers, crews, drivers and lone workers. Freight can wait in a yard. A driver who has stopped answering cannot.

Before it hits

Your network is only as strong as the parts you do not own

Incident management in this sector is mostly about the space between organisations.

A transport or logistics operation is a chain of dependencies. Power comes from a network operator. Planning, booking and check-in run on systems built by suppliers. Track, airspace, roads and berths belong to someone else. Drivers, handlers and engineers are often contractors or agency staff. When one link fails, the effect lands somewhere else: at a gate, a loading bay, a platform or a lay-by.

The recent reviews make the point clearly. At Heathrow, the risk that one supply point might fail was understood at a technical level but less well known outside the technical team.7 After the 2023 failure of the UK's flight planning system, several airlines and airports first heard about it from the media.8 In September 2025, airports that had done nothing wrong lost their check-in desks because a supplier was attacked.3

So incident management in this sector is mostly about the space between organisations. Who tells the partner sites? Who tells the drivers already on the road? Who decides to divert, and who tells the customer whose delivery will now be late? Who checks that the contractor on the night shift is out of the building?

That means three things have to be settled before anything happens. A severity scale that every site and partner understands. A map of who must be told, in what order, by what route. And a clear way to account for people, from passengers in a terminal to a driver parked alone at a closed depot.

None of this is exotic. Much of it is in the recommendations of the reviews that followed Heathrow and the air traffic failures: a second way to reach key people, earlier warnings to partners and a standard set of information for passengers.1,9 The work is in making it true across sites, shifts and suppliers before winter, not during the next incident.

Readiness

Readiness in six steps

Each step comes from a lesson in a recent official review. Finish them before the winter peak.
01

Map your single points of failure

Power intakes, control rooms, planning and check-in systems, fuel and key suppliers. At Heathrow, losing one of three supply points cut power to critical systems.2
02

Test backup for duration, not just start-up

Find out how long each backup source lasts. Heathrow's review found nearly all emergency power on the affected supply would have run out before main power returned.7
03

Give every key person a second route

The Heathrow review recommended a second means of contact for significant incidents, in addition to the existing alert calls.1
04

Check on-call staff can actually act

In 2023 an on-call engineer could not log in remotely and reached the control centre 3 hours 15 minutes after the failure began.8
05

Agree who tells partners, and how fast

After the 2023 air traffic failure, several airlines and airports first heard of it from the media.8
06

Set weather triggers in advance

Decide which warnings cancel which services. In a red warning, police advise people to avoid travel in the affected area.10

Rerouting and information

Rerouting and keeping people informed

The 2023 air traffic review asked airlines and airports to develop a standardised suite of passenger information during major incidents.9 The same discipline works for freight customers.

Decide the route, then say it once

Agree the diversion or alternative with the receiving site before announcing it. One instruction, one owner, one time stamp, sent to every site and driver affected.

Tell people what to do

Lead with the action: do not travel, rebook, collect from another depot, expect delivery tomorrow. Then explain the cause, if you know it.

Set the next update and keep it

Give a time for the next update, even if there will be nothing new. Silence is when rumours start and phone lines jam.

Reach people without data

Not everyone has the app. Use SMS, station and terminal staff, partner desks and recorded phone lines alongside the website.

Glossary

Words worth knowing

Severity level
A shared grade (SEV 1 to SEV 4) that sets who is alerted, how fast and how often.
Single point of failure
One component, site, system or supplier whose loss stops the whole operation.
Supply point
A connection to the electricity network. Large sites may have several, each feeding different systems.
Backup duration
How long a generator, battery or other fallback can run before it is exhausted.
Lone worker
Anyone who works without close or direct supervision, such as a driver or night security officer.
Welfare check
A planned contact to confirm a lone worker or driver is safe.
Trunking
Moving freight in bulk between hubs, usually overnight.
Diversion
Sending vehicles, freight or passengers through another site or route.
Manual fallback
A paper or offline way of doing a task when the system is down.
NIS Regulations
The Network and Information Systems Regulations 2018, which set security and incident reporting duties for essential services, including transport.
Competent authority
The regulator for an essential service under the NIS Regulations.
Holding line
A short, accurate statement used while facts are still being established.
Acknowledgement
A recipient's confirmation that an alert has been read and acted on.
Incident manager
The person who runs the response, keeps the log and sets the rhythm of updates.

Buyer's guide

Eight questions to ask any incident platform supplier

Many tools can send an alert. These questions separate a notification tool from a platform that coordinates the whole response.
01
Does it stop at the message?
Ask what the platform does after the alert is sent: tasks, log, escalation, recovery.
02
Can it prove who acknowledged?
Delivery is not acknowledgement. Ask how silence is detected and escalated.
03
Does it run your procedures?
Ask whether your plans become owned tasks with deadlines, or stay as documents.
04
Is there one shared picture?
Ask what leaders see live, and whether it needs someone to update a spreadsheet.
05
Does it work when your systems do not?
Ask how teams coordinate if email, chat or your network is down.
06
Can frontline staff raise the alarm?
Ask how a colleague on site escalates, with their location, in one action.
07
What evidence does it leave behind?
Ask for a sample timeline of the kind a regulator, insurer or inquiry would want.
08
Can you rehearse in it?
Ask whether you can run exercises on the same platform you would use for real.
