Foreword

Responsible AI is not a brake. It is what lets you keep going.

Most AI regrets are not caused by the technology failing. They are caused by decisions nobody quite made: a tool adopted without checking where the data went, a model trusted with a decision it was never tested for, a customer who was never told a machine was involved.

None of this needs a large compliance function or a thick policy manual. It needs a named owner, a short set of rules people can follow, a sensible way of judging which uses carry real risk, and the habit of checking that the guardrails still work.

This playbook sets out that practical middle ground. It covers governance, acceptable use, data protection under UK GDPR, risk assessment, human oversight, fairness, transparency, supplier checks and incident handling, and shows how the main frameworks fit together. It is written for leaders, not lawyers, so treat it as a guide and take specialist advice where the stakes are high.

Introduction

Move quickly where it is safe. Slow down where it matters.

Not every use of AI needs the same scrutiny. Drafting an internal email is not the same as screening job applicants or deciding who gets credit. Responsible adoption means matching the effort to the risk, so low-risk use flows freely and high-risk use gets the care it deserves.

Make someone accountable

A named owner, with authority and time, turns good intentions into decisions that actually get made.

Match controls to risk

Light rules for everyday use, deeper assessment for anything affecting people's rights, money or safety.

Keep people in charge

Humans review, decide and answer for the outcomes. AI informs the decision, it does not own it.

Why it matters

Governance is how you say yes with confidence

Clear rules do not slow AI adoption down. Uncertainty does.

For many leaders, the word governance conjures up committees, forms and delay. With AI, the opposite is usually true. Organisations without clear rules tend to stall, because nobody is sure what is allowed, so cautious people do nothing and bolder people do whatever they like.

Good governance answers a handful of questions so that everyone else does not have to. Which tools are approved? What data can go into them? Which uses need a closer look, and who signs them off? What happens when something goes wrong? Once those answers exist, most AI use can proceed without anyone asking permission.

The risks it guards against are real but ordinary. Personal or confidential data shared with a tool that keeps it. Decisions that treat some groups unfairly. Customers misled about whether they are dealing with a person. Outputs relied on without checking. Contracts that leave you exposed when a supplier changes its terms. Each of these has caught out organisations that were simply moving fast.

Regulation is part of the picture, and it is still taking shape. In the UK, existing law already applies: data protection, equality, consumer protection and sector rules do not pause because AI is involved. The EU has a dedicated AI Act, which can reach UK businesses that sell into the EU. Rules and guidance are changing, so build governance that can absorb change rather than chasing each update.

The good news is that you do not need to invent anything. Established frameworks such as the NIST AI Risk Management Framework and ISO/IEC 42001 set out what good looks like. Most organisations borrow the parts they need and scale them to their size.

Start small and real. One owner, one page of policy, a register of where AI is used and a quarterly review will put you ahead of many organisations, and give you something to build on as use grows.

Building blocks

Four building blocks of AI governance

Get these in place and most of the rest follows. Each can start as a single page and grow with use.

An accountable owner

A senior person who owns AI risk and adoption, with a small group drawn from IT, legal, data protection, HR and the business.

A short policy

An acceptable use policy in plain English: approved tools, data rules, what needs review and where to ask questions.

An AI register

A simple list of where AI is used, by whom, with what data, at what risk level and with what controls.

A regular review

A quarterly look at new uses, incidents, supplier changes and whether the controls still work in practice.
If you only do one thing this month, name the owner. Everything else depends on someone having the job.

01

The acceptable use policy

The single most useful governance document you can write. Keep it short enough to read in five minutes, and specific enough to answer real questions.
Approved tools
Name the AI tools staff may use for work, how to get access and what to do if they need something not on the list.
Data rules
Say plainly what must never go into AI tools, such as customer personal data in unapproved tools, and what is fine.
Checking and accountability
Make clear that people own anything they produce with AI, must check it and must not present it as unreviewed fact.
Uses that need sign-off
List the uses that need review first, such as anything affecting hiring, pay, credit, health or customer decisions.
Owner
Your AI owner, with HR and data protection
Bring this to the monthly review, with a date for each action.

Risk tiers

Sort every use by impact and autonomy

Two questions decide how much scrutiny a use needs: how much harm could a wrong output cause, and how far does it act without a person checking?
Assess carefully

High impact, human decides

Hiring, credit, health or legal support where a trained person makes the call. Needs a full assessment.
Highest scrutiny

High impact, AI acts alone

Automated decisions about people. Restricted by law in many cases. Senior sign-off, or do not do it.
Allow freely

Low impact, human decides

Drafting, summarising and research that a person reviews. Covered by the acceptable use policy.
Control and monitor

Low impact, AI acts alone

Routing, tagging or simple automations. Fine with testing, monitoring and an easy way to correct errors.

Data protection

Personal data does not stop being personal inside an AI tool

If you would need a DPIA for a new system, you need one for a new AI system.

If an AI system processes personal data, UK GDPR applies in full. That covers obvious cases such as analysing customer records, and less obvious ones such as staff pasting emails, CVs or meeting notes into an assistant.

Start with the basics you already know. You need a lawful basis for the processing, and you must be able to explain it. Use only the data you need for the purpose. Tell people how their data is used, in language they can understand. Keep it secure, keep it no longer than necessary, and make sure people can still exercise their rights.

A data protection impact assessment is required where processing is likely to result in a high risk to individuals, and AI often meets that bar. Even where it is not strictly required, a short DPIA is the most practical way to think through what data is used, what could go wrong and what controls you need. The ICO publishes guidance on AI and data protection that is worth reading alongside it.

Pay particular attention to decisions made solely by automated means that have legal or similarly significant effects, such as refusing credit or rejecting a job applicant. The law gives people specific protections here, including the right to human involvement and to contest the outcome. These rules have been updated by recent legislation, so check current ICO guidance before relying on an automated decision.

Suppliers matter too. When you use an AI service, the provider usually acts as your processor, so you need a contract that covers how they handle the data, which sub-processors they use, where data is stored and whether it can be used to train their models. Business editions and consumer editions of the same tool often have very different terms.

Finally, think about what goes in by accident. Clear rules, approved tools and simple training prevent most problems, because most data leaks into AI tools are well-meaning colleagues trying to save time.

Risk assessment

A proportionate assessment in five steps

Use this for any use that lands outside the low-risk corner. For most uses it fits on two pages.

Describe

What the AI does, who uses it, who it affects and what decision or output it feeds.

Map the data

What data goes in, where it is processed and stored, and whether any of it is personal or confidential.

Find the harms

What happens if it is wrong, biased, unavailable or misused, and who would be affected.

Set controls

Human review, testing, access limits, monitoring and a way for people to challenge outcomes.

Decide and record

The owner approves, rejects or asks for changes, and sets a date to review it.
Record the decision in your AI register, with the owner and the review date. An undocumented approval is hard to defend.

In practice

Three uses that need more than a policy

HR

Screening job applications

The risk
Unfair treatment of candidates and discrimination claims under the Equality Act.
Key controls
DPIA, bias testing across groups, human review of every rejection.
Tell people
Explain to applicants that AI is used and how to ask for a human review.
Watch out
Historical hiring data can teach a model old biases.
Customer service

A chatbot answering customers

The risk
Wrong answers given with confidence, and customers misled about who they are talking to.
Key controls
Answers drawn from approved content, clear limits and an easy route to a person.
Tell people
Say plainly that it is an AI assistant, at the start of the conversation.
Watch out
Promises the bot makes may be treated as promises your business made.
Finance

Prioritising debt collection

The risk
Vulnerable customers treated harshly by an automated score.
Key controls
Senior sign-off, regular outcome reviews and checks for vulnerability.
Tell people
Explain how decisions are made and how to challenge them.
Watch out
Sector regulators expect good customer outcomes whatever tool you use.

Supplier checks

Due diligence for every AI supplier

Proportionate checks before you sign, scaled to the risk of the use. Ask in writing and compare answers with the contract.
01

Confirm the use and the data

Be clear what the tool will do and what data it will see, so you ask the right questions.
02

Check data terms

Training use, retention, location, sub-processors and deletion, confirmed in the contract.
03

Ask how they test

How the supplier tests for accuracy, bias and misuse, and how often models change.
04

Look for assurance

Independent certifications, security documentation and help with your own DPIA.
05

Plan for change and exit

Notice of model or term changes, and how you leave with your data.
06

Record and review

Add the supplier to your AI register with an owner and a renewal check.
Remember
The same tool can carry very different terms in its consumer and business editions. Check which one your people are actually using.

Incidents

When AI gets it wrong

Something will go wrong eventually. A calm, rehearsed response limits the harm and builds trust. Fold these steps into the incident process you already have.

Make it easy to report

Staff and customers need an obvious way to flag a bad output, an unfair outcome or data in the wrong place.

Know how to pause

Decide in advance who can switch off a tool or feature, and how quickly it can be done.

Find the cause

Was it the data, the model, the prompt, the process or the person? The fix depends on the answer.

Meet your duties

Personal data breaches may need reporting to the ICO within strict deadlines. Affected people may need telling.
Treat near misses as gifts. Each one shows you a weak control before it causes real harm.

Principles

Five habits of responsible AI adopters

Organisations that avoid AI regrets tend to share the same habits, regardless of size or sector.
01
Be proportionate
Spend scrutiny where the impact on people is highest, and let everyday use flow.
02
Keep a person answerable
Every AI-assisted decision has a named human owner who can explain it.
03
Be open by default
Tell people when AI is involved in decisions about them, and how to reach a person.
04
Train for judgement
Teach people what the policy says and why, and how to spot a wrong or unfair output.
05
Review and adapt
Revisit uses, suppliers and rules regularly. The technology and the law both keep moving.

Glossary

Words worth knowing

AI governance
The roles, rules and reviews that decide how AI is used in an organisation.
Acceptable use policy
The plain-language rules for how staff may use AI tools and data.
AI register
A record of every AI use, its data, risk level, owner and controls.
DPIA
Data protection impact assessment, used to assess and reduce risks to individuals from processing personal data.
Automated decision
A decision made without meaningful human involvement. Special legal protections apply where effects are significant.
Human in the loop
A person reviews and can change AI output before it is used.
Bias
Systematic differences in outcomes that disadvantage particular groups.
Processor
A supplier that processes personal data on your behalf and under your instructions.
NIST AI RMF
A voluntary US framework for managing AI risk: govern, map, measure, manage.
ISO/IEC 42001
An international, certifiable standard for AI management systems.
EU AI Act
The EU's risk-based AI law, which can apply to UK firms serving EU markets.
Contestability
People's ability to challenge an AI-assisted outcome and reach a person.
