Executive summary

One laboratory outage. Two years of recovery.

United Kingdom: on 3 June 2024 a ransomware attack on Synnovis, which provides pathology services to major hospitals in south east London, stopped normal blood testing. Guy's and St Thomas' and King's College Hospital declared a critical incident the next morning, with blood transfusions particularly affected.1 NHS England later reported that 10,152 acute outpatient appointments and 1,710 elective procedures were postponed at the two trusts.2

The effects travelled far beyond the laboratory. The national blood service appealed for O type donors within a week and in July 2024 issued a national amber alert, saying the London hospitals had needed 94 per cent more O negative blood than a year earlier.3,4 In June 2025 King's College Hospital confirmed that a long wait for a blood test result caused by the attack was one of the factors that contributed to a patient's death.5 Almost two years on, one provider was still in business continuity for pathology.6

The pattern is international. United States: in 2024 a ransomware attack on Change Healthcare disrupted claims and payments for providers and, UnitedHealth later estimated, affected the data of about 190 million people; an attack on Ascension forced several hospitals to divert ambulances and move to paper records.7,8 And not every incident is cyber: in 2026 English hospitals declared critical incidents over IT failures and heat-related cooling failures.9,10

This report sets out the evidence, compares it with earlier lessons and the rules in the UK, the US, the EU and the Middle East, and ends with practical recommendations for any hospital or health system.

The bottom line
Assume that a clinical system will fail. Declare early in agreed words, reach and confirm the right staff in minutes, run practised downtime procedures with your suppliers, and keep patients and partners informed until the last result is back in the record.

The attack

From laboratory to ward in one morning

Pathology sits underneath almost everything a hospital does.

Synnovis is a pathology partnership that runs laboratory services for hospitals and GP practices in south east London, in the UK. On Monday 3 June 2024 a ransomware attack took its systems offline. By Tuesday morning Guy's and St Thomas' and King's College Hospital had declared a critical incident, and the chief executive of one trust told staff that blood transfusions were particularly affected.1

Pathology sits underneath almost everything a hospital does. Without results, surgeons cannot confirm a patient is fit for theatre, emergency teams wait longer to rule conditions in or out, and family doctors cannot manage long-term conditions. NHS England's tally of 10,152 outpatient appointments and 1,710 elective procedures postponed at the two trusts captures only part of that.2

Blood was the most visible pressure. NHS Blood and Transplant said the affected hospitals could not match patients' blood at the usual frequency, so they relied on O type blood, which is safe for all patients.3 On 25 July it issued a national amber alert: O negative stocks stood at 1.6 days and the London hospitals had needed an extra 1.7 days of O negative since June.4 The alert was stood down from 21 July 2025, almost a year later.19

Harm followed. Reporting in June 2025 described nearly 600 incidents linked to the attack, 170 of them involving harm to patients, most of it low harm.11 The same month King's College Hospital said a patient safety investigation had identified a long wait for a blood test result due to the attack as one of several contributing factors in a patient's death.5

Then came the long tail. Synnovis said in November 2025 that its review of the stolen data was complete; it described the data as unstructured, incomplete and fragmented.20 In June 2026 Bedfordshire Hospitals confirmed that data on 32,927 of its patients had been taken.18 And in April 2026 one mental health trust in south London was still in business continuity, with 122 recorded patient safety incidents involving incorrect, unavailable or delayed results.6

None of this is a judgement on the people who responded. Clinicians, scientists and managers worked around the clock. It is a measure of how deeply one system runs through modern care, in any country.

The pattern

What WannaCry taught, and what it did not

The common thread is not the cause. It is the first hour.

On Friday 12 May 2017 the WannaCry ransomware spread worldwide. In England, the National Audit Office found that at least 81 of the 236 NHS trusts were affected, along with 603 primary care and other NHS organisations, including 595 GP practices.16 NHS England recorded 6,912 cancelled appointments and estimated that over 19,000 would have been cancelled in total. Five A&E departments diverted patients.16

The NAO's findings on the response read as a checklist for today. The Department of Health had a plan with defined roles, but had not tested it at a local level. Many local organisations could not reach national bodies by email, and locally staff shared information through personal mobile devices, including WhatsApp.16

Synnovis was different in one important way: the system that failed belonged to a partner. That pattern repeats. United States: the February 2024 attack on Change Healthcare, a claims and payments business, left providers unable to get paid, verify insurance or file prior authorisations.7 United Kingdom: an ICO penalty notice in March 2025 fined Advanced, a software supplier, about £3.07m after a 2022 ransomware attack that disrupted NHS 111.21

Hospital systems are hit directly too. In May 2024 a cyber attack on Ascension, which operates 140 hospitals in 19 US states, took its electronic records offline; several hospitals diverted ambulances and staff moved to paper.8 In the UK, Wirral declared a major incident for cyber security reasons in November 2024.17

And not every critical incident is a cyber attack. In June 2026 a Portsmouth hospital declared a critical incident when chillers failed in a heatwave, affecting digital systems, theatres, cardiac catheter laboratories and scanning; another trust lost its MRI scanners to the heat.10,12 In July 2026 East Kent Hospitals declared one over IT problems without, at first, knowing the cause.9

The common thread is not the cause. It is the first hour: who declares, who is called in, how staff and patients are told, and whether care can carry on without the system.

The UK framework

United Kingdom: what NHS England's framework expects

The tools exist. The question is whether they are practised.

This page describes England. Every NHS-funded organisation there works to the NHS Emergency Preparedness, Resilience and Response Framework. Version 3, published in July 2022, defines a business continuity incident, a critical incident and a major incident, and sets incident response levels from 1, managed within one organisation, to 4, led nationally by NHS England.22

A critical incident, in the framework's words, is any localised incident where the level of disruption results in an organisation temporarily or permanently losing its ability to deliver critical services, or where patients and staff may be at risk of harm. It is principally an internal escalation response, and the framework asks organisations to use the format “Critical Incident declared by (organisation)”.22

The framework also sets out standard major incident messages, the METHANE and SBAR formats for sharing information, a 24/7 on-call system with a director able to allocate resources, and notification to the integrated care board, which informs NHS England through regional on-call. It expects a hot debrief within 48 hours of stand down and a structured debrief within 28 days.22

Compliance is tested every year against the NHS Core Standards for EPRR. The NHS Standard Contract requires providers of NHS-funded services, including independent providers, to comply with EPRR guidance.22 NHS England grades organisations from fully compliant, meeting 100 per cent of the relevant standards, through substantial and partial to non-compliant, and its 2026/27 process keeps the approach of previous years.23 In the 2024 round, one acute trust reported assessing itself against 62 core standards, plus a deep dive of 11 standards on cyber security.24

Cyber incidents carry extra duties. NHS trusts, foundation trusts, integrated care boards and certain independent providers are operators of essential services under the NIS Regulations, and must report network and information system incidents through the DSPT no later than 72 hours after becoming aware.14 The Cyber Security and Resilience Bill proposes an initial notice within 24 hours and a full report within 72 hours; those measures would follow Royal Assent and secondary legislation.15

Pressure from demand is measured separately, through the OPEL framework, which leaves declaring an incident as a decision for the provider under the EPRR framework.25 The tools exist. The question is whether they are practised.

Regional notes

How the rules differ: preparedness and reporting

The first hour looks the same everywhere. The rules around it do not. A summary of what applies to hospitals in each region, and how fast a cyber or data incident must be reported.
Regional note · United Kingdom
England: NHS-funded providers work to the EPRR Framework and annual Core Standards assurance.22,23 Operators of essential services in health report network and information system incidents through the DSPT within 72 hours; a Bill proposes a 24-hour initial notice.14,15
IN PRACTICE Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland have their own arrangements. Data breaches can need a separate report to the data protection regulator.
Regional note · United States
Medicare and Medicaid hospitals must meet the CMS emergency preparedness rule, 42 CFR 482.15: all-hazards plan, communication plan, training and testing, reviewed at least every 2 years, with exercises twice a year.26 The Joint Commission's revised Emergency Management standards apply to accredited hospitals from July 2022.27 HIPAA breach notices to individuals are due within 60 days of discovery.28
IN PRACTICE Many hospitals run their response with the Hospital Incident Command System (HICS).29
Regional note · European Union
Preparedness is set nationally. Under NIS2, health is a sector of high criticality: an early warning within 24 hours of becoming aware of a significant incident, a notification within 72 hours and a final report within one month.13 The CER Directive covers health; critical entities were to be identified by 17 July 2026.30
IN PRACTICE Both directives work through national law, transposed from 17 October 2024. Check your hospital's status with your national authority.
Regional note · Middle East (UAE and Saudi Arabia)
Dubai: the DHA healthcare emergencies framework, effective 14 January 2025, requires annual hazard analysis, an emergency operations centre for hospitals, a 24/7 contact for DHA and at least one exercise a year.31 Abu Dhabi launched a healthcare business continuity standard in May 2025.32 The UAE national standard is AE/SCNS/NCEMA 7000:2021.33
IN PRACTICE Saudi Arabia: CBAHI accreditation is linked to hospital licence renewal.34 JCI surveys use its 8th edition hospital standards from 1 January 2025.35
A summary for orientation, not legal advice. Requirements depend on sector, size, licence and jurisdiction, and change over time. Confirm the current text with your legal and compliance teams.

What it means

What it means for organisations in healthcare

If your laboratory system went dark at 08:10 tomorrow, who would declare?

For hospitals, the first lesson is that resilience now runs through partners. Pathology networks, imaging, electronic records, claims and payments, estates and community services are often delivered by other organisations. If they are not on your incident call lists, and you are not on theirs, the first hour will be spent finding phone numbers.1,7

For health systems, the second lesson is scale. Ascension's attack took offline the electronic records of a system with 140 hospitals in 19 states; Synnovis touched every hospital and practice that relied on one laboratory.2,8 Incident command has to work across sites, with one picture of who is coming in and what is still open.

For boards, the question is not whether a plan exists. The NAO's finding after WannaCry was that the plan had not been tested locally.16 Regulators and accreditors ask for exercises; in the US, CMS expects at least two a year.26 Boards should ask for evidence: time to declare, time to confirm staff, time to the first paper result.

For seasonal peaks, the risks stack. An IT failure on a day of full emergency departments and staff sickness leaves little slack. Linking your escalation levels to incident triggers and call-in lists means the response grows with the pressure.

For compliance, the clocks are short and they differ: 24 hours for an early warning under NIS2, 72 hours under England's NIS rules, 60 days for HIPAA breach notices to individuals.13,14,28 Know which apply before the day.

The practical test is simple. If your laboratory system went dark at 08:10 tomorrow, who would declare, how quickly would you know who was coming in, and could every ward get an urgent result on paper?

Glossary

Terms used in this report

EPRR
Emergency preparedness, resilience and response: the NHS England framework for incidents (UK).
Business continuity incident
Disruption to normal service below acceptable levels that needs special arrangements.
Critical incident
Localised loss of ability to deliver critical services, or risk of harm to patients and staff.
Major incident
An event with serious consequences needing special arrangements by one or more responder agencies.
HICS
Hospital Incident Command System: an incident command framework for hospitals, used widely in the US.
Core Standards
The minimum EPRR requirements NHS-funded organisations in England assess themselves against each year.
Pathology
Laboratory testing of blood, tissue and other samples.
O negative
The universal donor blood type, used when a patient's blood cannot be matched.
Ransomware
Malicious software that locks or steals data to extort payment.
NIS and NIS2
UK regulations and an EU directive that set security and incident reporting duties for essential services, including health.
DSPT
The Data Security and Protection Toolkit, used to report incidents in health and care in England.
JCI and CBAHI
Joint Commission International and Saudi Arabia's national accreditation body for healthcare.

Buyer's guide

Eight questions to ask any incident platform supplier

Many tools can send an alert. These questions separate a notification tool from a platform that coordinates the whole response.
01
Does it stop at the message?
Ask what the platform does after the alert is sent: tasks, log, escalation, recovery.
02
Can it prove who acknowledged?
Delivery is not acknowledgement. Ask how silence is spotted and escalated, and what you must configure.
03
Does it run your procedures?
Ask whether your plans become owned tasks with deadlines, or stay as documents.
04
Is there one shared picture?
Ask what leaders see live, and whether it needs someone to update a spreadsheet.
05
Does it work when your systems do not?
Ask how teams coordinate if email, chat or your network is down.
06
Can frontline staff raise the alarm?
Ask how a colleague on site escalates, with their location where available, in one action.
07
What evidence does it leave behind?
Ask for a sample timeline of the kind a regulator, insurer or inquiry would want.
08
Can you rehearse in it?
Ask whether you can run exercises on the same platform you would use for real.
