Executive summary

One laboratory outage. Two years of recovery.

On 3 June 2024 a ransomware attack on Synnovis, which provides pathology services to major hospitals in south east London, stopped normal blood testing. Guy's and St Thomas' and King's College Hospital declared a critical incident the next morning, with blood transfusions particularly affected.1 NHS England later reported that 10,152 acute outpatient appointments and 1,710 elective procedures were postponed at the two trusts.2

The effects travelled far beyond the laboratory. NHS Blood and Transplant appealed for O type donors within a week and in July 2024 issued a national amber alert, saying the London hospitals had needed 94 per cent more O negative blood than a year earlier.3,4 The alert was not stood down until July 2025.5 In June 2025 King's College Hospital confirmed that a long wait for a blood test result caused by the attack was one of the factors that contributed to a patient's death.6

The recovery was long. In early 2026 one mental health trust estimated that entering 161,560 pathology reports into patient records had been delayed, and patient data stolen in the attack was still being confirmed to trusts in 2026.7,8 Meanwhile trusts declared critical incidents for IT failures and heat-related cooling failures in the summer of 2026.9,10

This report sets out the evidence, compares it with the lessons of WannaCry and the NHS EPRR framework, and ends with practical recommendations.

The bottom line
Assume that a clinical system will fail. Declare early in the agreed words, reach and confirm the right staff in minutes, run practised downtime procedures with your suppliers, and keep patients and partners informed until the last result is back in the record.

The attack

From laboratory to ward in one morning

Pathology sits underneath almost everything a hospital does.

Synnovis is a pathology partnership that runs laboratory services for hospitals and GP practices in south east London. On Monday 3 June 2024 a ransomware attack took its systems offline. By Tuesday morning Guy's and St Thomas' and King's College Hospital had declared a critical incident, and the chief executive of one trust told staff that blood transfusions were particularly affected.1

Pathology sits underneath almost everything a hospital does. Without results, surgeons cannot confirm a patient is fit for theatre, emergency teams wait longer to rule conditions in or out, and GPs cannot manage long-term conditions. NHS England's tally of 10,152 outpatient appointments and 1,710 elective procedures postponed at the two trusts captures only part of that.2

Blood was the most visible pressure. NHS Blood and Transplant said the affected hospitals could not match patients' blood at the usual frequency, so they relied on O type blood, which is safe for all patients.3 On 25 July it issued a national amber alert: O negative stocks stood at 1.6 days and the London hospitals had needed an extra 1.7 days of O negative since June.4 The alert was stood down from 21 July 2025, almost a year later.5

Harm followed. Reporting in June 2025 described nearly 600 incidents linked to the attack, 170 of them involving harm to patients, most of it low harm.11 The same month King's College Hospital said a patient safety investigation had identified a long wait for a blood test result due to the attack as one of several contributing factors in a patient's death.6

Then came the long tail. Synnovis said in November 2025 that its review of the stolen data was complete; it described the data as unstructured, incomplete and fragmented.18 In June 2026 Bedfordshire Hospitals confirmed that data on 32,927 of its patients had been taken.8 And in April 2026 one mental health trust in south London was still in business continuity, with 122 recorded patient safety incidents involving incorrect, unavailable or delayed results.7

None of this is a judgement on the people who responded. Clinicians, scientists and managers worked around the clock. It is a measure of how deeply one system runs through modern care.

The pattern

What WannaCry taught, and what it did not

The common thread is not the cause. It is the first hour.

On Friday 12 May 2017 the WannaCry ransomware spread worldwide. The National Audit Office found that at least 81 of the 236 trusts in England were affected, along with 603 primary care and other NHS organisations, including 595 GP practices.12 NHS England recorded 6,912 cancelled appointments and estimated that over 19,000 would have been cancelled in total. Five A&E departments diverted patients.12

The NAO's findings on the response read as a checklist for today. The Department had a plan with defined roles, but had not tested it at a local level. Many local organisations could not reach national NHS bodies by email, and locally staff shared information through personal mobile devices, including WhatsApp.12

The NAO also judged that the attack was relatively unsophisticated and could have been prevented by following basic IT security best practice.12 Since then the government has set a goal for all health and social care organisations to achieve cyber resilience no later than 2030.19

Synnovis was different in one important way: the system that failed belonged to a partner. That pattern has repeated. An ICO penalty notice in March 2025 fined Advanced, a software supplier, about £3.07m after a 2022 ransomware attack that disrupted NHS 111 and exposed data, including details of how to gain entry to the homes of 890 people receiving care.20 In February 2025 HCRG Care Group, a large independent provider of NHS community services, confirmed it was investigating an IT security incident while services continued.21

And not every critical incident is a cyber attack. In June 2026 a Portsmouth hospital declared a critical incident when chillers failed in a heatwave, affecting digital systems, theatres, cardiac catheter laboratories and scanning; another trust lost its MRI scanners to the heat.10,13 In July 2026 East Kent Hospitals declared one over IT problems without, at first, knowing the cause.9

The common thread is not the cause. It is the first hour: who declares, who is called in, how staff and patients are told, and whether care can carry on without the system.

The framework

What the NHS framework expects

The tools exist. The question is whether they are practised.

Every NHS-funded organisation works to the NHS Emergency Preparedness, Resilience and Response Framework. Version 3, published in July 2022, defines a business continuity incident, a critical incident and a major incident, and sets incident response levels from 1, managed within one organisation, to 4, led nationally by NHS England.16

A critical incident, in the framework's words, is any localised incident where the level of disruption results in an organisation temporarily or permanently losing its ability to deliver critical services, or where patients and staff may be at risk of harm. It is principally an internal escalation response, and the framework asks organisations to use the format “Critical Incident declared by (organisation)”.16

The framework also sets out standard major incident messages, the METHANE and SBAR formats for sharing information, a 24/7 on-call system with a director able to allocate resources, and notification to the ICB, which informs NHS England through regional on-call. It expects a hot debrief within 48 hours of stand down and a structured debrief within 28 days.16

Compliance is tested every year against the NHS Core Standards for EPRR. The NHS Standard Contract requires providers of NHS-funded services, including independent providers, to comply with EPRR guidance.16 NHS England grades organisations from fully compliant, meeting 100 per cent of the relevant standards, through substantial and partial to non-compliant, and its 2026/27 process keeps the approach of previous years.22 In the 2024 round, one acute trust reported assessing itself against 62 core standards, plus a deep dive of 11 standards on cyber security.23

Cyber incidents carry extra duties. NHS trusts, foundation trusts, ICBs and certain independent providers are operators of essential services under the NIS Regulations, and must report network and information system incidents through the DSPT no later than 72 hours after becoming aware.14 The Cyber Security and Resilience Bill proposes an initial notice within 24 hours and a full report within 72 hours; those measures would follow Royal Assent and secondary legislation.15

Pressure from demand is measured separately, through the OPEL framework, which makes declaring an incident a decision for the provider under the EPRR framework.24 The tools exist. The question is whether they are practised.

UK relevance

What it means for UK organisations in healthcare

If your laboratory system went dark at 08:10 tomorrow, who would declare?

For NHS trusts, the lesson of Synnovis is that resilience now runs through partners. Pathology networks, imaging, electronic records, estates and community services are often delivered by other organisations. If they are not on your incident call lists, and you are not on theirs, the first hour will be spent finding phone numbers.1,18

For independent providers with NHS contracts, the expectations are the same. The NHS Standard Contract requires compliance with EPRR guidance, and certain independent providers are operators of essential services under the NIS Regulations.14,16 The 2025 attack on HCRG Care Group showed that the threat does not distinguish between sectors.21

For boards, the question is not whether a plan exists. The NAO's finding after WannaCry was that the plan had not been tested locally.12 Annual EPRR assurance asks the question every year; boards should ask it more often, and ask for evidence: time to declare, time to confirm staff, time to first paper result.22

For winter, the risks stack. An IT failure on a day at the highest OPEL level, with staff sickness and full emergency departments, leaves little slack. Linking OPEL levels to your incident triggers and call-in lists means the response grows with the pressure.24

For staff, long incidents are exhausting. Synnovis and its aftermath ran for months. Rotas, welfare checks and a clear stand down matter as much as the first alert.

The practical test is simple. If your laboratory system went dark at 08:10 tomorrow, who would declare, how quickly would you know who was coming in, and could every ward get an urgent result on paper?

Recommendations

Seven steps for healthcare providers

In priority order. The first three can start this week.
01

Write declaration triggers

Use the EPRR definitions and wording; name who can declare.16
02

Call staff in by role, and confirm

Alerts by role on several channels, with acknowledgement and escalation.
03

Agree an out-of-band channel

Decide how staff talk if email is down or untrusted.12
04

Put suppliers on your incident lists

Pathology, imaging, EPR and estates, with joint exercises.2,8
05

Practise downtime, including blood

Paper requests, phoned results, O type use and back-entry.3,7
06

Link pressure and incident triggers

Tie OPEL levels and heat or cold alerts to your severity ladder.10,24
07

Debrief and report on time

Hot debrief within 48 hours, structured within 28 days.16

Glossary

Terms used in this report

EPRR
Emergency preparedness, resilience and response: the NHS England framework for incidents and emergencies.
Business continuity incident
Disruption to normal service below acceptable levels that needs special arrangements.
Critical incident
Localised loss of ability to deliver critical services, or risk of harm to patients and staff.
Major incident
An event with serious consequences needing special arrangements by one or more responder agencies.
Core Standards
The minimum EPRR requirements NHS-funded organisations assess themselves against each year.
OPEL
Operational pressures escalation levels, from 1 to 4.
Pathology
Laboratory testing of blood, tissue and other samples.
O negative
The universal donor blood type, used when a patient's blood cannot be matched.
Ransomware
Malicious software that locks or steals data to extort payment.
NIS Regulations
The Network and Information Systems Regulations 2018, which set security and reporting duties for essential services.
DSPT
The Data Security and Protection Toolkit, used to report incidents in health and care.
ICB
Integrated care board, the NHS body that plans and coordinates services across a local area.
