Executive summary

A cyber bill that started without AI is ending with it, but most of the detail is still to come.

The Cyber Security and Resilience (Network and Information Systems) Bill was introduced on 12 November 2025 to update the UK's 2018 NIS Regulations.1,2 It passed its Lords Third Reading on 2 November 2026 and has gone back to the Commons to consider Lords amendments.3 No date for Royal Assent has been set.

The headline changes for most businesses are about scope and speed. Medium and large managed service providers (MSPs) and data centres above set thresholds come into the regime, regulators can designate critical suppliers, and significant incidents must be notified within 24 hours with a fuller report within 72 hours.4

AI arrived late. On 12 October 2026 the government said it was considering how the regime could be strengthened against AI cyber and loss of control risks.5 On 26 October peers agreed a government amendment that lets future regulations give the Secretary of State inspection and information powers where a compromise may be caused by loss of oversight or control of an AI agent.6,7

Much is still proposed rather than settled. The AI powers only bite once regulations are consulted on and approved.8 Peers also inserted, against the government, a recurring review of the regime that must consider frontier AI and the powers of the AI Security Institute. The Commons may yet change or remove it.6

The bottom line
Do not wait for Royal Assent. If you run essential services, provide managed IT or host infrastructure, or depend on someone who does, the regime will reach you. Prepare now for 24 hour reporting, supplier evidence and logs that show what your AI agents did.

Seven things the evidence shows

01
The bill is close to the finish line, but not over it.
The Lords completed all stages on 2 November 2026 and returned the bill to the Commons with amendments, including two the government opposed.3,6
02
Scope widens to the supply chain.
Medium and large MSPs come into scope under the Information Commission, data centres under Ofcom, and regulators can designate critical suppliers.4
03
Reporting gets faster and wider.
Initial notice within 24 hours and a full report within 72 hours, with the NCSC informed at the same time, and affected customers told and kept updated.4
04
AI agent powers are enabling, not yet operative.
The new clause on loss of control of an AI agent lets regulations confer inspection, search and information powers. The detail, including what counts as an AI agent, comes later.7,8

Peers rejected direct duties on AI developers.
A cross-party amendment requiring frontier AI developers to report incidents in 24 hours was defeated by 165 votes to 95.6
06
A recurring scope review was forced on the government.
Amendment 163, agreed by 202 to 155, requires a review within 12 months of passing and further reviews at intervals of not more than 12 months.6,7
07
Penalties now track turnover.
Serious breaches can attract up to £17 million or 4% of worldwide turnover, whichever is higher.4

The bill's journey

A 2018 law meets a supply chain problem

Most of the duties that matter start with secondary legislation, not with Royal Assent.

The NIS Regulations 2018 were the UK's only cross-sector cyber law. They covered operators of essential services in energy, transport, health, drinking water and digital infrastructure, plus some digital services such as cloud computing.4 The government's own case for reform is that the threat moved faster than the rules.

Attackers increasingly go through suppliers. The government cites the June 2024 attack on Synnovis, a pathology supplier to the NHS, which disrupted more than 11,000 appointments and operations, with estimated losses of £32.7 million.4 It also cites a May 2024 incident involving an MSP that exposed payroll data on around 270,000 armed forces personnel.4

The bill answers with three pillars: wider scope, more effective regulators and new powers for the government to act.4 The scope pillar is the one most businesses will feel. MSPs, data centres, large load controllers and designated critical suppliers all come in.

It is also a framework bill. Most measures, including data centre duties, MSP duties, incident reporting and critical suppliers, start only when secondary legislation is made after Royal Assent.4 That is why parts of the regime remain unclear even at this late stage.

That design shaped the AI debate. Peers argued throughout that a bill with cyber security in its title was silent on AI.6 The government's answer was to add a power that future regulations can use, rather than detailed rules in the bill itself.

Regulators stay sector based. Twelve competent authorities oversee the regime, with the Information Commission for MSPs and digital services and Ofcom for data centres.4 Expect differences in emphasis between sectors.

The AI amendments

Two weeks from 'considering' to a new clause

The new clause is a power to make rules about AI agents. The rules themselves are still to be written.

On 12 October 2026 the government told Parliament that, given how fast AI capabilities are advancing, it was considering how the regime could be strengthened so that AI cyber and loss of control risks are better understood. It named near miss reporting and learning from AI incidents as examples.5

The accompanying announcement described loss of control as autonomous AI systems evading human oversight.9 Three days later, The Register reported officials telling MPs they were still looking at whether the bill was the right vehicle.10

By Report stage on 26 October the government had tabled Amendment 70. It lets regulations confer functions on the Secretary of State where a compromise is or may be attributable to loss of oversight or control of an AI agent.6 Those functions can include appointing inspectors, entering and searching premises, seizing documents and requiring information, including information held outside the UK.7

The minister set out three further measures for secondary legislation: agent risk requirements, preserving information about agent malfunctions, and telling affected organisations such as suppliers.6 The 3 November factsheet lists a fourth: post-incident review powers for ministers.8

Peers wanted more. A cross-party amendment would have required frontier AI developers to report unauthorised access to UK essential services within 24 hours. The government argued the bill places reporting duties on regulated entities, not developers, and peers rejected it by 165 to 95.6

Proposals for a statutory audit role for the AI Security Institute were not pressed to a vote. The government argued such a role would undermine its voluntary access to frontier models.6

What it means

The regime will reach you through your contracts

If you buy managed IT, you are part of this regime whether or not your name is on a register.

Most UK organisations will never register with a regulator under this bill. But if you buy managed IT, host in a data centre or supply an essential service, the bill will reach you through those relationships. MSPs will have to report significant incidents and tell affected customers.4

That cuts both ways. Customers gain a right to know when their provider's incident has likely affected them, and to be kept updated while it continues.4,6 Providers will expect customers to have a contact, a process and a decision maker ready to receive that notice.

For MSPs the change is fundamental. Firms that have operated without sector regulation will register with the Information Commission, meet security requirements and face turnover based penalties.4 Expect them to tighten contracts, access and logging, and to ask more of customers.

For AI the signal is clear even though the rules are not. The government expects regulated firms to show they can monitor agents, keep records of their actions and explain what went wrong.8 Any agent with access to production systems is now a governance question, not only an innovation one.

Boards should note the direction of travel. The minister proposed mandatory requirements on governance, including board level accountability and named, accredited individuals with senior responsibility.6 The voluntary Cyber Governance Code of Practice already sets expectations for directors.13

Timing remains uncertain. The Commons must still consider the Lords amendments, and most duties need secondary legislation and consultation first.3,4 That leaves a preparation window, but not a long one.

Common mistakes

Four things organisations get wrong

Patterns we expect as the regime beds in.

Assuming they are out of scope

Scope follows the supply chain. Critical supplier designation can catch small firms, and contracts will carry duties further.

Treating 24 hours as plenty

The clock starts at awareness. Without a named decision maker and a template, a day passes quickly.

Running agents without a record

Proposed rules point to logs of agent actions and control attempts. Many pilots keep neither.

Leaving MSP contracts unchanged

Notification, evidence, access and AI use terms need updating before duties commence, not after an incident.
The organisations that cope best will treat the bill as a supply chain project, not a compliance form.

Recommendations

Six steps to take now

Ordered by how quickly they reduce risk.
01

Map your position

Decide if you are an essential service, a digital or managed service, a likely critical supplier or a customer.
02

Rehearse the 24 hour notice

Run a tabletop exercise that ends with a draft notice inside 24 hours and a fuller account at 72.
03

Inventory your AI agents

List every agent with system access, what it can do, who owns it and how it is stopped.
04

Turn on the right logs

Keep records of agent actions, permissions and overrides long enough to investigate.
05

Reopen supplier contracts

Agree notification, evidence and AI use terms with MSPs and hosting providers.
06

Brief the board

Name an accountable executive, set a reporting rhythm and track the consultations.

Glossary

Terms used in this report

NIS Regulations 2018
The UK's existing cross-sector cyber law for essential and some digital services.
Operator of essential services
An organisation providing a service such as energy, water or health that the regime regulates.
Relevant managed service provider
A medium or large firm providing ongoing IT management to customers with access to their systems.
Critical supplier
A supplier a regulator designates because its disruption could cause significant harm.
Competent authority
The sector regulator for NIS, such as Ofcom or the Information Commission.
Secondary legislation
Regulations made under powers in an Act, often after consultation.
AI agent
An AI system that can take actions, not just answer. The bill leaves the legal definition to regulations.
Loss of oversight or control
When operators cannot adequately monitor or control an agent. Also defined later by regulations.
Near miss
An event that could have caused an incident but did not. Reporting will be voluntary.
AISI
The AI Security Institute, the government body that tests advanced AI models.
Ping-pong
The exchange of amendments between the Commons and Lords before a bill can become law.
NIS2
The EU's updated network and information security directive, Directive (EU) 2022/2555.
