Executive summary

The money is real. The returns, for most, are not yet.

Gartner now expects worldwide AI spending to reach about $2.67 trillion in 2026, up 49.5% on 2025, and has raised its forecast twice this year.1,2 Over the same period, the most widely cited surveys show little change in the share of organisations reporting a financial return. McKinsey's 2026 State of AI found 37% of respondents attributing any EBIT impact to AI, essentially unchanged on a year earlier, and only about 6% attributing 5% or more.3,4

The independent evidence is cooler still. A study of nearly 9,000 executives in seven countries, including the UK, found AI had raised annual labour productivity growth by about 0.2% over the past three years, with executives expecting 0.6% a year over the next three.5 That is a real effect, but far smaller than the investment implies.

Agents raise the stakes. Vendor-sponsored surveys this year report that large shares of firms have had to reverse an agent's action or met failures they could not trace.6 These numbers come from companies that sell agent or security tooling, so treat them as signals, not measurements. The signal is consistent all the same.

The organisations seeing value share habits: they redesign workflows rather than bolt AI on, measure impact against a baseline, put a senior owner on the hook and run agents inside a governed control layer.3,7

The bottom line
The 2026 reckoning is not a verdict that AI does not work. It is a verdict on how most organisations buy and deploy it. Fund fewer, measured use cases, put controls around agents before they act, and stop what does not pay.

The gap

Where the money is going, and why returns lag

Most of 2026's AI money is being spent on capacity. Value has to be built inside your own workflows.

The scale of 2026 AI spending is hard to overstate. Gartner's September forecast of $2.67 trillion includes about $1.48 trillion for infrastructure, so more than half the total is data centres, servers and chips rather than software that changes how a business runs.2 Gartner's own analyst called the build-out the largest infrastructure project humanity has ever undertaken.

That matters for how to read the gap. Much of the money is a bet by technology providers on future demand. Enterprise budgets are smaller but rising: KPMG's quarterly pulse put average planned AI investment at $210 million over the next 12 months among the large organisations it surveys, up from $186 million in the first quarter.7

The returns side moves more slowly. McKinsey's 2026 survey of 1,719 respondents found adoption almost universal, with nearly nine in ten using AI in at least one function, yet the share reporting any EBIT impact stayed at 37%.4 Gartner said in January that better predictability of ROI must come before AI can truly scale.15

Costs are also creeping in from an unexpected direction. One in five McKinsey respondents said operating costs, including token charges, already constrain their use of AI.4 Usage-based pricing means a successful pilot can become an expensive production service.

Investors are now asking the same question as boards. In late October Alphabet reported negative free cash flow of $9.07 billion for the quarter and its shares fell about 13%, their worst day on record.18 Several causes were cited, so this is not a simple verdict on AI. But it shows that the market is pricing return on AI capital, not only ambition.

For an enterprise buyer, the lesson is simple. The supply side will keep spending. Your job is to make sure your own share of that spending buys something you can measure.

The evidence

Real gains, small totals, weak measurement

Individual productivity is not the same as financial return. Work has to change for one to become the other.

The most careful evidence of 2026 points in one direction: AI is producing real but modest gains so far. The NBER study, which surveyed nearly 9,000 executives across the US, UK, Germany and four other countries, found AI raised annual labour productivity growth by about 0.2% over three years, with essentially no net effect on employment.5 Executives expect 0.6% a year over the next three.

A separate NBER paper based on about 750 executives was more positive, finding productivity gains that vary by sector and are strongest in high-skill services and finance. It also found executives perceive larger gains than they can yet measure, which the authors link to a lag in revenue realisation.21

Task-level studies are more striking. Stanford's 2026 AI Index reports large gains in some settings, such as clinicians reporting far less time on notes, while noting agents still lag on multi-step planning and financial analysis.22

The gap between task and firm is the heart of the problem. McKinsey found 80% of respondents say AI improved their own productivity, against 37% reporting any EBIT impact.3 Time saved by an individual becomes money only if the work is redesigned, so the hour is either removed from cost or reinvested in something customers pay for.

The limits of the evidence deserve equal attention. Almost every figure here is self-reported. Surveys by consultancies and vendors draw on clients and prospects, define success differently and rarely track the same firms over time. Few organisations have baselines, so their own claims are hard to test.

The weakest evidence tends to be the loudest. The widely shared 2025 finding that 95% of organisations saw no return came from a small, preliminary sample.13 Treat any single headline figure, positive or negative, with the same scepticism.

Agents

When software acts on its own judgement

Rollbacks are not the failure. Being unable to see, stop or undo an agent is.

Agents moved from slides to production in 2026. McKinsey found 40% of organisations with over $1 billion in revenue now scale agents in at least one function, up from 27% a year earlier; among smaller firms the share stayed at 22%.23

The reliability evidence comes mostly from vendors, and it should be read that way. Kore.ai, which sells an agent platform, commissioned a May 2026 survey of over 400 US IT leaders: 79% had to reverse an action taken by an agent, 70% had met a failure their teams could not trace and 42% reported lost revenue from an agent failure.6

Sinch, a communications platform vendor, surveyed more than 2,500 AI decision makers and found 74% had rolled back or shut down a live customer communications agent.17 Its most interesting result was that the rollback rate was higher, 81%, among organisations with fully mature guardrails.24 One reading is that good controls make problems visible and stoppable, which is the point.

Salt Security, an API security vendor, found 49.8% of more than 380 respondents had confirmed or suspected unexpected or unauthorised agent activity. Only 17.5% could automatically stop a misbehaving agent in production.10

None of these is a neutral measurement. Samples are self-selected, definitions vary and each sponsor sells a remedy. But they agree with public incidents, such as the 2025 case in which a coding agent deleted a live database during a code freeze.12

The economic consequence is simple. An agent that is right most of the time but cannot be traced, paused or undone carries hidden costs: review effort, rework, customer remediation and regulatory exposure. Those costs belong in the business case from day one.

How agent failures spread

One action, many systems

A single wrong step can travel across systems. These are the points to catch it.
01
Permissions wider than the task
Agents inherit the access of their account. Scope each to the minimum it needs.
02
Chained actions with no checkpoint
In Kore.ai's survey, 40% said one agent failure cascaded across systems.6 Add approval points.
03
Activity no one can reconstruct
Only 12.5% in Salt Security's survey could consistently trace an agent's full path.10 Log every tool call.
04
No fast way to stop or undo
Agents need a kill switch, reversible actions and a tested restore route.

Who is seeing value

What the 6% do differently

The organisations reporting real returns are not using different models. They run AI differently.

Redesign the work

Nearly three quarters of McKinsey's high performers redesigned workflows around AI, against about a quarter of others.3

Measure against cost

Among organisations reporting returns, 48% consistently assess AI value against cost, against 12% overall.7

Put a senior name on it

UK organisations

What it means for UK organisations

UK firms have adopted AI widely. The next step is using it deeply, and measuring what it changes.

UK adoption has grown fast. The ONS estimates about 35% of businesses with 10 or more employees use at least one AI technology, up from about 12% in late 2023, rising to 49% among firms with 250 or more staff.11

Use is shallow. Only 10% of adopting businesses describe their use as extensive, and the average adopter uses about 1.6 AI technologies.11 Most UK firms have bought a tool; far fewer have changed how they work, which is exactly where the return is made.

The ONS also finds around half of businesses report no headcount effect from AI, and has not yet published firm-level productivity estimates.11 UK executives are part of the NBER sample, which found modest productivity effects to date.5

For mid-sized UK organisations, the reckoning is an advantage. You can avoid the pilot sprawl and runaway usage bills larger firms are now unwinding, and buy on evidence rather than fear of missing out.

Three UK-specific points apply. First, UK GDPR and the ICO's expectations apply to any agent that touches personal data, so audit trails and the ability to correct or reverse actions are compliance requirements, not extras. Second, sector regulators expect accountable humans for consequential decisions. Third, exchange rates and usage pricing mean AI costs quoted in dollars can move without warning; budgets need headroom and caps.

The practical stance is to treat AI like any other capital decision: a baseline, a target, a named owner, a cost envelope and a date to decide whether to scale or stop.

Recommendations

Seven moves to make AI pay

Drawn from the habits of organisations reporting returns, and from the 2026 failures.
01
Fund fewer use cases, with targets
Pick a small number where value is measurable in money, time or risk, and set the target before you start.
02
Baseline before you build
Measure cycle time, cost per case, error rate or revenue now, so the pilot can be judged.
03
Redesign the workflow, not just the task
Decide where saved time goes: removed cost, more volume or better service.
04
Put agents in a control layer
Least privilege, approval points for high-impact actions, full logging, a kill switch and tested rollback.
05
Cap and track running costs
Budget per use case, alerts on usage and a monthly cost per outcome.
06
Report value like any investment
A one-page quarterly view for the board: spend, outcomes, incidents and decisions.
07
Set stop rules in advance
If a use case misses its target by an agreed date, stop it and redeploy the budget.

Glossary

Terms used in this report

AI agent
An AI system that takes actions, such as calling tools or changing records, towards a goal.
EBIT
Earnings before interest and tax; a common measure of operating profit.
Baseline
A measure of performance before a change, used to judge its effect.
Capex
Capital expenditure; here, spending on data centres, servers and chips.
Control layer
The policies and tooling between AI models and business systems: access, approvals, logging and monitoring. KPMG calls this a harness layer.
Rollback
Reversing an agent's action or withdrawing an agent from live service.
Observability
The ability to see what a system did and why, from logs and traces.
Token
The unit by which most AI model usage is metered and billed.
Trough of disillusionment
Gartner's term for the phase after peak hype when results fall short of expectations.
Workflow redesign
Changing how work is done end to end, not just adding a tool to an existing step.
