Foreword

The companies that check who you are can lose the proof.

In late October 2026 DigiCert, one of the best known names in website security, disclosed that a network migration in February 2025 had left an internal archive of customer records reachable from the internet. Most records held business contact details, but a small minority held passports, driving licences or utility bills that customers had uploaded to prove who they were.1 The data was read in mid 2026 and the access was confirmed in October.1,2

In the same fortnight Chainalysis, whose tools many compliance teams use to screen crypto transactions, said an intrusion had exposed names, usernames, password hashes and live tokens for all its users. It took its main services offline while it rebuilt access.3

Neither firm's core service appears to have been subverted. But both cases make the same point: the suppliers you trust to verify identity, or to hold your credentials, are themselves a place your data can leak from. Most organisations cannot say which suppliers hold copies of their staff's passports, or which hold tokens that act on their behalf. This playbook helps you find out, fix the contracts and be ready for the call.

Introduction

Three habits make a supplier breach survivable.

You cannot stop a supplier making a mistake. You can decide what they hold, what they must tell you, and how fast you can respond.

Know who holds what

Keep one list of suppliers that hold identity documents, credentials, tokens or bulk personal data, with an owner for each.

Give them less, and bind them

Send only what is needed, have it deleted when done, and put notification, security and exit terms in writing.

Be ready for the call

Have a tested plan to rotate credentials, assess the risk and decide on reporting within 72 hours.

What happened

Two breaches, one lesson

Suppliers in security and compliance are rarely questioned as hard as they should be.

DigiCert's incident report is unusually candid. A migration moved an internal business system out from behind a VPN. It was treated as infrastructure work rather than a security change, so nobody reviewed what became reachable. A self-registration feature without multi-factor authentication was exposed, and a permissions gap let those accounts read archived records.1

Unknown accounts were spotted in June 2026 and treated as a sign-up problem. Only in October did DigiCert establish that records had been read between 27 June and 2 July. It says no certificates were affected, and it is still working out which individuals' documents were involved.1,2

Chainalysis's case moved faster but grew in the telling. Its first notice on 30 October described non-sensitive identity data. By 4 November it said all users' password hashes and live tokens had been exposed, and customers had to allowlist IP addresses, reset passwords and rotate API keys before services returned.3

The two firms hold different things for different reasons. One keeps evidence of identity to validate customers. The other holds credentials that let customers use its platform. Both were trusted precisely because their job is security and compliance.

That trust is the problem. Suppliers in these categories are rarely questioned as hard as a new payroll or CRM provider, yet what they hold is often more dangerous to lose. Documents that prove identity can be reused for fraud for years, and tokens can act as you.

So start with a map. You do not need a perfect inventory of every supplier. You need to know, by the end of this month, which ones hold identity documents, credentials or bulk personal data, and who in your organisation owns each relationship.

Contract terms

Six clauses to check in every tier 1 contract

UK GDPR Article 28 sets the minimum for processors.4 These go further where it counts.
01

Breach notice in hours, not "without undue delay"

The law requires a processor to tell you without undue delay; you have 72 hours to report.5 Ask for 24 hours, with named contacts.
02

What they will tell you

Data types, people affected, time window, cause, and updates as scope changes.
03

Retention and deletion

Identity documents deleted once checks are complete, with written confirmation.
04

Sub-processors

A current list, notice of changes and the same terms passed down.
05

Security change control

Network and platform migrations reviewed as security changes, with re-checked monitoring.
06

Audit and exit

Rights to evidence and audit, plus return or deletion of all data at the end.

Minimisation

Five rules for sending less

UK GDPR expects you to keep personal data no longer than needed.6 Apply the same test to what suppliers keep for you.
01
Verify, then delete
Ask for confirmation of a check, not a stored copy of the document.
02
Send the least that works
A redacted document or a reference number often does the job.
03
Set a clock on every copy
Agree a retention period for each data type, and check it is applied.
04
Scope every token
Grant integrations the narrowest permissions and shortest lifetimes they will accept.
05
Clean up at exit
Ending a contract is not the end of the data. Get deletion in writing.

The first 72 hours

From the supplier's call to a decision

Your 72-hour reporting clock may start when you become aware of a breach of your data.5

Log and assign

Record the time you were told, name an incident lead and open a single channel with the supplier.

Establish what they held

Use your supplier map: which data types, which people, which credentials.

Cut and rotate

Reset passwords, rotate API keys and revoke tokens. Add IP allowlisting if offered.

Assess and decide

Judge risk to people and decide, with your DPO or adviser, whether to report to the ICO and tell individuals.

Track the scope

Expect the picture to change. Diarise follow-ups and revisit decisions with each update.
First statements often understate scope. Plan for the second and third update, not just the first.

Three scenarios

What a supplier breach looks like in practice

HR and security

Your ID checker leaks passports

The trigger
A verification supplier says some uploaded ID documents were exposed.
Do first
Ask exactly whose documents were held, and for what period.
Tell people
Warn affected staff about identity fraud and phishing; offer practical support.
Fix for next time
Contract for deletion after checks, and stop sending full scans.
IT

A SaaS tool's tokens are exposed

The trigger
A platform says users' password hashes and live tokens were exposed.
Do first
Rotate keys, revoke tokens and reset passwords, including anywhere reused.
Check
Logs for unusual use of the integration during the exposure window.
Fix for next time
Shorter token lifetimes, IP allowlisting and SSO with MFA.
Finance and HR

Your payroll provider is breached

The trigger
A processor reports theft of staff records held for you.
Do first
Confirm data types and numbers; start your 72-hour assessment.
Tell people
Clear, early message to staff with what to watch for.
Fix for next time
Test the exit plan and check sub-processors.

Glossary

Words worth knowing

Controller
Under UK GDPR, the organisation that decides why and how personal data is used.
Processor
An organisation that handles personal data on a controller's behalf, under contract.
Article 28 contract
The written terms UK GDPR requires between a controller and a processor.
Certificate authority
A trusted organisation that issues digital certificates after checking who is asking.
API key
A secret that lets one system use another's services. Treat it like a password.
Live token
A credential that keeps a session or integration logged in, often bypassing MFA.
IP allowlisting
Only accepting connections from approved network addresses.
Data minimisation
Collecting and keeping only the personal data you actually need.
Sub-processor
A supplier your supplier uses to handle your data.
Tabletop exercise
A structured walk-through of an incident to test plans and roles.
