Executive summary

Protecting crowded places is mostly about people and procedures, and hospitality is at the heart of it.

United Kingdom: the Terrorism (Protection of Premises) Act 2025, known as Martyn's Law, received Royal Assent on 3 April 2025.1 It will require the people in control of qualifying premises, including bars, pubs, restaurants, cafés, hotels and nightclubs, to take steps to protect the public from terrorism.1,2 Premises where 200 to 799 people can reasonably be expected at the same time fall into the standard tier, and those with 800 or more into the enhanced tier.3 The Security Industry Authority (SIA) will regulate, and expects the Act to come into force in spring 2027; no date has yet been set.4,5

Elsewhere, most protective security advice for crowded places is guidance. In the United States, CISA asks owners and operators to Connect, Plan, Train and Report.6 In the European Union, the Commission's good practices ask operators for a security plan, a trained security lead and a crisis plan to alert, confine or evacuate; binding duties are national.7 In Dubai, regulation sets security conditions for hotels.8

The evidence behind the law points to communication and preparation as much as physical security. The Manchester Arena Inquiry found that a concern about the attacker did not reach anyone who could act on it, and that on the night the emergency plans "were not known by everyone who should have known about them".9,10 At London Bridge and Borough Market, where attackers entered bars and restaurants, all eight people who died did so within 15 minutes of the attack beginning.11 Both are UK cases, and their lessons apply to any venue.

This report sets out what applies where, what the inquiries found, and the practical steps for hospitality leaders in any country.

The bottom line
Do not wait for a law. Count your peak numbers, write the four procedures, name a decision maker on every shift and make sure one alert reaches every member of staff, then rehearse it before your busiest season.

Regional notes

Protecting crowded places: what applies where

Venue security rules differ sharply. Some are law, some are guidance. Check each site against the rules where it operates.
Regional note · United Kingdom
Martyn's Law received Royal Assent on 3 April 2025 but is not yet in force; the SIA expects commencement in spring 2027.1,4 Premises where 200 or more people are expected at once will need evacuation, invacuation, lockdown and communication procedures.3,12
IN PRACTICE The following pages set out the Act. Count the busiest realistic night, staff included.
Regional note · United States
Federal advice for crowded places is guidance, not law. CISA's action guide for mass gatherings asks owners and operators to Connect, Plan, Train and Report, including public address scripts and Run, Hide, Fight training.6 The SAFETY Act offers liability protections, by application, for approved anti-terrorism technologies and services.18
IN PRACTICE Plan with local law enforcement and fire services, and check state and local rules and OSHA's emergency action plan rule.
Regional note · European Union
The Commission's 2017 Action Plan and 2019 good practices are guidance, not law. They ask operators, hospitality included, for vulnerability assessments, a security plan, a trained security lead with a backup, and a crisis plan to alert, confine or evacuate.7
IN PRACTICE Binding duties come from national law and vary by member state. The good practices also call for a spokesperson and key messages agreed in advance.
Regional note · Middle East (UAE, Saudi Arabia)
Dubai: SIRA's Preventive Systems Manual (2025) requires luxury hotels to have a security operations manager or supervisor around the clock and a surveillance room that can reach guards, hotel officials and emergency services.8 Saudi Arabia: a tourism accommodation licence needs a valid Civil Defense permit and connection to the Shomoos platform.19
IN PRACTICE Requirements vary by emirate, star rating and licence. Confirm them with SIRA, the relevant emirate authorities, or the Saudi Ministry of Tourism and Civil Defense.
A summary for orientation, not legal advice. Requirements depend on sector, size, licence and jurisdiction, and change over time. Confirm the current text with your legal and compliance teams.

United Kingdom

United Kingdom: what the Act requires, and of whom

For most hospitality venues, the duty is a set of procedures that every shift can follow.

United Kingdom: the Act's long title describes its purpose: to require persons with control of certain premises or events to take steps to reduce the vulnerability of the premises or event to, and the risk of physical harm to individuals arising from, acts of terrorism, and to confer related functions on the SIA.1

It applies to premises wholly or mainly used for purposes in Schedule 1. Statutory guidance says the food and drink use "includes bars, pubs, restaurants, cafés and food halls", the hotels use includes hostels and holiday parks, and nightclubs sit under entertainment and leisure.2 The Home Office's own examples include a pub with a beer garden and a hotel with grounds used for dining and events.3

Tier depends on numbers. Premises are in the standard tier where 200 or more people, including staff, can reasonably be expected to be present at the same time from time to time, and in the enhanced tier at 800 or more.3 Qualifying events have an 800-person threshold and further conditions.3

Standard tier premises must notify the SIA and have, so far as is reasonably practicable, appropriate public protection procedures: evacuation, invacuation, lockdown and communication.12 The Home Office describes these as simple, low-cost steps.3 There is no statutory training requirement, but staff with responsibilities must know the procedures and their roles.12

Enhanced tier premises must also have public protection measures relating to monitoring, movement, physical safety and security, and security of information; document compliance and give the document to the SIA; and, where the responsible person is an organisation, designate a senior individual.20

The SIA will assess compliance through desk-based assessments and inspections, usually with at least 72 hours' notice.4 Penalties reach £10,000 for standard duty premises and £18m or 5% of worldwide revenue for enhanced duty premises, with daily penalties for continued failure.16

UK case study

Manchester Arena: when the plan and the message do not reach people

The concern was raised in time. It did not reach anyone who could act on it.

On 22 May 2017, 22 people were killed in the attack at the end of a concert at Manchester Arena.4 The public inquiry reported in three volumes. Volume 1, published on 17 June 2021, found that the security arrangements should have prevented or minimised the devastating impact of the attack, and failed to do so.9

One finding is directly relevant to every venue. A member of the public raised a concern about the attacker with a steward around 15 minutes before the concert ended. The steward had no radio. A colleague with a radio tried to report it, but someone else was talking on the channel, and the concern went no further. The inquiry found the efforts made were inadequate, and that there had been enough time to investigate and act.9

Volume 2, published on 3 November 2022, examined the emergency response and made 149 recommendations.10 It found that significant aspects of the response went wrong. The police declaration of Operation Plato, the response to a suspected marauding terrorist attack, was not communicated to the other emergency services as the police plan required, and the fire service did not arrive until over two hours after the attack.10

Its conclusion on planning applies far beyond the emergency services: plans "were not known by everyone who should have known about them", were not always clear, not always understood and not always put into practice.10 It also found single points of failure, and called for checks to make sure that what needed to be done had been done.10

The inquiry recorded that venue staff acted quickly: the head of security instructed staff to divert people away from the scene, and a radio message asked staff with first aid skills to go there.10 It also called for more members of the public to have first responder skills.10

Volume 1 explicitly anticipated a Protect Duty, an obligation for those responsible for publicly accessible locations to consider and implement security measures.9 Martyn's Law is that duty.

UK case study

London Bridge: bars, restaurants and the first 15 minutes

Before police arrive, what happens inside depends on the staff on shift.

On the evening of 3 June 2017 three attackers drove a van into pedestrians on London Bridge, crashed outside a pub and then attacked people at street level, in a restaurant courtyard and in bars and a restaurant on Stoney Street. Armed officers stopped them about ten minutes after the attack began.11

The Chief Coroner found that the emergency operation was generally well managed, and that all eight people who died had suffered injuries that could not have been treated at the scene and died within 15 minutes of the attack beginning.11 He also recorded police evidence that a clear legal duty on private site owners would help, contrasting the extensive health and safety duties to visitors with the lack of any comparable duty to protect from terrorism.11

For hospitality, the lesson is about time. Before police arrive, what happens inside a bar or restaurant depends on staff: whether they recognise what is happening, lock the doors, move guests away from the entrance and tell everyone on site.

Terrorism is, thankfully, rare. Other emergencies are not. In the UK, the Home Office reported 6,732 police reports of spiking between May 2022 and April 2023, 80% of them in night-time economy venues and almost half in bars.13 The UK's Crime and Policing Act 2026 includes a new offence of administering a harmful substance, including by spiking; it is not yet in force.22,23

Technology failures reach the front desk too. In September 2022 IHG told the stock exchange that unauthorised activity had significantly disrupted its booking channels and apps.14 A booking or payment outage on the busiest Saturday of the year is a crisis of a different kind, but it needs the same things: one decision maker, one instruction to every site and confirmation that it has been read.

What it means

What it means for organisations in Hospitality

A plan that works on a quiet weekday may not work on the busiest night of the year.

Wherever a venue operates, the hard part is the same: a varied, often temporary workforce that must know what to do on a noisy, crowded night. The rules around it differ. In the UK, Martyn's Law will make procedures a legal duty for venues of 200 or more.3,12 In the US and the EU, the core advice is guidance; in Dubai and Saudi Arabia, licensing and security conditions apply.6,7,8,19

The guidance points the same way. CISA asks operators to plan, train staff and prepare public address scripts.6 The EU good practices ask for a trained security lead with a backup and a crisis plan to alert, confine or evacuate.7 The UK Home Office's restaurant example relies on a shift manager who enacts the procedures and an induction briefing for every new starter.12

The UK inquiries show where it breaks. Concerns that do not reach a decision maker. Channels that are busy at the moment they are needed. Plans that exist but are not known.9,10 Each of these has a practical fix: a named decision maker on every shift, more than one way to raise and send an alert, and a way to confirm who has received it.

Peak periods are the sharpest test. Venues are fuller, shifts are longer, many staff are new or temporary, and guests are spread across function rooms, terraces and bedrooms. A plan that works on a quiet weekday may not work on the busiest night of the year.

Groups with venues in several countries face a second task: a single way of working that meets each country's rules. One severity scale, one set of roles and one way to alert every member of staff can sit on top of local duties, with a named owner for each country.

The same capability serves every emergency. A venue that can lock down and tell every member of staff within a minute can also run a fire evacuation, respond to a spiking report or move to a paper booking list when systems fail. Building it is good practice whatever the law says.

Recommendations

Seven steps for every venue

In priority order. Most cost time, not money.
01

Know the rules for every site

Security, fire, licensing and workplace duties, by country, each with a named owner.3,7,8
02

Count peak numbers for every site

Include staff and outside areas, and record the method. In the UK it sets your tier.3,17
03

Write the four procedures, one page per site

Evacuation, invacuation, lockdown and communication, with routes, safe areas and named roles.12
04

Give every shift a decision maker

A duty manager who can declare a lockdown at once, known to all staff.
05

Make one alert reach everyone, and confirm it

More than one channel, with replies tracked and non-responders chased.9
06

Brief every starter, including seasonal staff

A short induction on the procedures and their own role.7,12
07

Rehearse before your busiest season

A lockdown walk-through on a late shift, and a head office exercise across venues.
