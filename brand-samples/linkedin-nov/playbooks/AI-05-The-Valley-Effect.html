Foreword

The AI you use is shaped by decisions made a long way from your office.

A small number of companies, most of them in the United States, build the models behind the assistants your people use every day. Their choices about pricing, features, data handling and safety arrive in your business through software updates you did not ask for and contracts you rarely read.

That is not a reason for alarm. These companies have built remarkable tools, and competition between them has made those tools better and more widely available very quickly. But it is a reason for leaders to understand who the players are, how their incentives work and where the points of leverage sit.

This playbook gives you that map. It explains who is shaping AI, how it reaches you through the platforms you already pay for, how governments are responding, and what a sensible UK business can do to stay flexible, keep control of its data and avoid being locked into a single supplier.

Introduction

Know the map before you pick the route.

AI arrives through a handful of channels: the productivity suite you already own, the cloud platform you build on, and the assistants your people choose for themselves. Understanding who sits behind each channel helps you make choices that still look sensible in three years.

Know who is behind your tools

Most AI features are built on a small number of underlying model families. Knowing which ones you rely on is the first step to managing the risk.

Treat models as replaceable parts

The leading models leapfrog each other regularly. Design your use of AI so that swapping one model for another is a decision, not a project.

Keep your data on your terms

Where data is processed, how it is retained and whether it is used for training are contract questions. Ask them before you sign, not after.

Who is shaping AI

Why so few firms sit at the frontier

The frontier changes hands often. Build so that you can change with it.

Building a leading AI model takes three things in enormous quantity: computing power, capital and specialist talent. Training the largest models requires vast clusters of specialised chips running for long periods, in data centres that draw as much power as a small town. Very few organisations can marshal those resources, which is why the frontier is dominated by a short list of names.

Compute is the tightest bottleneck. Nvidia's graphics processors and software have become the default foundation for training and running large models, and demand for them has shaped the whole industry. The large cloud providers, Microsoft, Amazon and Google, are building their own chips and data centres at huge scale, partly to reduce that reliance and partly to sell capacity to everyone else.

Capital follows compute. The leading labs have formed deep partnerships with the cloud giants: OpenAI with Microsoft, Anthropic with Amazon and Google, while Google DeepMind and Meta draw on the resources of their parent companies. These partnerships matter to you because they influence which models appear first, and most smoothly, on which platforms.

Talent is the third constraint. The researchers and engineers who can build and train frontier systems are a small community, concentrated in a few cities, and they move between labs often. Ideas spread quickly as a result, which is one reason no lab stays ahead for long.

The outcome is a frontier race in which capabilities improve quickly and leadership changes hands regularly. For a business buyer, that is broadly good news: competition keeps quality rising and gives you alternatives. The risk is not that the technology stalls. It is that you build your processes so tightly around one supplier that you cannot take advantage when another moves ahead.

Beyond the Valley

The challengers outside Silicon Valley

The frontier is not only American. Strong alternatives come from Europe, China and the open-weight community.

Mistral, France

A European lab offering both open-weight and commercial models, plus its own assistant. Often considered by firms who want a European supplier.

DeepSeek, China

Released capable open-weight models that drew wide attention for their efficiency, sharpening the debate about how much compute the frontier really needs.

Qwen, China

Alibaba's model family, with many versions released as open weights and widely used by developers around the world.

The open-weight ecosystem

Thousands of adapted models built on releases from Meta, Mistral, Chinese labs and others, which firms can run on their own infrastructure.
Open-weight models from any country can be run in your own environment, but check licence terms and data handling with care before using them in the business.

Distribution

The assistant arrives with the software you already own

The company that owns the menu decides which AI appears in it.

For most businesses, the first serious AI tool was not chosen. It appeared in a menu. Microsoft has built Copilot into Microsoft 365 and Windows, Google has built Gemini into Workspace and Android, and Apple and Samsung are building assistants into phones. The model behind the button is often invisible to the user.

This is the most powerful force in the market. A lab with an excellent model still needs distribution, and the companies that own the operating systems, office suites and cloud platforms control the most valuable shelf space. That is why partnerships between labs and platforms matter so much, and why the same model can feel quite different depending on where you meet it.

Built-in assistants have real advantages. They sit inside your existing security boundary, they can see the documents and emails your people already have access to, and they are managed through admin tools your IT team knows. For many everyday tasks, that convenience wins.

The trade-off is choice. When AI comes bundled with a suite, the suite owner decides which model you use, how it is priced and when features change. Specialist assistants such as Claude or ChatGPT, used directly, may be stronger for particular tasks such as long-document analysis, writing or coding, and give you a second option if the bundled tool falls short.

Cloud platforms add a third route. Amazon Bedrock, Google Cloud Vertex AI and Microsoft's Azure AI services each offer models from several labs through one account. For businesses building their own AI features, that makes switching between models much easier than it would otherwise be.

The practical lesson is to know which route each of your AI tools arrived by, and who controls it. That single inventory is the basis of every sensible decision in the rest of this playbook.

Rules and safety

How governments are responding

Approaches differ sharply by region and are changing fast. Treat this as orientation, not legal advice, and check the current position.
Region
Broad approach
What it means for you
European Union
The AI Act: a risk-based law with obligations phased in over several years, including rules for general-purpose models.
Relevant if you sell into or operate in the EU, or use AI in high-risk areas.
United Kingdom
A principles-based approach led by existing regulators, with UK GDPR and ICO guidance applying to personal data.
Your sector regulator and data protection duties are the main guide.
United States
No single federal AI law. Policy has shifted between administrations, and several states have passed their own rules.
Supplier terms and features may change as US policy moves.
China
Specific rules for generative AI and algorithms, with content and registration requirements.
Relevant when using Chinese models or services. Check data flows.
Frontier safety
Labs publish their own safety policies, and government AI security institutes test leading models.
Ask suppliers how they test and govern their models.

Open weights, explained

Some models are released so anyone can download and run them. That brings real flexibility, and some responsibilities that hosted services handle for you.
What it means
The trained model is published, so you can run it on your own servers or a cloud provider of your choice.
Why it helps
Data can stay in an environment you control, costs are more predictable, and no single supplier can switch it off.
The trade-offs
You take on hosting, security, updates and safety testing. Licences vary and some carry restrictions.
When to consider it
Sensitive data, high volumes of routine tasks, or a need to fine-tune a model on your own material.
Owner
Your IT lead or managed service partner

Your exposures

Four ways the Valley reaches your balance sheet

Every business using AI carries some version of these. The aim is to see them clearly, not to avoid AI.

Dependency

If one supplier's assistant runs a core process, an outage, policy change or price rise lands directly on your operations.

Pricing power

AI is often sold in bundles and tiers. Once workflows depend on it, your negotiating position weakens.

Choice

Bundled tools can crowd out better options for specific jobs. Keep the freedom to use the right tool for each task.

Sovereignty

Where data is processed and stored, and under which legal regime, matters to clients, regulators and insurers.
None of these is a reason to wait. They are reasons to buy with your eyes open and to keep an exit route.

Regulated sector

A firm with strict data residency needs

The situation
Client contracts require UK or European data processing.
The exposure
Some AI features process data in other regions by default.
The flexible choice
Use enterprise plans with regional processing, or open-weight models in your own cloud.

From exposure to sensible response

Practical steps that cost little now and save a great deal later.
Concern

Processes, prompts and data built around one tool.
Keep prompts, templates and data in formats you own and can move.
Price rises
Per-seat AI add-ons creeping across the whole organisation.
License by role and need, and review usage before renewal.

A model you rely on is updated or retired.
Keep a short set of test tasks and rerun them when models change.

Processing in regions your clients did not agree to.

An outage or policy change stops a critical process.

Staff using personal accounts because approved tools fall short.
Offer good approved options and make them easy to reach.

Principles

Five rules for staying in control

The businesses that do well will use the best of the Valley without being owned by it.
01
Know your supply chain
List every AI tool in use, the model behind it, and the company that controls it.
02
Own your assets
Your prompts, templates, evaluation tests and data are yours. Store them where you can take them elsewhere.
03
Keep at least two options
Approve a second assistant or model for important work, so a single change never stops you.
04
Read the data terms
Confirm where data is processed, how long it is kept and whether it is used for training.
05
Review every year
The market moves quickly. Revisit your choices at each renewal, with real usage and results in hand.

Glossary

Words worth knowing

Frontier model
One of the most capable AI models available at a given time, usually from a large lab.
Compute
The processing power, mostly specialised chips, used to train and run AI models.
GPU
A graphics processing unit. The chip type most widely used for AI work.
Open-weight model
A model whose trained parameters are published, so others can download and run it.
Closed model
A model available only as a hosted service from its developer or partners.
API
A way for software to send requests to a model and receive answers.
Model family
A set of related models from one lab, often in different sizes and speeds.
Data residency
Where data is physically stored and processed.
Sovereignty
Control over data and systems under the laws and jurisdiction you choose.
Lock-in
When switching supplier becomes too costly or disruptive to be practical.
EU AI Act
The European Union's risk-based law governing how AI systems are built and used.
Bundled AI
AI features included in, or sold as add-ons to, software you already use.
